Management Analyst Career Assessment

Studying how an organization works and showing how to fix it

Section 1 of 11

1Welcome

Management analysts study how an organization does its work and recommend ways to do it better, faster, or cheaper. Some are called consultants.

It pays well and it is growing in North Dakota, but nobody starts here. You will explore what the work is really like and whether it fits you.

This takes about 15 minutes.

2What is a Management Analyst?

A management analyst looks at how a business or agency actually operates, finds where time and money get wasted, and writes up what should change. People often call this consulting. Analysts inside one company are sometimes called business analysts or process improvement staff. The tools are interviews, data, spreadsheets, and a clear written report at the end.

The Reality: This is not an entry-level job, and you should not plan on it as a first career step. Nearly every posting asks for a bachelor's degree, and most ask for several years of experience in the field you would be analyzing. You cannot tell a hospital how to run its billing if you have never worked in billing. What people actually do first is work in an industry, become the person who knows how the process really works, then move into analysis. Consulting also means selling your recommendations to people who did not ask for them, and watching some of them get ignored.

Your Responsibilities:

  • Interview staff about how work really gets done
  • Gather and analyze data on cost, time, and output
  • Map out a process step by step to find the waste
  • Compare what an organization does to what others do
  • Write clear reports with specific recommendations
  • Present findings to managers and answer hard questions
  • Help plan and track the changes that get approved
  • Check afterward whether the change actually worked

You are the person who finds the wasted hour nobody noticed and proves it.

3A Day in the Life

8:00 AM — Review yesterday's notes from the clinic scheduling staff.

9:00 AM — Shadow the front desk for an hour. Time every step of check-in.

10:30 AM — Interview two schedulers about where things break down.

12:00 PM — Lunch.

12:45 PM — Build a chart showing where the wait time actually comes from.

2:00 PM — Draft three recommendations, with a cost estimate for each.

3:30 PM — Present the draft to the clinic manager. Two questions you cannot answer yet.

4:30 PM — Write down what you still need to find out before the final report.

Half digging for facts, half explaining them. Deadlines are real and the report has your name on it.

4Training in North Dakota

Almost every job asks for a bachelor's degree plus years of industry experience. There is no license. What gets you hired is knowing an industry well enough that your recommendations are credible, plus the analysis skills to back them up with numbers. Many analysts add a master's degree later. The realistic North Dakota path is to build a career in health care, energy, banking, agriculture, or government first.

Training Pathways:

  • Industry experience first (5 or more years): Work in an industry and become the person who understands the whole process. Volunteer for improvement projects. This is what makes an analyst believable.
  • Bachelor's degree (4 years): Business administration, management, accounting, or business analytics. This is the minimum most postings list, and the analytics coursework is what separates candidates.
  • Add a master's degree (about 2 more years): An MBA is common in this field and opens senior and consulting jobs. North Dakota has affordable online options you can do while working.

Where to Train in North Dakota:

  • University of North Dakota (Grand Forks) — Master of Business Administration, 33 credits, about 2 years, fully online with live Zoom classes one evening a week, with optional concentrations including Business Analytics. UND lists the total program cost as under $21,000 — und.edu
  • North Dakota State University (Fargo) — Management major, B.S., minimum 120 credits, with tracks you can shape toward operations and organizational work — catalog.ndsu.edu
  • Minot State University (Minot) — Management, B.S., 120 credits, on campus or online, plus a Master of Science in Management — minotstateu.edu
  • University of Mary (Bismarck) — Business Administration, B.S., offered traditionally, online, and evening, with an accelerated option the school says can finish in about 2.6 years, and a Year-Round Campus option that combines the bachelor's and an MBA in four years — umary.edu
  • Valley City State University (Valley City) — Business Administration, 120 credits, with Project Management and Organizational Behavior in the Human Resource & Management concentration — catalog.vcsu.edu
  • North Dakota State College of Science (Wahpeton and Fargo) — Business Management, AAS, Administration and Finance emphasis, 61 credits, in person or with online courses. A two-year start that transfers — catalog.ndscs.edu
  • Lake Region State College (Devils Lake) — Business Administration, AA, 63 credits, built to transfer toward a bachelor's degree — lrsc.edu

Before You Start: High school diploma or GED to begin a degree. You need to be comfortable with data and spreadsheets, and you need to write and speak clearly, because the report is the product. Statistics helps more than calculus.

Tip: Start with financial aid, scholarships, and any help an employer offers — that is where the money starts. After that, ask a career counselor or Job Service North Dakota whether WIOA or other programs can help with what is left over, like training, tools, work boots, or exam fees. What can be paid for depends on the program and on your own situation, so ask before you sign up rather than after. Registered apprenticeships let you earn a paycheck while you learn — see jobsnd.com/job-seeker/apprenticeships for ND programs.

5Certification & Licensing

No license is required, and there is no single credential employers expect. Unlike accounting or nursing, this field has no required license and no one certificate that opens doors. Employers judge you on your degree, your industry experience, and the quality of your last report. Credentials here are optional extras.

What Actually Counts:

  • No license needed — you can work as a management analyst, business analyst, or consultant with no credential at all. Most people in this job have none
  • A bachelor's degree — this is the real gate. Business administration, management, accounting, or analytics are the usual majors
  • Industry experience — several years in the field you analyze. This is what employers and clients actually weigh, and it cannot be substituted with a certificate
  • A master's degree — an MBA is common at the senior and consulting level. UND's is 33 credits and fully online (und.edu)
  • HR credentials, if you work in that area — some analysts specialize in workforce and organization questions. The SHRM-CP requires no degree and no prior HR experience, and the SPHR requires 7 years of professional-level HR experience with no degree, or 5 with a bachelor's (shrm.org, hrci.org)
  • Beware of certificate claims — many groups sell consulting or process-improvement certificates. Before paying, ask an employer in your field whether they have ever heard of it

The Exams: There is no entry exam for this career. If you pursue an HR credential alongside it, the HRCI exams are given at Pearson VUE centers or online: the PHR is 2 hours with 90 scored questions, and the SPHR is 2 hours and 30 minutes with 115 scored questions (hrci.org).

Cost: Your degree is the real cost. UND lists its online MBA total program cost as under $21,000. Ask a career counselor what tuition and book help is available.

Keeping It Current: Nothing to renew, because nothing is required. Optional credentials like the HRCI ones last three years and need recertification credits (hrci.org).

6Salary & Job Outlook

North Dakota Management Analyst Pay (May 2025):

  • Starting out (bottom 10–25%): $23.68–$29.56/hour ($49,260–$61,490/year)
  • Middle (median — half earn more, half earn less): $43.38/hour ($90,220/year)
  • Experienced (top 25–10%): $47.84–$61.24/hour ($99,500–$127,380/year)

What that means: The middle North Dakota management analyst earns about $43.38 an hour, or about $90,220 a year. Two things in these numbers deserve a warning. First, the spread at the bottom is wide: the 25th percentile is $29.56 and the median is $43.38, a gap of almost $14 an hour. That is the difference between a junior analyst inside one company and an experienced consultant, and it tells you the first years pay far less than the median. Second, North Dakota pays well below the country here. The national median is about $48.97 an hour and the national top tenth is about $82.52, against North Dakota's $61.24. Large consulting firms cluster in big cities, and North Dakota has few of them.

Jobs in North Dakota: About 1,120 people do this work in ND today. By 2034, that should be about 1,270 (about 13% growth). Expect about 110 job openings every year in ND from growth and people retiring or changing jobs. Across the U.S., the change is about 9% growth — much faster than average.

Outlook: Strong for a professional job. North Dakota expects about 110 openings a year and 13% growth over ten years, faster than the country. Health systems, energy companies, and state government keep hiring people to study and improve their own operations.

Who Hires in ND: Consulting firms, large hospitals and health systems, energy and oil companies, banks and insurance companies, agricultural cooperatives, North Dakota state government, universities, and large manufacturers.

Work Setting: An office, plus time at whatever worksite you are studying. Mostly indoors. Consultants travel to clients; analysts employed by one company mostly stay put. Some of this work is fully remote.

Physical Demands: Low. Sitting, screen time, and presenting while standing. Eye, back, and wrist strain are the real risks. A good chair, a sit-stand desk, a second monitor, and screen reading or magnification software are ordinary requests. Consulting adds driving and travel days.

Sources: U.S. Bureau of Labor Statistics, Occupational Employment and Wage Statistics, North Dakota, May 2025 (SOC 13-1111); North Dakota and U.S. long-term employment projections 2024–2034, via O*NET OnLine. Numbers are rounded.

7Where This Leads

Path 1: Industry Job — Where you start. Learn how the work really happens.

Path 2: Business or Process Analyst — Study and improve one company from inside.

Path 3: Management Analyst — Full analysis and recommendation work.

Path 4: Senior Analyst — Lead studies and mentor junior staff.

Path 5: Consultant — Work with outside clients for a firm.

Path 6: Independent Consultant — Run your own practice.

8The Real Talk

What's Great:

  • Pay well above the ND average
  • Growing faster than the country
  • Every project is a new problem
  • You see how whole organizations work
  • Can lead to your own practice
  • Skills transfer across industries

What's Hard:

  • Not an entry-level job
  • Bachelor's degree is the real gate
  • Early-career pay is far below the median
  • People resist your recommendations
  • ND pay trails the national rate
  • Deadlines and travel in consulting

Be Honest: Are you willing to spend years learning an industry before you analyze it? Can you write a clear report and defend it to people who disagree? Can you handle good recommendations being ignored?
