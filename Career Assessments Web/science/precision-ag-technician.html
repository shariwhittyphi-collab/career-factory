Precision Ag Technician Career Assessment

Farm equipment, field maps, and technology that helps growers

Section 1 of 11

1Welcome

GPS can guide a tractor. A field map can show where a crop did well. A precision ag technician helps people get useful results from those tools.

Explore the work, the training options, and what a day might feel like. You do not have to choose this career.

Go at your pace. Read what interests you and try the short questions at the end. There are no right or wrong answers.

2What is a Precision Ag Technician?

You help growers use technology on farm equipment and understand field information. Work may include setting up guidance equipment, checking sensors, making maps, and helping a customer solve a problem. Some positions focus more on equipment; others focus more on crop information or customer support.

  • Help set up or check GPS guidance and connected equipment.
  • Work with field maps and records of planting or harvest.
  • Find out why a screen, sensor, or connection is not working as expected.
  • Show a grower how to use a tool and explain the next step.
  • Keep notes and work with the service shop and sales team.

The real picture: This is more than driving a tractor. It includes patient troubleshooting, computer work, and explaining things. Job titles and duties vary by employer.

3A Day in the Life

One possible day: This is an example, not a schedule every employer follows.

  • Morning: Check messages and talk with the shop about a customer’s guidance problem.
  • Later: Review a field map and prepare a short demonstration.
  • Midday: Travel to a farm with a coworker and check a connection on parked equipment.
  • Afternoon: Help the grower understand the settings, document the fix, and ask a specialist about a problem you cannot solve yet.
  • Before leaving: Confirm the next visit and update the service record.

Planting and harvest may bring longer days and urgent calls. Other days may include training, preparing equipment, and quieter office work. Ask about the actual hours, travel area, and time away from home.

4Training in North Dakota

There is more than one learning route. An employer may want an associate degree, a bachelor’s degree, technical experience, or a combination. Compare real job requirements before paying for training.

  • Lake Region State College — Precision Agriculture Certificate: 15 credits in GPS, precision systems, electronic devices, and field operations. A shorter option that can lead toward its agriculture associate degree. Ask about the course schedule and how it fits the job you want.
  • NDSCS in Wahpeton — Precision Agriculture Technician, AAS: 62 credits over the first and second year, with practical GPS, data, electrical, and hydraulic work. The program includes a 400-hour paid summer internship. A personal course plan may take longer.
  • NDSU — Agricultural Technology: a university route with a Precision Agriculture emphasis. Ask the adviser about the current degree plan and transfer options.

Try something first: A dealership visit, high school co-op, or job shadow lets you see the work before choosing a program. See Student opportunities near home. Gooseneck’s precision-ag internship is for students already pursuing a relevant college degree; it is different from its high school co-op.

Helpful school subjects: Agriculture, computers, math, science, shop, and communication. If your school does not offer an ag class, ask a counselor about another way to get exposure.

Paying for training: Start with financial aid, scholarships, grants, and employer help. Ask the school and employer for the full cost, including tools, travel, a computer, and housing. Then ask your career counselor about other help, including WIOA. Ask before you enroll. Employer tuition help may come with a work commitment; read the terms first.

5Training and Employer Requirements

Ask each employer what you need before starting and what you will learn after you are hired. A school certificate, an employer training course, and a state license mean different things.

  • Equipment and software training may be provided by the dealership or manufacturer.
  • Field roles may require a driver’s license, travel, and safe work around large machines.
  • Ask how new workers learn equipment safety, when they have supervision, and how physical tasks are shared.
  • Drones, chemical application, or other specialist work may bring extra rules and credentials. Those duties are not part of every precision-ag job.

Before accepting a role: Ask about required credentials, training costs, testing, and whether any course must be renewed. Do not assume a short certificate meets every employer’s requirements.

You can ask about accessible equipment, written instructions, extra practice, or other support while learning.

6Pay and Job Outlook

The published numbers cover a broader group. O*NET reports pay and outlook for all agricultural technicians, not precision-ag technicians alone. Do not read these as a Gooseneck wage offer.

  • North Dakota middle pay (May 2025): $22.58 an hour / $46,960 a year. Half earned more and half earned less.
  • Lower 10th–25th percentiles: $17.62–$21.19 an hour. Upper 75th–90th percentiles: $28.64–$30.90 an hour. These are pay distributions, not guaranteed starting or experienced rates.
  • For the broader group, ND employment is projected from 140 in 2024 to 160 in 2034: 12% growth and about 20 openings a year, including replacements.

Where people work: Equipment dealerships, agricultural service businesses, and farm-related organizations. Gooseneck had precision-ag technician openings in Bowman, Dickinson, and Williston when checked on October 4, 2026. A listing can close; check the employer’s current board.

Work setting: A mix of office, shop, customer visits, and field work. Some jobs involve driving, standing, climbing equipment steps, working outside, or handling components. Ask about the actual tasks and available support.

Compare an offer: Ask about hourly or salary pay, overtime, seasonal hours, travel, benefits, tools, and training. Local pay may differ from the broader statewide figures.

7Where This Can Lead

  • Precision-ag support: Help customers with settings, maps, and equipment questions.
  • Field specialist: Work on more difficult setup and troubleshooting tasks as you gain experience.
  • Training or sales: Demonstrate tools and help customers compare options.
  • A related direction: Farm equipment repair, agricultural technology, crop services, or data and mapping work. Additional training may be needed.

These are possibilities to explore, not automatic promotions. Ask someone doing the work how they reached their role.

8The Real Talk

Things you might like:

  • Work connected to rural communities.
  • A mix of computers, equipment, and people.
  • Seeing a customer use something you helped set up.
  • Learning new technology over time.

Things to ask about:

  • Busy seasons and after-hours calls.
  • Travel distances and overnight trips.
  • Frustrating problems that take several attempts.
  • Training time and physical tasks at a particular location.

A question for yourself: Would you enjoy helping someone solve a technology problem when you do not know the answer yet? You can explore the answer with a short visit.
