Forklift Operator Career Assessment

Moving and stacking loads safely in warehouses, plants, and yards

Section 1 of 11

1Welcome

Pallets of food, parts, and supplies move through North Dakota warehouses every day. Forklift operators move them.

This job mixes careful driving, safety, and steady work. Explore what it feels like before deciding whether it interests you.

Go at your pace. The short questions at the end are for reflection. There are no right or wrong answers.

2What is a Forklift Operator?

You drive a forklift or similar machine to load, move, and stack materials. You work in warehouses, factories, lumber yards, and shipping docks.

  • Inspect the forklift before each shift.
  • Load and unload trucks and rail cars.
  • Move pallets to the right shelf or work area.
  • Check labels and order numbers so the right load goes to the right place.
  • Watch for people and obstacles while driving.
  • Record what was moved, sometimes with a handheld scanner.

The real picture: Some jobs are mostly driving. In others, you also pick orders, wrap pallets, and help on the dock.

3A Day in the Life

One possible day: Every warehouse runs on its own schedule.

  • Start of shift: Walk around the forklift and check tires, forks, horn, and brakes.
  • Morning: Unload two trucks and put pallets away on high racks.
  • Midday: Move materials to the production line as they are needed.
  • Afternoon: Load outgoing orders onto trucks in the right order.
  • End of shift: Park and charge or fuel the forklift and report any problems.

Busy days can feel fast. Safety always comes before speed.

4How You Learn the Job

Most people learn on the job. Many employers hire people with a high school diploma or GED, and some do not require one.

  • Employer training: Federal safety rules require each operator to finish training and an evaluation before driving on their own. The training mixes classroom learning, hands-on practice, and a driving test at work.
  • Start nearby: A warehouse, stocker, or material mover job can lead to forklift work.
  • Refreshers: Operators are evaluated again at least every three years, or after an accident or near miss.

There is no state forklift license. A card from a class somewhere else does not replace your employer’s own training and evaluation, so ask before paying for a class.

5Training and Employer Requirements

Requirements depend on the employer. Ask what you need to start.

  • Completion of the employer’s forklift training and evaluation.
  • Good eyesight, hearing, and attention to people nearby.
  • Ability to climb on and off the machine and sometimes lift boxes.
  • Willingness to work shifts, which may include nights or weekends.
  • Drug testing for some employers.

Before you begin: Ask who trains new operators and how much practice time you get.

Ask about written instructions, an accessible workstation, extra practice time, or other support that helps you learn and do the work well.

6Pay and Job Outlook

North Dakota forklift operator pay — May 2025:

  • Middle pay: $23.68 an hour ($49,260 a year). Half of workers earned more and half earned less.
  • Half of workers earned between $21.65 and $27.07 an hour.
  • 1 in 10 earned less than $19.58 an hour, and 1 in 10 earned more than $30.14.
  • These numbers show what workers earned. They are not a promise of what a new worker will start at.
  • North Dakota had about 990 of these jobs in 2024. The state projects about 1,060 in 2034, a change of +7%. That works out to about 100 openings a year, mostly from people who retire or change jobs.

Where people work: Warehouses, distribution centers, factories, grain and farm supply businesses, lumber yards, and shipping companies.

Work setting: Usually indoors in a warehouse or plant, sometimes in outdoor yards. Shifts may include nights and weekends.

Compare an offer: Ask about base pay, shift pay, overtime, benefits, and how the employer trains and evaluates operators.

7Where This Can Lead

  • Experienced operator: Drive other equipment, such as reach trucks or loaders.
  • Lead or trainer: Train new operators and lead a shift.
  • Warehouse or shipping supervisor: Run a dock or a whole shift.
  • Truck driving: Some operators go on to earn a commercial driver’s license.

Ask your employer which paths are open.

8The Real Talk

Things you might like:

  • Running equipment instead of lifting everything by hand.
  • Clear tasks with a finish line each shift.
  • Starting without a degree.
  • Jobs in many industries.

Things to ask about:

  • Hot, cold, or noisy warehouses.
  • Night or weekend shifts.
  • Pressure to move fast.
  • The risk of accidents if safety rules are not followed.

North Dakota projects about 7% growth in these jobs from 2024 to 2034, with about 100 openings a year.

A question for yourself: Do careful, steady driving and safety rules sound like a good fit for you?
