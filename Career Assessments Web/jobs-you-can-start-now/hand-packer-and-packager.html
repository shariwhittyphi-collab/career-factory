Hand Packer and Packager Career Assessment

Easy to get into, and worth planning your way out of

Section 1 of 11

1Welcome

Hand packers and packagers pack finished product into boxes, bags, and cartons by hand, usually on a line.

This job is shrinking in North Dakota — about 9% fewer positions expected by 2034. It is still a real way in, and this page is honest about what it is and what to do next.

This takes about 15 minutes.

2What is a Hand Packer and Packager?

You pack product into its container, check it is correct and undamaged, weigh or count it, seal and label it, and stack it for shipping. The work is set by the pace of the line or by a daily target, and it is usually the same motion repeated hundreds or thousands of times a shift.

The Reality: If nobody packs, product sits finished in the building and never reaches anybody. The honest picture of the job itself: it is repetitive, the pace is set for you, and your quota usually includes everything around it — the setup, the restocking, the checking — not just the packing. That is the part nobody counts when they tell you the number. You are often your own quality control as well, which means a mistake is yours even though nobody gave you time to catch it. And repeated motion for eight hours is hard on wrists, hands, and shoulders in a way that builds up quietly over months.

Your Responsibilities:

  • Pack product into boxes, bags, or cartons
  • Check for damage, defects, and correct count
  • Weigh, measure, or count to specification
  • Seal, label, and date packages
  • Stack finished cartons on pallets
  • Keep your station stocked with boxes and materials
  • Record output and report problems on the line
  • Meet a production target that includes your own setup and checking

The quota is the job, and the quota almost always includes work that is not packing.

3A Day in the Life

7:00 AM — Clock in. Set up your station, stock boxes and labels.

7:15 AM — Line starts. Pack, check, seal, stack.

9:15 AM — Break — fifteen minutes, which goes fast once you have walked there.

9:30 AM — Back on the line. Restock materials between runs.

11:30 AM — Lunch.

12:00 PM — Product changeover. Reset the station for a different size.

2:00 PM — Break.

2:15 PM — Finish the run, log output, clean down the station.

A fixed rhythm set by the line, with the breaks built around production rather than around you.

4Training in North Dakota

No school needed. Trained in a day or two. You learn the station, the specification, and the quality checks on shift. There is nothing to study beforehand.

Training Pathways:

  • Direct hire: Food plants, manufacturers, and distribution operations hire packers year-round, often through staffing agencies.
  • Through a staffing agency: Common in this job. Ask what the pay is when you convert to the employer directly, because it is usually higher.
  • Plan the step out: This job is shrinking. Use it to get inside a plant, then move to assembly, machine operation, or quality control, which pay substantially more.

Where to Train in North Dakota:

Before You Start: No diploma or experience required. You need to stand and repeat a motion for a full shift and stay accurate. Most positions require you to be 18.

Tip: Most of these jobs need no school, so the cost of starting is not tuition — it is the things you need in the first month. Non-slip shoes, work clothes or a uniform, a way to get to work, and getting through the two or three weeks before the first paycheck arrives. Ask the employer first: many will supply a uniform, some give a shoe allowance, and some pay weekly rather than every two weeks, which matters a lot at the start. After that, ask a career counselor or Job Service North Dakota whether WIOA or other programs can help with what is left over, like work clothes, boots, tools, or getting to work. What can be paid for depends on the program and on your own situation, so ask before you start rather than after.

5Certification & Licensing

Nothing is required. No certificate or license. In food plants the employer provides the food safety and hygiene training you need.

Worth knowing about:

  • Good Manufacturing Practices (GMP) training — provided by food and pharmaceutical plants
  • Food handler card — relevant in food packing, usually employer-arranged
  • Forklift certification — free from the employer, and the usual first step out of hand packing
  • OSHA 10 — a short safety course that helps when you apply for better plant positions

The Exams: None to start.

Cost: Nothing.

Keeping It Current: Plant training is normally repeated yearly.

6Salary & Job Outlook

North Dakota Hand Packer and Packager Pay (May 2025):

  • Starting out (bottom 10–25%): $10.69–$12.93/hour ($22,240–$26,890/year)
  • Middle (median — half earn more, half earn less): $15.11/hour ($31,440/year)
  • Experienced (top 25–10%): $19.06–$20.42/hour ($39,640–$42,480/year)

What that means: The bottom of this range is often agency work. The top is usually someone who has been in one plant for years or who also runs equipment. If you are hired through a staffing agency, ask directly what the rate becomes when the employer takes you on, because the gap is frequently large.

Jobs in North Dakota: About 610 people do this work in ND today. By 2034, that should be about 550 (a decline of about 9%). Expect about 70 job openings every year in ND from growth and people retiring or changing jobs. Across the U.S., the change is declining.

Outlook: Declining. North Dakota expects about 9% fewer of these positions by 2034, falling from roughly 610 to 550, with only about 70 openings a year. Automated packing equipment is the reason. The job is not disappearing tomorrow, but it is not a career to plan ten years around.

Who Hires in ND: Food processing plants, manufacturers, agricultural product companies, distribution centers, and staffing agencies that supply all of them.

Work Setting: Indoors on a plant or warehouse floor. Often cold in food plants. Loud enough that hearing protection is common. Shift work including nights at many plants.

Physical Demands: High in a specific way. Not heavy lifting so much as constant repetition — standing in one spot and repeating the same motion for hours, which is hard on wrists, hands, and shoulders.

Sources: U.S. Bureau of Labor Statistics, Occupational Employment and Wage Statistics, North Dakota, May 2025 (SOC 53-7064); North Dakota and U.S. long-term employment projections 2024–2034, via O*NET OnLine. Numbers are rounded.

7Where This Leads

Path 1: Hand Packer — Where you start, and where this page recommends you do not stop.

Path 2: Machine Operator — Running the packing or filling equipment instead of feeding it. A clear step up.

Path 3: Quality Control Inspector — Checking product formally rather than informally.

Path 4: Team Assembler — Median about $7.60 an hour higher — see that page.

Path 5: Forklift Operator — Free certification, and it gets you off the line.

Path 6: Production Lead — Running a line and a crew.

8The Real Talk

What's Great:

  • Hired quickly, often within a week
  • No school, diploma, or experience needed
  • Gets you inside a plant, where the better-paying positions are
  • Indoor work with a predictable routine
  • Shift work can suit people who want nights or early finishes
  • Free forklift certification is usually available once you are inside

What's Hard:

  • Declining — about 9% fewer positions expected and only 70 openings a year
  • The lowest bottom-end pay in this area
  • Repetitive strain on wrists, hands, and shoulders builds up quietly
  • The quota usually includes your own setup, restocking, and checking
  • You are frequently your own quality control with no time allowed for it
  • Breaks are built around the line, not around you

Be Honest: Can you repeat the same motion accurately for eight hours? Will you pay attention to your wrists and hands early rather than after they hurt? And are you willing to treat this as a way into a plant rather than a place to stay?
