Substance Abuse Counselor Career Assessment

Helping people work toward recovery from alcohol and drug problems

Section 1 of 11

1Welcome

When someone decides to change their relationship with alcohol or drugs, a counselor is often part of that journey.

This career mixes listening, planning, and hope. Explore what it feels like before deciding whether it interests you.

Go at your pace. The short questions at the end are for reflection. There are no right or wrong answers.

2What is a Substance Abuse Counselor?

You help people who struggle with alcohol, drugs, or other addictive behaviors. You meet with people one-on-one and in groups, and you work with their families and other providers.

  • Meet with clients to understand their needs and goals.
  • Write treatment plans with each person.
  • Lead individual and group sessions.
  • Track progress and adjust plans.
  • Work with doctors, nurses, social workers, and courts.
  • Step in to help when someone is in crisis.

The real picture: Recovery is rarely a straight line. Counselors celebrate progress and keep going through setbacks.

3A Day in the Life

One possible day: Every program works differently.

  • Morning: Review notes and meet with the team about new clients.
  • Later: Meet one-on-one with two clients.
  • Midday: Lead a group session.
  • Afternoon: Update treatment plans and call a client’s doctor.
  • Before leaving: Finish notes and plan tomorrow.

Some days are hopeful and others are heavy. Supervision and teamwork help counselors keep going.

4How You Learn the Job

This career takes college and a state license. North Dakota licensed addiction counselors complete board-approved college coursework, at least 960 hours of supervised clinical training, and a national exam.

  • North Dakota programs: Minot State University and the University of Mary offer bachelor’s programs in addiction counseling. Nueta Hidatsa Sahnish College in New Town offers an associate program.
  • Clinical training: You practice under supervision in an approved program.
  • Another route: Licensed professional counselors in North Dakota need a master’s degree in counseling; some also treat addiction.
  • Start nearby: Recovery support, peer support, or human service assistant jobs can be a first step.

Paying for training:

  1. Start with financial aid, scholarships, and grants. The school's financial aid office can help.
  2. Ask employers whether they help pay for training.
  3. Ask for the full cost, including books, fees, travel, and a computer.
  4. Ask your career counselor about other help, such as WIOA. WIOA is a federal program that can help some people pay for job training. Ask before you enroll.

Employer help with school may come with a promise to keep working there. Read the terms first.

5Training and Employer Requirements

To practice as a licensed addiction counselor in North Dakota, you need a license from the North Dakota Board of Addiction Counseling Examiners. Confirm the exact education rules with the board before you choose a program.

  • Board-approved college coursework.
  • At least 960 hours of supervised clinical training.
  • Passing the board-approved exam.
  • A background check for most jobs.
  • Continuing education to keep the license.

Before you begin: Ask schools how they help students find clinical training placements.

Ask about written instructions, an accessible workstation, extra practice time, or other support that helps you learn and do the work well.

6Pay and Job Outlook

North Dakota substance abuse counselor pay — May 2025:

  • Middle pay: $33.00 an hour ($68,630 a year). Half of workers earned more and half earned less.
  • Half of workers earned between $26.66 and $37.17 an hour.
  • 1 in 10 earned less than $20.80 an hour, and 1 in 10 earned more than $41.65.
  • These numbers show what workers earned. They are not a promise of what a new worker will start at.
  • North Dakota had about 1,260 of these jobs in 2024. The state projects about 1,500 in 2034, a change of +20%. That works out to about 130 openings a year, mostly from people who retire or change jobs.

Where people work: Treatment centers, hospitals, human service centers, tribal health programs, correctional facilities, and private practices.

Work setting: Offices and group rooms, sometimes with evening hours. Some counselors work by video.

Compare an offer: Ask about base pay, supervision for your license, caseload size, benefits, and help with continuing education.

7Where This Can Lead

  • Experienced counselor: Specialize in youth, families, or co-occurring mental health needs.
  • Clinical supervisor: Train and supervise new counselors.
  • Program director: Lead a treatment program.
  • More education: A master’s degree can lead to a professional counselor license.

Ask counselors near you which paths are open.

8The Real Talk

Things you might like:

  • Helping people rebuild their lives.
  • Strong demand in North Dakota.
  • A licensed profession with room to grow.
  • Teamwork with other helpers.

Things to ask about:

  • Emotional weight and burnout.
  • Lots of paperwork.
  • Clients who relapse or stop coming.
  • Years of school and training before full licensure.

North Dakota projects about 20% growth in these jobs from 2024 to 2034, with about 130 openings a year.

A question for yourself: Can you listen without judging and keep caring when change is slow?
