Emergency Medical Technician Career Assessment

First responder role

Section 1 of 11

1Welcome

Emergency Medical Technicians (EMTs) are the first link in the emergency healthcare chain.

You'll learn what EMTs really do, the training required, and whether this high-stakes career is right for you.

Heads up: This job involves life-and-death situations, trauma, and stressful calls. We'll be honest about that.

This takes about 15 minutes.

2What is an Emergency Medical Technician?

An EMT is trained to provide emergency medical care and transportation for sick and injured people.

EMT Levels (North Dakota):

  • Emergency Medical Responder (EMR): Shortest course. Basic first care until the ambulance crew takes over.
  • EMT: Usually one semester or less of training. Basic life support, oxygen, CPR, bleeding control, splinting broken bones.
  • Advanced EMT (AEMT): Advanced training after EMT. IV fluids, advanced airway, certain medications.
  • Paramedic: Highest level. About 1-2 years of training after EMT. Advanced life support, cardiac drugs, intubation.

Your Day-to-Day:

  • Respond to 911 calls (could be anything)
  • Assess patients quickly and accurately
  • Provide first aid, oxygen, CPR
  • Operate ambulances safely (often in dangerous traffic)
  • Transport patients to hospitals
  • Document everything
  • Work with paramedics, police, fire departments
  • Comfort scared, injured, angry people
  • Return to the station and prepare for the next call

No two calls are the same. You never know what you'll face.

3A Day in the Life

6:00 AM — Shift starts. Walk-through of the ambulance. Check equipment, medications, oxygen. Nothing is missing because people's lives depend on it.

6:30 AM — Return to station. Wait for calls. Some days are quiet. Some days are chaos.

7:15 AM — First call: Car crash on the interstate. Two patients. You coordinate with police and fire. One patient is stable. One is critical. Assess. Stabilize. Transport fast.

8:30 AM — At hospital. Hand off patients. Document the call. Quick breakfast, if there's time.

9:00 AM — Call: Elderly woman, chest pain. You arrive within minutes. Take her vital signs. She's worried. You're calm. You get her to the hospital safely.

10:00 AM — Back to station. Restock the ambulance. Clean equipment.

10:30 AM — Multiple calls. Cardiac arrest. Overdose. Traffic accident. You treat each one like it's your family member.

2:00 PM — Lunch (quick, always listening for dispatch)

2:30 PM – 6:00 PM — More calls. Home health check. Fall victim. Intoxicated person.

6:00 PM — End of shift. You're exhausted. Maybe mentally scarred. But you did it. You helped people on their worst days.

4Training in North Dakota

EMT License Required. You cannot work as an EMT in ND without a state EMT license.

Training Pathways:

  • EMT: About one semester or less. Bismarck State College's EMT course is 16 weeks. Cost varies by program. Colleges, ambulance services, fire departments.
  • Advanced EMT (AEMT): Extra training after you are an EMT. Cost varies by program.
  • Paramedic: About 1-2 years after EMT (Bismarck State College: 3 semesters). In ND you earn a certificate or 2-year associate degree (AAS). Cost varies by school.

In North Dakota: EMT courses at Bismarck State College (Bismarck) and NDSCS with Sanford Health (Fargo), plus courses run by local ambulance services and fire departments. Paramedic programs: Bismarck State College, NDSCS/Sanford Health (Fargo), and Dakota College at Bottineau (Minot and Bottineau, with distance sites).

Prerequisites: Vary by program. Most require a CPR (BLS) card and a background check. Many employers also want a valid driver's license.

Clinical Experience: You'll ride on real ambulances. See real emergencies. Learn from paramedics and experienced EMTs.

5Certification & Licensing

North Dakota EMT License — Required to work legally. You must pass the National Registry (NREMT) first.

National Registry of Emergency Medical Technicians (NREMT) Exam

  • Written exam: Tests knowledge of emergency medicine, patient care
  • Practical exam: Skills testing (airway, bleeding control, immobilization, etc.)
  • Pass rate: About 3 out of 4 pass on the first try (National Registry, recent years)
  • Cost: $104 each time you take the exam

Continuing Education: Renew every 2 years. National Registry EMTs need 40 hours of continuing education (CE) per renewal.

Background Check: Programs and employers usually check your record. Some employers also drug test. Ask each program and employer.

6Salary & Job Outlook

North Dakota EMT Salary (BLS, May 2025):

  • Lower pay (bottom 10%): $15.33/hour ($31,890/year)
  • Middle pay (median): $19.07/hour ($39,670/year)
  • Higher pay (top 10%): $26.01/hour ($54,100/year)
  • Paramedics earn more. ND paramedic median: $29.96/hour ($62,320/year).

Benefits: Health insurance, retirement (varies by employer), paid time off.

Employers in ND: Fire departments, hospital-based EMS, private ambulance services, rural EMS, air ambulance services.

Job Outlook: Good. Jobs for EMTs and paramedics are expected to grow 6% nationally from 2025 to 2035 (BLS). Aging population needs more emergency care.

Work Schedule: 12-24 hour shifts. On-call. Weekends, nights, holidays. Unpredictable but structured.

7Career Advancement

Path 1: Paramedic — Become a paramedic. Higher pay, more advanced care, more responsibility. About 1-2 years of school.

Path 2: Management — Become a supervisor, shift commander, EMS chief. Run an operation. Handle budgets, staffing, protocols.

Path 3: Specialty Areas — Flight paramedic (air ambulance), tactical medic (SWAT), wilderness EMS, event medical.

Path 4: Education — Teach EMT/Paramedic courses. Develop training programs. Work in academia.

Path 5: Related Healthcare — Nursing, physician assistant, emergency medicine doctor. Your EMS background helps you understand emergency care from the ground up.

Path 6: Stay in Field — Some EMTs do this for 30+ years. It's a calling, not just a job. Respect.

8The Real Talk

What's Great:

  • You make a direct difference. Every shift matters.
  • Strong camaraderie. EMS crews bond tight.
  • Never boring. Constant learning.
  • Respect in the community
  • Good pay for the education level
  • Job security

What's Hard:

  • Trauma. Death. You see things civilians don't.
  • PTSD and burnout are real. This job breaks some people.
  • Long shifts. Physical exhaustion.
  • Difficult patients (drunk, violent, non-compliant)
  • Time away from family. Holidays spent on the job.
  • Stress. Every call could go wrong.
  • Low pay compared to the stress and responsibility
  • Bloodborne pathogen exposure
  • Risk of injury or illness on the job

Be Real With Yourself: Can you handle seeing suffering? Can you make quick decisions? Can you stay calm under pressure? Can you mentally process what you see? If not, reconsider.
