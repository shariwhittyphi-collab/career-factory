Power Distributor and Dispatcher Career Assessment

Controlling the flow of electricity on the grid

Section 1 of 11

1Welcome

Power distributors and dispatchers (system operators) control how electricity flows across the power grid.

You will explore what this control-room career is really like and whether it fits you.

This takes about 15 minutes.

2What is a Power Distributor and Dispatcher?

System operators watch the power grid from a control room. They balance supply and demand, open and close circuits, direct crews during outages, and prevent blackouts.

The Reality: You are responsible for keeping the lights on for many people. During storms and outages, you make fast decisions under pressure. Control rooms run 24/7.

Your Responsibilities:

  • Monitor the power grid on computer screens
  • Balance power supply and demand
  • Direct line crews during outages
  • Switch circuits safely
  • Respond to alarms and emergencies
  • Follow federal reliability rules (NERC)
  • Keep detailed logs
  • Coordinate with other utilities

You keep the grid stable.

3A Day in the Life

6:00 AM — Shift handoff in the control room.

7:00 AM — Demand rises as people wake up. Adjust generation.

10:00 AM — Plan a safe switching order for crew maintenance.

1:00 PM — A storm hits. Outages pop up. Direct crews.

4:00 PM — Restore power area by area.

6:00 PM — Hand off to night shift.

High-focus control room work.

4Training in North Dakota

Federal NERC certification is required for many roles. Most system operators come up from other utility jobs (like lineworker or plant operator) and earn NERC certification.

Training Pathways:

  • Electric power program (2 years): Online at BSC.
  • Utility experience first: Lineworker, substation, or plant operator.
  • NERC certification: Required for many control room jobs.

Where to Train in North Dakota:

  • No dedicated dispatcher program was found in ND
  • Bismarck State College (online) — Electric Power Technology, AAS or certificate
  • Bismarck State College — Power Process Technology, AAS

Before You Start: High school diploma or GED. Strong math. Utility experience is often expected. Good color vision.

Tip: Start with financial aid, scholarships, and any help an employer offers — that is where the money starts. After that, ask a career counselor or Job Service North Dakota whether WIOA or other programs can help with what is left over, like training, tools, work boots, or exam fees. What can be paid for depends on the program and on your own situation, so ask before you sign up rather than after. Registered apprenticeships let you earn a paycheck while you learn — see jobsnd.com/job-seeker/apprenticeships for ND programs.

5Certification & Licensing

NERC System Operator Certification. NERC (North American Electric Reliability Corporation) certifies grid operators.

Certifications Employers Look For:

  • Reliability Coordinator (RC)
  • Balancing, Interchange & Transmission (BT)
  • Transmission Operator (TO)
  • Balancing & Interchange (BI)

The Exams: Computer-based exams at Pearson VUE testing centers.

Cost: Check NERC for current fees; employers often pay.

Keeping It Current: Renew every 3 years with continuing education hours (140–200 depending on the credential).

6Salary & Job Outlook

U.S. Power Distributor and Dispatcher Pay (May 2025) — ND data not published:

  • Starting out (bottom 10–25%): $34.83–$43.37/hour ($72,440–$90,200/year)
  • Middle (median — half earn more, half earn less): $51.31/hour ($106,730/year)
  • Experienced (top 25–10%): $62.95–$74.17/hour ($130,940–$154,270/year)

What that means: North Dakota wage data is not published for this job because it is so small. These are national numbers. Very high pay.

Jobs in North Dakota: About 70 people do this work in ND today. By 2034, that should be about 70 (a 3% dip). Expect about 10 job openings every year in ND from growth and people retiring or changing jobs. Across the U.S., the change is about 3% fewer jobs.

Outlook: A very small field in ND with about 10 openings a year. Usually filled by experienced utility workers.

Who Hires in ND: Electric utilities, cooperatives, and transmission companies.

Work Setting: Control rooms. Rotating 12-hour shifts, 24/7.

Physical Demands: Light. Sitting at screens for long shifts.

Sources: U.S. Bureau of Labor Statistics, Occupational Employment and Wage Statistics, United States, May 2025 (SOC 51-8012); North Dakota and U.S. long-term employment projections 2024–2034, via O*NET OnLine. Numbers are rounded.

7Where This Leads

Path 1: Senior System Operator — Lead shifts.

Path 2: Reliability Coordinator — Top NERC credential.

Path 3: Control Room Supervisor — Manage the team.

Path 4: Grid Planner — Plan the grid.

Path 5: Trainer — Teach operators.

8The Real Talk

What's Great:

  • Very high pay
  • Important work
  • Online training option
  • Utility benefits
  • Desk-based work

What's Hard:

  • Rotating 12-hour shifts
  • High pressure during outages
  • Usually need utility experience first
  • Very few openings
  • Federal certification exams

Be Honest: Can you stay calm under pressure? Are you OK with 24/7 shift work? Are you willing to start in another utility job first?
