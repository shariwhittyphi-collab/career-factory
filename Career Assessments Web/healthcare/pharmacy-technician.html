Pharmacy Technician Career Assessment

Helping pharmacists get the right medicine to the right person

Section 1 of 11

1Welcome

A prescription comes in. Someone has to check it, count or measure the medicine, label it, and have it ready for the pharmacist to check. Pharmacy technicians do much of that work.

This career is a way into healthcare that needs careful, exact work. Explore what it feels like before deciding whether it interests you.

Go at your pace. The short questions at the end are for reflection. There are no right or wrong answers.

2What is a Pharmacy Technician?

You help a pharmacist prepare and hand out medicine. You enter prescriptions into the computer, count or measure medicine, keep the shelves stocked, and help customers. A pharmacist checks the work and answers medicine questions.

  • Check prescriptions and enter them into the computer.
  • Count, measure, and label medicines.
  • Keep medicines stored safely and stocked.
  • Check deliveries and pull medicine that has expired.
  • Answer phones and help customers at the counter.
  • Send medicine questions to the pharmacist.

The real picture: The work is exact. A small mistake with medicine matters, so every step is checked.

3A Day in the Life

One possible day: Retail and hospital pharmacies run differently.

  • Morning: Check in a delivery and put medicine in the right places.
  • Later: Enter new prescriptions and count tablets for the pharmacist to check.
  • Midday: Help a line of customers pick up prescriptions.
  • Afternoon: Call an insurance company about a claim, then restock shelves.
  • Before leaving: Clean the counting area and finish the day's records.

Many pharmacies are open evenings and weekends. Hospital pharmacies may be open around the clock.

4How You Learn the Job

North Dakota requires training and registration for pharmacy technicians. The North Dakota State Board of Pharmacy says technicians must complete a training program accredited by the American Society of Health-System Pharmacists (ASHP), either at a school or through an accredited on-the-job program, and pass a national certification exam. Check the board's current rules before you enroll.

  • School programs: North Dakota State College of Science in Wahpeton offers pharmacy technician training. Rasmussen University in Fargo offers an associate degree.
  • Employer programs: Some pharmacies offer accredited on-the-job training. Ask whether a program is accredited before you start.
  • Certification: Plan to study for and pass a national pharmacy technician certification exam.
  • While you train: Ask the board whether you can register as a technician in training while you finish your program.

Paying for training:

  1. Start with financial aid, scholarships, and grants. The school's financial aid office can help.
  2. Ask employers whether they help pay for training.
  3. Ask for the full cost, including books, fees, travel, and a computer.
  4. Ask your career counselor about other help, such as WIOA. WIOA is a federal program that can help some people pay for job training. Ask before you enroll.

Employer help with school may come with a promise to keep working there. Read the terms first.

5Training and Employer Requirements

Heads up: North Dakota will not register you as a pharmacy technician until you have finished an accredited training program (the state college in Wahpeton or an approved employer program such as a chain pharmacy's) and passed a national certification exam. While you train you register as a technician-in-training under a pharmacist, and you have two years to finish. The Wahpeton program takes one class a year (fall only), uses a selective process with ACT or placement testing, and requires a background check, immunizations, and health insurance before internships.

Pharmacy technicians in North Dakota register with the State Board of Pharmacy. Ask the board and the employer what you need.

  • An accredited training program, at a school or through an employer.
  • National certification.
  • Registration with the North Dakota State Board of Pharmacy, renewed each year.
  • Continuing education to keep your registration.
  • A background check, which employers usually require.

Before you begin: Ask whether the employer pays for training and the exam, and how long you have to finish.

Ask about written instructions, an accessible workstation, extra practice time, or other support that helps you learn and do the work well.

6Pay and Job Outlook

North Dakota pharmacy technician pay — May 2025:

  • Middle pay: $22.86 an hour ($47,560 a year). Half of workers earned more and half earned less.
  • Half of workers earned between $19.89 and $26.08 an hour.
  • 1 in 10 earned less than $18.45 an hour, and 1 in 10 earned more than $30.70.
  • These numbers show what workers earned. They are not a promise of what a new worker will start at.
  • North Dakota had about 920 of these jobs in 2022. The state projects about 1,030 in 2032, a change of +12%. That works out to about 100 openings a year, mostly from people who retire or change jobs.

These North Dakota projections cover 2022–2032, the newest North Dakota figures shown for this job when checked in October 2026.

Where people work: Drugstores, grocery store pharmacies, hospitals, clinics, and long-term care pharmacies.

Work setting: A pharmacy counter and work area. About 3 in 4 pharmacy technicians say they stand for most of the day.

Compare an offer: Ask about base pay, shifts, weekends, benefits, paid time off, and whether the employer pays for training and certification.

7Where This Can Lead

  • Lead technician: Train new technicians and manage stock.
  • Hospital pharmacy: Prepare medicine for hospital patients, sometimes with extra training.
  • Specialty work: Some technicians move into insurance billing, inventory, or pharmacy software.
  • Pharmacist: Becoming a pharmacist takes a doctor of pharmacy degree, which is many more years of school.

Ask your employer whether they help pay for more training.

8The Real Talk

Things you might like:

  • Being part of healthcare.
  • Clear steps and exact work.
  • Shorter training than many healthcare careers.
  • Pharmacies in many North Dakota towns.

Things to ask about:

  • Standing for most of the shift.
  • Busy rushes and customers who are sick or in a hurry.
  • Evening, weekend, or holiday hours.
  • Training, certification, and yearly registration to keep up.

North Dakota expects about 12% growth, with about 100 openings a year.

A question for yourself: Do you like exact, careful work that helps people stay healthy?
