Food Preparation Worker Career Assessment

The work that happens before anyone orders

Section 1 of 11

1Welcome

Food preparation workers do the cutting, portioning, and setting up that a kitchen needs before service starts.

It pays about $3.20 an hour more than fast food, needs no school, and is the most common way people get from a counter job into a kitchen.

This takes about 15 minutes.

2What is a Food Preparation Worker?

You wash, peel, cut, and portion food, make simple cold items like salads and sandwiches, label and date containers, and stock the line so the cooks have everything within reach when service starts. It is volume work — not one onion, forty pounds of them — and it has to be consistent, because a cook timing a dish needs every piece the same size.

The Reality: If nobody preps, the cooks open the doors with nothing to cook with. The work is repetitive and mostly done before the restaurant is busy, which means a lot of it is quiet, early, and unglamorous. It is also where a kitchen judges whether you are careful: a prep cook who portions accurately and labels everything is noticed quickly, and a prep cook who does not is noticed just as quickly. Knife work for hours is harder on your hands and wrists than people expect.

Your Responsibilities:

  • Wash, peel, and cut vegetables and fruit in volume
  • Portion meat, sauces, and sides to a set weight
  • Make cold items — salads, sandwiches, dressings
  • Label and date every container
  • Rotate stock so the oldest gets used first
  • Stock the line before service
  • Keep the walk-in cooler organized
  • Follow temperature and food safety rules and log them

Consistency is the whole job, and a cook can tell within one dish whether you have it.

3A Day in the Life

6:00 AM — Clock in. Check the prep list left from last night.

6:15 AM — Start the long items — stocks, roasts, anything that needs hours.

7:30 AM — Vegetable prep. Volume knife work.

9:30 AM — Portion proteins to weight. Label and date everything.

10:30 AM — Break.

11:00 AM — Stock the line for the lunch cooks.

12:30 PM — Cold items — salads and sandwich build for the afternoon.

2:00 PM — Clean down, organize the walk-in, write tomorrow’s prep list.

Early, steady, and largely finished before the dining room fills.

4Training in North Dakota

No school needed. Trained on the job, usually within a couple of weeks. You learn knife cuts, portions, and the labelling system by doing them alongside someone experienced. The learning curve is real but short, and nobody expects speed in the first month.

Training Pathways:

  • Hired straight into prep: Common in restaurants, hotels, hospitals, and school kitchens. Say if you have any kitchen or fast food experience.
  • From dishwashing: The dish pit is the standard door into a kitchen, and prep is the usual first step up from it.
  • Prep to line cook: This is the point of the job for a lot of people. Restaurant cooks are the fastest-growing job in this area, and prep is how most of them got there.

Where to Train in North Dakota:

Before You Start: No diploma or experience required. You need steady hands, care with a knife, and the patience for repetitive volume work. Food handler training is usually arranged by the employer.

Tip: Most of these jobs need no school, so the cost of starting is not tuition — it is the things you need in the first month. Non-slip shoes, work clothes or a uniform, a way to get to work, and getting through the two or three weeks before the first paycheck arrives. Ask the employer first: many will supply a uniform, some give a shoe allowance, and some pay weekly rather than every two weeks, which matters a lot at the start. After that, ask a career counselor or Job Service North Dakota whether WIOA or other programs can help with what is left over, like work clothes, boots, tools, or getting to work. What can be paid for depends on the program and on your own situation, so ask before you start rather than after.

5Certification & Licensing

No license or certificate is required. Nothing official is needed to start. Most employers arrange food safety training for you.

Worth having:

  • Food handler card — short online course, usually employer-paid
  • ServSafe Food Handler — the more recognized version, useful when you change employers
  • Knife skills training — some employers and two-year programs offer short courses, and it genuinely speeds you up
  • Allergen training — increasingly expected in kitchens

The Exams: A short online multiple-choice test at the end of a food handler course.

Cost: Usually under $25, and often paid by the employer. Ask before paying for it yourself.

Keeping It Current: Food handler cards typically last two to three years.

6Salary & Job Outlook

North Dakota Food Preparation Worker Pay (May 2025):

  • Starting out (bottom 10–25%): $13.66–$15.15/hour ($28,400–$31,510/year)
  • Middle (median — half earn more, half earn less): $17.69/hour ($36,800/year)
  • Experienced (top 25–10%): $18.48–$21.08/hour ($38,430–$43,840/year)

What that means: The top of this range is usually a lead prep cook in a hospital, hotel, or large institutional kitchen rather than a small restaurant. Institutional kitchens also tend to offer day shifts and steadier hours than restaurants do.

Jobs in North Dakota: About 860 people do this work in ND today. By 2034, that should be about 870 (about 1% growth). Expect about 150 job openings every year in ND from growth and people retiring or changing jobs. Across the U.S., the change is growing slowly.

Outlook: Flat — about 1% growth in North Dakota, with roughly 150 openings a year. It is a smaller job than most on this page, and the real reason to take it is where it leads rather than how many of them there are.

Who Hires in ND: Restaurants, hotels, hospitals, nursing homes, school and college kitchens, grocery store delis, catering companies, and camp kitchens.

Work Setting: Indoors in a kitchen, often early mornings. Cooler and quieter than the line. Institutional kitchens are usually weekdays with no nights.

Physical Demands: Moderate to high. Standing for the whole shift, repetitive knife and hand work, lifting cases and stock pots, cold work in the walk-in.

Sources: U.S. Bureau of Labor Statistics, Occupational Employment and Wage Statistics, North Dakota, May 2025 (SOC 35-2021); North Dakota and U.S. long-term employment projections 2024–2034, via O*NET OnLine. Numbers are rounded.

7Where This Leads

Path 1: Prep Cook — Where you start.

Path 2: Lead Prep — Write the prep list, order stock, train new people.

Path 3: Line Cook — The usual next step — about the same wage today, but far more growth and five times the openings.

Path 4: Institutional Cook — Hospitals, schools, and care homes. Day shifts, steadier hours.

Path 5: Deli or Bakery Worker — Grocery store departments use the same skills.

Path 6: Kitchen Manager — Running the operation rather than working in it.

8The Real Talk

What's Great:

  • Pays about $3.20 an hour more than fast food
  • No school or experience needed
  • Mostly early shifts, often done before the evening rush
  • Quieter and less pressured than the line
  • The standard route into cooking, which is the fastest-growing job in this area
  • Hospital and school kitchens offer weekday day shifts

What's Hard:

  • Repetitive, and hours of knife work is hard on hands and wrists
  • Early starts, often before six in the morning
  • Flat growth and relatively few openings — you may wait for one
  • Cold work in the walk-in
  • Largely invisible — nobody eating ever knows you exist
  • Standing on a hard floor all shift

Be Honest: Can you do the same careful thing forty times without it getting sloppy on the thirtieth? Can you start work before six in the morning? Are you comfortable with work nobody sees?
