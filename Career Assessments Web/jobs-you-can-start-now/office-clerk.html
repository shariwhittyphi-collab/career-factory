Office Clerk Career Assessment

Records, mail, copies and the many tasks that keep an office moving

Section 1 of 11

1Welcome

Papers need filing. Mail needs sorting. Records need to be entered and found again later. Office clerks handle many of the tasks that keep an office running.

This job can be a first step into office work. Explore what it feels like before deciding whether it interests you.

Go at your pace. The short questions at the end are for reflection. There are no right or wrong answers.

2What is an Office Clerk?

You do a mix of office tasks. You file and scan records, enter information into computers, sort mail, make copies, and answer questions at a counter or on the phone. The exact mix depends on the office.

  • File, scan, and find paper and electronic records.
  • Enter information into computer systems and check it.
  • Sort and send mail and packages.
  • Make copies and prepare documents.
  • Answer phones and help people at a front counter.
  • Keep track of office supplies.

The real picture: Many clerk jobs are in government offices, schools, and large companies. Some are mostly computer work; others include more mail, counter work, or lifting boxes of records.

3A Day in the Life

One possible day: The schedule depends on the office.

  • Morning: Sort the mail and deliver it to the right desks.
  • Later: Scan a stack of old paper records and check that each file saved correctly.
  • Midday: Help a person at the counter who needs a form.
  • Afternoon: Enter information from forms into a computer system.
  • Before leaving: Put away files and note what still needs to be done.

Some work is repetitive. Many clerks like knowing exactly what needs to be done and seeing a stack get smaller.

4How You Learn the Job

Most office clerks learn on the job. Many employers ask for a high school diploma or GED. Read the actual job ad for what each employer wants.

  • Build basic skills first: Typing, careful data entry, email, and a simple spreadsheet.
  • Practice accuracy: Getting names, dates, and numbers exactly right matters more than speed at first.
  • On-the-job training: Each office teaches its own filing system, forms, and computer programs.
  • Optional education: Short office skills or computer classes can help. Free classes may be available at libraries and job centers.

Paying for training:

  1. Start with financial aid, scholarships, and grants. The school's financial aid office can help.
  2. Ask employers whether they help pay for training.
  3. Ask for the full cost, including books, fees, travel, and a computer.
  4. Ask your career counselor about other help, such as WIOA. WIOA is a federal program that can help some people pay for job training. Ask before you enroll.

Employer help with school may come with a promise to keep working there. Read the terms first.

5Training and Employer Requirements

No state license is needed for general office clerk work. Some specialized clerk jobs have their own rules. Ask the employer what you need to start.

  • Basic computer and typing skills.
  • Careful attention to detail.
  • A background check for some government, school, health, and bank jobs.
  • Training on privacy rules for the records you handle.
  • Some jobs include lifting mail bins or boxes; ask how much.

Before you begin: Ask who trains new staff and how you will know you are doing a task the right way.

Ask about written instructions, an accessible workstation, extra practice time, or other support that helps you learn and do the work well.

6Pay and Job Outlook

North Dakota office clerk pay — May 2025:

  • Middle pay: $24.11 an hour ($50,160 a year). Half of workers earned more and half earned less.
  • Half of workers earned between $20.52 and $29.86 an hour.
  • 1 in 10 earned less than $16.15 an hour, and 1 in 10 earned more than $35.93.
  • These numbers show what workers earned. They are not a promise of what a new worker will start at.
  • North Dakota had about 9,960 of these jobs in 2024. The state projects about 9,760 in 2034, a change of -2%. That works out to about 1,140 openings a year, mostly from people who retire or change jobs.

Where people work: State, city, and county offices; schools and colleges; hospitals and clinics; banks; and large and small businesses.

Work setting: An office with a desk and computer, plus time at copiers, scanners, mail areas, or a front counter.

Compare an offer: Ask about base pay, hours, benefits, paid time off, training, and whether the job is full time, part time, seasonal, or temporary.

7Where This Can Lead

  • Experienced clerk: Take on more complicated records and special tasks.
  • Administrative assistant: Add scheduling, documents, and more contact with people.
  • Specialized clerk: Billing, payroll, bookkeeping, or medical records may need extra training.
  • Office supervisor: Lead other clerks as experience grows.

Working inside a school, hospital, or government office can also help you learn about other jobs there.

8The Real Talk

Things you might like:

  • Clear tasks you can finish.
  • Indoor work with regular hours in many offices.
  • Jobs in many kinds of workplaces.
  • A way to learn how an organization works.

Things to ask about:

  • Repetitive tasks for long stretches.
  • Sitting and computer work for much of the day.
  • Lifting boxes or mail bins.
  • Busy times when many forms arrive at once.

North Dakota projects a small drop in office clerk jobs. There are still about 1,140 openings a year, mostly from people retiring or moving to other jobs.

A question for yourself: Do you like steady, careful work where you can see what you finished?
