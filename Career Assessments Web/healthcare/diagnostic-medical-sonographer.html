Diagnostic Medical Sonographer Career Assessment

Ultrasound technician pathway

Section 1 of 11

1Welcome

Diagnostic medical sonography is the art and science of using ultrasound to see inside the human body.

You'll learn what it's really like to be an ultrasound technician, the training required, and whether this career fits your goals.

This takes about 15 minutes.

2What is a Diagnostic Medical Sonographer?

A diagnostic medical sonographer uses ultrasound equipment to create images of the inside of the human body. These images help doctors diagnose and treat disease.

No X-rays. No radiation. Just sound waves. Ultrasound is safe for patients of all ages, including babies in the womb.

Your Job Includes:

  • Operate ultrasound machines (sophisticated technology)
  • Position patients and find the right scan angles
  • Take clear images of organs: heart, liver, kidneys, blood vessels, thyroid
  • Recognize abnormal findings and alert the radiologist
  • Document images and measurements
  • Work with patients who are sometimes in pain or distress
  • Communicate findings clearly to doctors
  • Handle sensitive situations (pregnant patients, ill patients)

You're part detective (finding the problem), part technician (running the equipment), and part healthcare worker (caring for patients).

3A Day in the Life

7:00 AM — Arrive. Prepare ultrasound rooms. Check equipment. Run quality assurance tests.

7:30 AM — Review schedule. Meet with radiologists about complex cases.

8:00 AM — First patient: 65-year-old man with chest pain. Do an echocardiogram (heart ultrasound). It's a 30-minute procedure. You're extremely focused.

8:45 AM — Report findings to radiologist. She sees something concerning. You help her get better images.

9:30 AM — Next patient: pregnant woman, 20-week checkup. She's anxious. You're calm, explain everything, show her the baby on the screen. She cries happy tears.

10:30 AM — Abdominal ultrasound on a patient with kidney pain. Image quality is critical for diagnosis.

11:30 AM — Document everything. Write detailed reports on findings.

12:00 PM — Lunch (might be at your desk).

1:00 PM – 4:30 PM — More scans. Vascular ultrasound. Thyroid ultrasound. Guided biopsies (using ultrasound to help doctors take tissue samples).

4:30 PM — Clean equipment. Sterilize probes. Document final charts.

5:00 PM — Done. Day was demanding but meaningful.

High Variation: Every day is different. Every patient is different. No two ultrasounds are identical.

4Training in North Dakota

Certification and License Required. In North Dakota, sonographers must have a state license. To get one, you need a national credential (such as ARDMS or ARRT sonography).

Education Pathways:

  • Associate Degree (2 years): Community college, hands-on training. Costs vary by school.
  • Bachelor's Degree (4 years): University program, advanced topics. Costs vary by school.
  • Specialized Certificates (1-2 years): After another healthcare background (X-ray tech, RN), move into sonography
  • X-Ray First, Then Ultrasound On the Job: Some hospitals and clinics train their X-ray technologists in ultrasound at work. ARDMS lets people with a 2-year patient-care degree (such as X-ray, nursing, or respiratory therapy) take its exams after 12 months of full-time, paid ultrasound work (at least 1,680 hours) in a hospital, clinic, or private practice. People with a bachelor's degree in any major can use the same 12-month work route. Volunteer or unpaid hours do not count. ND accepts ARDMS certification for the license. Ask the employer how they support this before you count on it.

In North Dakota: Bismarck State College (Bismarck) offers a 2-year AAS in sonography. NDSU and Sanford Health (Fargo) offer a bachelor's degree with a sonography or echocardiography (heart) track. Both are CAAHEP-accredited. Dakota College at Bottineau also offers an AAS at its Minot campus (with Trinity Health). Its accreditation page does not list sonography accreditation, so ask the school which ARDMS exams its graduates can take. Programs are competitive — both BSC and Sanford require at least a 3.0 GPA.

Nearby Out-of-State Sonography Programs (each school states it is CAAHEP-accredited)

  • Southeast Technical College (Sioux Falls, SD): Associate degree (AAS), about 24 months. Also has a separate cardiac (heart) sonography degree. You do not need a college degree to apply.
  • Mayo Clinic School of Health Sciences (Rochester, MN): 21-month certificate. You must already have an associate degree or higher (or attend one of Mayo's partner colleges), plus a CNA or other healthcare certification.
  • St. Cloud Technical & Community College (St. Cloud, MN): Associate degree (AAS). Very competitive (15 seats). Most students already have a college degree.
  • Concordia University (St. Paul, MN): Associate degree (AAS), about 2 years. Hybrid format.
  • St. Catherine University (St. Paul, MN): Bachelor's degree.

A common path for North Dakotans: Take about 2 years of college first (for example at the University of Mary or a state college) to finish the prerequisite classes. If that ends in an associate degree, it also meets Mayo's degree requirement. Then apply to a sonography program.

Tip: Montana has no CAAHEP-accredited sonography program. Always confirm accreditation at caahep.org before you enroll.

Clinical Training: You'll spend 50%+ of your time in real hospitals scanning real patients under supervision. This is essential.

Subspecialties (advanced): After initial training, you can specialize in cardiac, vascular, ob/gyn, abdominal, or neurosonography. This can open more job options.

5Certification

ARDMS (American Registry for Diagnostic Medical Sonography) — Registered Diagnostic Medical Sonographer (RDMS)

The industry standard. Most employers require it.

Requirements:

  • Complete an accredited sonography program, OR
  • Have a 2-year healthcare degree or any bachelor's degree, plus 12 months of full-time ultrasound clinical experience, OR
  • Already hold a credential from ARRT or CCI

The Exams: You pass two exams. First, the SPI physics exam (about 110 questions, 2 hours). Second, a specialty exam such as Abdomen or OB/GYN.

Cost: About $575 total ($275 for SPI + $300 for one specialty exam, such as Abdomen or OB/GYN)

Pass Rate: Pass rates vary by exam. Ask programs for their graduates' pass rates.

Continuing Education: Every 3 years, complete continuing education credits (online courses, conferences), and pay a yearly ARDMS renewal fee. Your ND license renews every 2 years.

6Salary & Job Outlook

North Dakota Diagnostic Medical Sonographer Salary (BLS, May 2025):

  • Entry-level (low end of ND pay): about $32/hour ($67K/year)
  • Median (middle): about $40.55/hour ($84,340/year)
  • Experienced (top 10%): about $49/hour ($103K/year) or more

Excellent Benefits: Health insurance, retirement, paid time off, tuition reimbursement for continuing education.

Job Outlook: Growing fast. BLS projects 14% growth nationwide (2025-2035). North Dakota expects about 20% growth by 2034 (about 250 jobs in 2024, about 20 openings a year).

Job Market in ND: Hospitals, clinics, imaging centers, and private practices all hire sonographers.

Work Environment: Mostly Monday-Friday, 8am-5pm, though some on-call duties possible. Some evening/weekend shifts.

7Where Does This Lead?

Path 1: Specialize — Cardiac (heart), vascular (vessels), ob/gyn (pregnancy), neuro (brain). Higher pay. More interesting cases.

Path 2: Administration — Manage imaging department, schedule, staff, budget. Business skills valued.

Path 3: Quality Assurance — Ensure all images meet standards. Teach other sonographers.

Path 4: Sales/Support — Work for ultrasound equipment companies. Train facilities on new machines.

Path 5: Education — Teach sonography. Develop curriculum. Work in academia.

Path 6: Nursing or Physician Assistant — Your ultrasound background helps you understand imaging. Some sonographers go this route.

Path 7: Research — Work on new techniques, new applications of ultrasound. Hospitals and universities.

Portable Career: Your ARDMS credential is recognized nationwide. Each state has its own license rules.

8The Real Talk

What's Great:

  • Technology-focused. You enjoy learning equipment.
  • Every scan is a puzzle. Problem-solving daily.
  • Impact on diagnosis and treatment
  • Flexible career paths
  • Job security
  • Decent salary without 10+ years of school

What's Hard:

  • Hand/wrist strain. Repetitive motion all day.
  • Standing for long periods
  • Difficult patients (pain, anxiety, noncompliance)
  • Pressure to get perfect images quickly
  • HIPAA compliance and patient privacy (serious responsibility)
  • Seeing serious diagnoses (cancer, fetal abnormalities, etc.)
  • Technical equipment fails sometimes
  • On-call duties in some facilities
  • Continuing education is mandatory and costs money

Be Honest: Do you like technology? Can you stay focused for 30+ minutes? Are you patient with anxious people? Can you handle seeing serious health issues? If mostly yes, this is good.
