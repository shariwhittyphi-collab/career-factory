Medical Assistant Career Assessment

Clinical and administrative healthcare role

Section 1 of 11

1Welcome

Medical assistants are the backbone of healthcare offices and clinics. You'll do both clinical and administrative work.

This assessment will help you understand the job, what it takes to succeed, and whether it's right for you.

This takes about 15 minutes.

2What is a Medical Assistant?

A medical assistant works in hospitals, clinics, physician offices, and other healthcare settings. You do two jobs in one: clinical work and office work.

Clinical Duties:

  • Vital signs (blood pressure, temperature, pulse, respiration)
  • Prepare patients for exams
  • Assist with procedures (blood draws, injections, EKGs)
  • Collect patient samples
  • Sterilize instruments
  • Answer patient questions

Administrative Duties:

  • Schedule appointments
  • Answer phones
  • Check patients in
  • File medical records
  • Handle billing and insurance
  • Maintain patient confidentiality

You're part of the clinical team and the business team. You need both skills.

3A Day in the Life

8:00 AM — Arrive early. Log into computer. Check schedule. Prepare exam rooms.

8:30 AM — First patient arrives. Welcome them. Check insurance. Update records. Escort to exam room.

8:45 AM — Take vital signs. Patient is nervous about blood pressure. You reassure them. It's normal.

9:00 AM — Doctor comes in. You assist with exam. Hand instruments. Answer questions the doctor asks you to clarify.

9:20 AM — Patient needs a blood draw. You're trained and certified. You do it smoothly. Patient is relieved.

9:45 AM — Return to front desk. Schedule that patient's follow-up. Process insurance paperwork. Answer phones.

10:00 AM – 12:00 PM — Cycle repeats. More patients. More vitals. More paperwork. Phone keeps ringing.

12:00 PM — Lunch (usually quick)

1:00 PM – 5:00 PM — Afternoon patients. More clinical, more administrative. You're multitasking constantly.

5:00 PM — Close out charts. Make sure everything is documented. Log off.

Fast-paced. Varied. Patient-focused. Never boring.

4Training in North Dakota

Certification NOT Required in ND (like most states). You can work as an MA without certification.

BUT — Most employers prefer or require it. Certification shows you know what you're doing. To give medicines or shots in ND, you must be nationally certified and registered with the ND Board of Nursing as a Medication Assistant III (MA III). Online-only training does not qualify.

Training Pathways:

  • Certificate Program (about 1 year or less): College program with clinical and administrative training. Costs vary, so check with the program.
  • Associate Degree (2 years): More comprehensive. Includes general education. Costs vary, so check with the program.
  • On-the-Job Training: Some clinics hire and train MAs on the job. No tuition. But to give medicines in ND, you still need national certification and in-person medication training (MA III).

In North Dakota: Dakota College at Bottineau (Bottineau, Minot, or online; certificate or associate degree, about 1 year for the certificate) and Bismarck State College (Bismarck or online; certificate) offer programs.

Prerequisites: High school diploma/GED. Some clinical labs, good communication.

5Certification

National Board Certification (CMA or RMA)

  • CMA (AAMA): Certified Medical Assistant (American Association of Medical Assistants). Well known.
  • RMA: Registered Medical Assistant (American Medical Technologists). Also well-respected.
  • NCMA or CCMA: Other national certifications (NCCT or NHA). ND college programs prepare you for the RMA, NCMA, or CCMA exam.

Requirements for CMA:

  • Complete a medical assistant program accredited by CAAHEP or ABHES, OR
  • Complete another qualifying MA program with a practicum (AAMA "Alternative Pathway")

The Exam: 200 multiple choice questions, 160 minutes (plus breaks). Tests clinical and administrative knowledge.

Cost: $125 for AAMA members, $250 for non-members

Pass Rate: About 69% of first-time test takers passed (July 2024 to April 2025)

Continuing Education: The CMA (AAMA) must be renewed every 5 years with 60 CE points (or by retaking the exam). Other certifications have their own renewal rules.

6Salary & Job Outlook

North Dakota Medical Assistant Pay (BLS, May 2025):

  • Starting pay (lowest 10%): about $18/hour ($37,650/year)
  • Middle (median): $22.11/hour ($45,980/year)
  • Top-paid (top 10%): about $28/hour ($58,230/year) or more

Benefits: Health insurance, retirement, paid time off, continuing education support.

Job Outlook: Excellent. Medical assistants are in high demand. Aging population needs more healthcare. BLS projects 13% job growth nationally from 2025 to 2035, much faster than average, with about 109,700 openings each year.

Work Environment: Mostly Monday-Friday, 8am-5pm or 9am-6pm. Some specialty clinics may have evening/weekend hours.

Job Market in ND: Hospitals, clinics, private practices, urgent care, specialty offices — all hire MAs. Certified MAs have the most options.

7Career Advancement

Path 1: Specialize — Phlebotomy certification, EKG certification, X-ray assistant training. Higher pay.

Path 2: Front Office — Office manager, receptionist supervisor, scheduling coordinator. Less clinical, more administrative.

Path 3: Clinical Coordinator — Manage clinical staff, maintain equipment, ensure quality.

Path 4: Nursing — Many MAs go to nursing school. You already understand healthcare. LPN or RN from here.

Path 5: Physician Assistant (PA) or Nurse Practitioner (NP) — Your MA experience helps you understand the field. Ladder-building career.

Path 6: Other Healthcare Roles — Dental assisting, medical coding, health information management. Your skills transfer.

Path 7: Stay and Grow — Become an experienced MA, mentor new staff, run a tight clinic. Respected role.

8The Real Talk

What's Great:

  • Fast-paced. No boredom.
  • Direct patient interaction. You help people.
  • Variety (clinical + administrative)
  • Entry point to healthcare careers
  • Good job security
  • Flexible work options available

What's Hard:

  • Standing all day. Wear good shoes.
  • Multitasking constantly. Phones, patients, paperwork, all at once.
  • Emotional labor. Sick patients, anxious patients, difficult patients.
  • Blood, bodily fluids. You see and handle it.
  • Infection control. HIPAA. Strict rules.
  • Can be underpaid for the work.
  • Exposure to illness (especially during cold/flu season)
  • Time pressure. Schedules are tight.

Be Real: Do you like helping patients? Can you multitask? Can you stay calm when the schedule is crazy? If yes, you can do this.
