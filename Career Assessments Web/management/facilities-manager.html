Facilities Manager Career Assessment

Boilers, roofs, and the 2 AM call when the heat goes out at forty below

Section 1 of 11

1Welcome

A facilities manager keeps the building alive. Heating and cooling, plumbing, electrical, roofs, elevators, grounds, parking lots, snow, security, and every contractor who works on any of it. In North Dakota that means you own the hardest weather in the country.

This is the most hands-on manager job in this series and the one most likely to wake you up at night. About 300 people do it in North Dakota. This page tells you what it really is.

This takes about 15 minutes.

2What is a Facilities Manager?

A facilities manager is responsible for the physical building and everything in it that has to keep working. You run the maintenance crew or the contracts with the companies that do the work. You schedule preventive maintenance so the boiler does not fail in January. You manage the budget for repairs and for the big projects — a new roof, a chiller replacement, a parking lot. You handle inspections, fire safety, and the code requirements that come with an occupied building. In North Dakota you also own snow and ice, which is not a small line item and is not optional.

The Reality: This job comes up from the trades far more often than it comes down from a business degree. Most facilities managers were maintenance techs, HVAC techs, electricians, or plumbers first, and the reason they got the job is that they can tell when a contractor is lying to them. Then the second honest part: you are on call. When the heat fails at 2 AM at forty below, or a pipe bursts over a weekend, or eight inches falls before a Monday, your phone rings and you get dressed. Some employers pay for that. Many just expect it. Ask about it in the interview, because it is the single biggest difference between two facilities jobs that look identical on paper.

Your Responsibilities:

  • Keep the heating, cooling, plumbing, and electrical systems running
  • Schedule preventive maintenance so things fail on your calendar, not theirs
  • Supervise maintenance staff or manage the contractors who do the work
  • Build and defend the repair budget and the capital project budget
  • Handle fire safety, inspections, and building code requirements
  • Plan and oversee big projects — roofs, boilers, parking lots, remodels
  • Manage snow removal, grounds, and parking through a North Dakota winter
  • Take the emergency call, whatever time it comes

You are the reason the building is warm, dry, lit, and safe.

3A Day in the Life

6:30 AM — Phone call: no heat in the east wing. Meet the HVAC tech at the building.

7:30 AM — It is a failed circulating pump. Approve the part, get it ordered, move on.

9:00 AM — Walk the roof with a contractor who is bidding the replacement. Ask hard questions.

10:30 AM — Fire inspection walkthrough. Two extinguishers are out of date. Note them.

12:00 PM — Lunch, interrupted twice.

1:00 PM — Build the capital request for next year’s boiler work with real numbers.

3:00 PM — Meet the snow removal contractor about this winter’s contract and response times.

4:30 PM — Walk the mechanical room before you leave. Listen. Something sounds different.

Half in the office, half in the mechanical room, and always on call.

4Training in North Dakota

Most facilities managers came up through a trade. IFMA credentials are the recognized add-on. There is no license for the job itself, though the trade license you may already hold is worth a great deal here. The usual road is trade work, then lead maintenance, then facilities manager. IFMA — the International Facility Management Association — offers the two credentials employers actually recognize, and they are the fastest way to turn trade experience into a management title.

Training Pathways:

  • Trade first, then up (5 to 10 years): HVAC, electrical, plumbing, or building maintenance, then lead maintenance tech, then facilities manager. This is the most common road and the one that earns the most respect from the crew, because you can actually tell whether a contractor’s bid is honest.
  • Trade plus the IFMA FMP credential (add 4 to 12 months): The Facility Management Professional is four online modules with no prerequisites. It adds the business side — finance, operations and maintenance, leadership and strategy, project management — to hands-on experience you already have. This is the highest-value combination for someone already in the trades.
  • Two-year technical degree, then a bachelor’s completion (4 years total): A ND two-year degree in HVAC, electrical, or construction, then a facilities or construction management bachelor’s. North Dakota does not have a facility management degree, so this road usually means finishing online out of state.

Where to Train in North Dakota:

  • ND State College of Science (Wahpeton) — Electrical Technology, HVAC and building trades, and Robotics, Automation and Mechatronics Technology; these are the trade degrees facilities managers most often come from (catalog.ndscs.edu)
  • Bismarck State College (Bismarck) — Electric Power Technology, Power Process Technology, Energy Services & Renewable Technician, and Energy Management, plus a Bachelor of Applied Science in Energy Management (bismarckstate.edu/academics/programs and catalog.bismarckstate.edu/catalog/degrees/bas)
  • Bismarck State College (Bismarck) — Management AAS and the Leadership certificate, online or on campus, for the supervision side (catalog.bismarckstate.edu/catalog/degrees/career-technical-education/management)
  • North Dakota State University (Fargo) — Construction Management major, the closest four-year ND degree to this work (catalog.ndsu.edu/curriculum/undergraduate/construction-management-major)
  • Honest note: no North Dakota college offers a facility management degree. The nearest one is Dunwoody College of Technology in Minneapolis, which offers a fully online two-year Bachelor of Science completion in Facilities Management for people who already hold a two-year technical degree in construction, electrical, HVAC, welding, or surveying. Dunwoody says its first graduates finish in spring 2028, so it is brand new (dunwoody.edu/construction/facilities-management)
  • IFMA (ifma.org) — the FMP and CFM credentials, both earned online from anywhere, which is how most ND facilities managers get credentialed

Before You Start: A high school diploma or GED. Real hands-on knowledge of building systems, usually from a trade. A driver’s license. The ability to read a mechanical drawing and a contractor’s bid. A phone you will answer at night.

Tip: Start with financial aid, scholarships, and any help an employer offers — that is where the money starts. After that, ask a career counselor or Job Service North Dakota whether WIOA or other programs can help with what is left over, like training, tools, work boots, or exam fees. What can be paid for depends on the program and on your own situation, so ask before you sign up rather than after. Registered apprenticeships let you earn a paycheck while you learn — see jobsnd.com/job-seeker/apprenticeships for ND programs.

5Certification & Licensing

No license required for the title, but IFMA’s FMP and CFM are the recognized credentials. The job itself is not licensed. If you hold a trade license already — electrical, plumbing, boiler operator — keep it, because it makes you far harder to fool and far harder to replace. On top of that, IFMA offers two credentials that employers know by name.

IFMA Credentials and Trade Licenses:

  • FMP (Facility Management Professional), IFMA — no prerequisites at all. Four modules: Finance & Business, Operations & Maintenance, Leadership & Strategy, and Project Management. IFMA says it takes 4 to 12 months at your own pace, or 4 to 6 weeks with instructor-led virtual courses, and that over 18,000 people hold it (ifma.org)
  • FMP cost — IFMA publishes individual modules at $550 for full members and $680 standard, or a bundle at $1,850 for full members and $2,150 standard. Group pricing applies for two or more people (ifma.org)
  • CFM (Certified Facility Manager), IFMA — the senior credential. IFMA lists two ways in: five years of facility management experience with any education, or three years of FM experience plus a bachelor’s or master’s degree in facility management. Ethics training is also required within 30 days of passing (ifma.org)
  • CFM exam and cost — 120 multiple-choice questions in three hours. IFMA publishes $615 for full members and $910 for non-members (ifma.org)
  • Your trade license — an ND electrical, plumbing, or boiler license held by the facilities manager is worth real money to an employer, because it means some work does not have to be contracted out at all

The Exams: The FMP has a final assessment at the end of each of the four modules rather than one big exam. The CFM is a single 120-question multiple-choice exam with a three-hour limit (ifma.org).

Cost: FMP: $550 per module for IFMA full members or $680 standard, or a four-module bundle at $1,850 member and $2,150 standard. CFM exam: $615 member, $910 non-member. Many ND employers will pay for the FMP because it makes you better at spending their money. Ask before you enroll.

Keeping It Current: IFMA requires continuing maintenance to keep the CFM current, so ask IFMA for its published recertification cycle and fees before you sit the exam. A trade license renews on its own board’s schedule.

6Salary & Job Outlook

North Dakota Facilities Manager Pay (May 2025):

  • Starting out (bottom 10–25%): $29.86–$39.38/hour ($62,120–$81,910/year)
  • Middle (median — half earn more, half earn less): $44.25/hour ($92,040/year)
  • Experienced (top 25–10%): $58.70–$69.25/hour ($122,100–$144,050/year)

What that means: The middle North Dakota facilities manager earns about $92,040 a year, against a national middle of about $106,660. That is a real gap of about $14,600, and it widens at the top: the ND 90th percentile is about $144,050 while the national 90th is about $176,120. At the lower end the two are much closer — ND’s 25th percentile of about $81,910 is almost identical to the national $82,020. Read that as: a newer facilities manager here is paid about what one is paid anywhere, but an experienced one in North Dakota does not reach what an experienced one reaches in a bigger market. On-call pay, or the lack of it, is the other thing that moves the real number. Two jobs paying the same salary are not the same job if one of them calls you out six nights a winter for free.

Jobs in North Dakota: About 300 people do this work in ND today. By 2034, that should be about 310 (about 5% growth). Expect about 30 job openings every year in ND from growth and people retiring or changing jobs. Across the U.S., the change is about 4% growth.

Outlook: Fair, and small but steady. O*NET shows North Dakota going from about 300 facilities managers in 2024 to about 310 in 2034, roughly 5% growth, with about 30 openings a year. That is modest, but it is a job that cannot be sent anywhere else — the building is here, so the person who runs it is here. Nationally the job grows about 4% with 13,200 openings a year, so ND is very slightly ahead of the country. The demand in this state is steadier than the raw number looks, because every hospital, school, college, big office, and government building needs one, and the ND climate punishes any employer that tries to go without. If 30 openings a year is too thin for your timeline, the closest neighbours with more room are general and operations manager, at about 1,050 ND openings a year, and first-line supervisor of mechanics and maintenance workers.

Who Hires in ND: Hospitals and clinics, school districts, colleges and universities, state and city government, large office buildings and property management companies, hotels, banks, manufacturing plants, and big retail. In ND, health care and education are the two biggest employers of facilities managers.

Work Setting: Split between an office and the building itself — mechanical rooms, roofs, crawl spaces, parking lots, and the boiler room. You are outside in ND winter more than any other manager in this series. Regular weekday hours plus on-call nights and weekends. Snow does not care what day it is.

Physical Demands: Moderate to high, and the highest of these seven jobs. Climbing ladders and stairs, walking roofs, getting into tight mechanical spaces, working around noise and heat, and being outside at twenty below to look at a frozen line. You are not doing the heavy work every day, but you have to be able to get to where it is.

Sources: U.S. Bureau of Labor Statistics, Occupational Employment and Wage Statistics, North Dakota, May 2025 (SOC 11-3013); North Dakota and U.S. long-term employment projections 2024–2034, via O*NET OnLine. Numbers are rounded.

7Where This Leads

Path 1: Maintenance Technician — Learn the building systems with your hands.

Path 2: Lead Maintenance Tech — Run the day for a small crew.

Path 3: Facilities Supervisor — Own the schedule, the work orders, and part of the budget.

Path 4: Facilities Manager — The whole building, the whole budget, and the phone at night.

Path 5: Director of Facilities — Several buildings or a whole campus.

Path 6: Energy or Project Manager — Specialize in big capital projects or in cutting utility costs.

8The Real Talk

What's Great:

  • Your trade experience is the qualification, not a degree
  • The FMP credential has no prerequisites, so you can start it today
  • The work cannot be moved out of state — the building is here
  • You see a problem, you fix it, and it stays fixed
  • Middle pay of about $92,040 with a top tenth near $144,050
  • Hospitals, schools, and government all need this, so the job is recession-steady

What's Hard:

  • On call. The 2 AM heat failure at forty below is a real Tuesday, not a story
  • ND pay trails the country by about $14,600 at the middle and by more at the top
  • Only about 30 openings a year statewide
  • You are outside in ND weather when the weather is the emergency
  • You get blamed for the building whether or not anyone funded the repair you asked for
  • Budgets get cut and the boiler ages anyway

Be Honest: Will you answer the phone at 2 AM and go? Can you argue for a repair budget, lose, and still keep the building running? Do you know enough about how buildings work to tell a good contractor from an expensive one? And can you be outside in North Dakota winter when things break, because that is exactly when they break?
