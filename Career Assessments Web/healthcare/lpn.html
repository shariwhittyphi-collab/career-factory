Licensed Practical Nurse (LPN)

Career Assessment Tool — North Dakota

This tool will help you decide if becoming a Licensed Practical Nurse is the right choice for you.

You will learn about what LPNs really do, how to get trained in North Dakota, and what comes next in your career.

Important: Answer all questions honestly. This is just for you—there are no wrong answers.

What is a Licensed Practical Nurse?

A Licensed Practical Nurse, or LPN, provides direct patient care under the supervision of a Registered Nurse (RN) or physician. LPNs work in hospitals, clinics, nursing homes, home health care, and other healthcare settings. LPNs perform nursing tasks that are more advanced than a CNA but less independent than an RN. This is a mid-level nursing position that bridges the gap between entry-level and advanced nursing roles.

What Will You Actually Be Doing Every Day?

Here are the real tasks LPNs do:

  • Provide basic patient care (bathing, grooming, toileting)
  • Take and record vital signs (blood pressure, temperature, pulse, breathing)
  • Administer medications orally and by injection (under RN supervision)
  • Change dressings on wounds and monitor healing
  • Assist patients with eating and drinking
  • Help patients move and exercise
  • Collect blood and urine samples for testing
  • Insert and monitor catheters
  • Chart patient information in medical records
  • Report patient changes and concerns to the RN immediately
  • Comfort and communicate with patients
  • Assist with medical procedures and examinations
  • Help teach patients about their medications and care
What You Need to Know: LPN work is more complex than CNA work but still physically demanding. You will be on your feet for long shifts. You must understand medications and recognize when something is wrong with a patient. You will work nights, weekends, and holidays. The work requires both hands-on skills and thinking skills. You are responsible for patient safety and must work under RN supervision at all times. You will face emotional situations and sometimes difficult patients.

What Training Do You Need in North Dakota?

The Requirements:

  • Complete a Board-approved Practical Nursing program (about 1 year for a certificate; 2 years for an associate degree)
  • Programs include classroom learning, skills labs, and clinical practice
  • Complete clinical rotations in different healthcare settings
  • Pass the NCLEX-PN exam (National Council Licensure Examination for Practical Nurses)
  • Apply for licensure with the North Dakota Board of Nursing
  • Background check and health screening requirements

Prerequisites:

  • High school diploma or GED
  • Minimum age requirements (usually 18)
  • Some programs may require prerequisite courses (anatomy, physiology, chemistry)
  • CPR certification (the Dakota Nursing Program requires current BLS CPR before you apply)

What is the NCLEX-PN Exam?

After you finish your LPN program, you must pass the NCLEX-PN (National Council Licensure Examination for Practical Nurses). This is a computerized exam that tests your knowledge and judgment as a practical nurse. Here is what it covers:

What the Exam Tests:

  • Safe and effective care of patients
  • Medication administration and safety
  • How to recognize changes in a patient's condition
  • Communication and patient teaching
  • Understanding anatomy, physiology, and disease processes
  • Infection control and safety procedures
  • Legal and ethical responsibilities of an LPN
  • How to work with the nursing team
  • Basic nursing care procedures

Exam Format:

  • Computer-based adaptive testing
  • Between 85 and 150 questions (varies per test taker)
  • Time limit: 5 hours total (includes tutorial and breaks)
  • Questions get harder as you answer correctly; easier if you answer incorrectly
  • Official results come from the Board of Nursing, usually within 6 weeks (unofficial quick results may be bought after 2 business days)
About the Exam: You can take the NCLEX-PN soon after you graduate, once the Board of Nursing says you are eligible to test. The exam fee is $200. If you do not pass, you must wait at least 45 days to try again, and you can test up to 8 times a year. Your LPN program will prepare you. Many programs include NCLEX prep courses.

What Comes After LPN?

Being an LPN opens many doors for your nursing career:

Path 1: Advance to Registered Nurse (RN)

This can take about 1 more year. In the Dakota Nursing Program, LPNs can go on to a second year to earn an associate degree and become an RN. Many LPNs do this. You gain more independence and make significantly more money. Many employers support LPNs going back to school for their RN.

Path 2: Specialize in a Nursing Area

You can get additional certifications and training:

  • IV therapy certification
  • Wound care specialist
  • Oncology (cancer care)
  • Pediatric (children) nursing
  • Geriatric (elderly care) certification
  • Hospice and palliative care
  • Home health nursing

Path 3: Advance Within LPN Roles

Many LPNs advance without additional degrees:

  • Charge nurse positions
  • Nursing supervisor or manager roles
  • Clinic nurse manager
  • Staff educator or trainer for new nurses
  • Quality assurance roles

Path 4: Stay as an LPN and Build Experience

Many LPNs have fulfilling, well-paying careers without advancing to RN. You develop deep expertise in your specialty area and become trusted by patients and doctors.

Should You Become an LPN?

LPNs are vital to healthcare. You work with patients every day and directly impact their recovery and comfort.

LPN is a good choice if:

  • You want more responsibility and independence than a CNA role offers
  • You are ready for about a year of focused study and training
  • You can handle medications and critical thinking about patient care
  • You want to work in healthcare without the time/cost of a 4-year RN degree
  • You are interested in specializing in a nursing area
  • You want earning potential of about $51,000-$74,000 per year
  • You can work nights, weekends, and holidays
  • You handle stress and difficult situations well

Think twice if:

  • You do not like taking responsibility for decisions
  • You cannot go back to school for about a year
  • You are afraid of needles or giving injections
  • You cannot handle the emotional side of healthcare
  • You cannot work flexible shifts including nights and weekends
  • You do not want to work under another nurse's supervision
  • The cost of LPN training worries you (though scholarships exist)
The Choice is Yours: LPN training is shorter and less expensive than RN training, but offers more pay and responsibility than CNA work. Take this assessment honestly. Research programs. Talk to working LPNs. Then decide what's right for you.
