Social and Human Service Assistant Career Assessment

Connecting people with the services and support they need

Section 1 of 11

1Welcome

Applying for help can be confusing and stressful. Human service assistants help people find their way through it.

This job mixes helping, organizing, and problem solving. Explore what it feels like before deciding whether it interests you.

Go at your pace. The short questions at the end are for reflection. There are no right or wrong answers.

2What is a Social and Human Service Assistant?

You help people get services like food, housing, health care, and job help. You often work alongside social workers and case managers.

  • Meet with people to learn what they need.
  • Help fill out applications and gather papers.
  • Connect people with programs and agencies.
  • Keep records and write reports.
  • Visit people at home or in the community.
  • Follow up to see how things are going.

The real picture: Job titles vary: case aide, family support worker, outreach worker, or program assistant.

3A Day in the Life

One possible day: Every agency is different.

  • Morning: Check messages and meet with a supervisor about new cases.
  • Later: Help a family apply for food assistance.
  • Midday: Help at the food pantry.
  • Afternoon: Call agencies to find housing help for a client.
  • Before leaving: Update case notes.

Some days you see quick wins. Other problems take weeks to solve.

4How You Learn the Job

Paths vary. Some employers want a bachelor’s degree, and others hire people with an associate degree, some college, or experience.

  • North Dakota programs: Human services programs are offered at Bismarck State College, United Tribes Technical College, the University of Mary, Valley City State University, Nueta Hidatsa Sahnish College, Turtle Mountain College, Sitting Bull College, and Rasmussen University in Fargo.
  • On-the-job training: Agencies teach their programs and record systems.
  • Start nearby: Volunteering or front-desk work at an agency can open the door.

Paying for training:

  1. Start with financial aid, scholarships, and grants. The school's financial aid office can help.
  2. Ask employers whether they help pay for training.
  3. Ask for the full cost, including books, fees, travel, and a computer.
  4. Ask your career counselor about other help, such as WIOA. WIOA is a federal program that can help some people pay for job training. Ask before you enroll.

Employer help with school may come with a promise to keep working there. Read the terms first.

5Training and Employer Requirements

We did not find a North Dakota license for this job. Requirements depend on the employer. Ask what you need.

  • A high school diploma, and often some college or a degree.
  • A background check.
  • A driver’s license for home and community visits.
  • Comfort with computers and record keeping.

Before you begin: Ask about caseload size and how supervisors support new staff.

Ask about written instructions, an accessible workstation, extra practice time, or other support that helps you learn and do the work well.

6Pay and Job Outlook

North Dakota social and human service assistant pay — May 2025:

  • Middle pay: $22.24 an hour ($46,260 a year). Half of workers earned more and half earned less.
  • Half of workers earned between $18.88 and $26.10 an hour.
  • 1 in 10 earned less than $17.92 an hour, and 1 in 10 earned more than $31.81.
  • These numbers show what workers earned. They are not a promise of what a new worker will start at.
  • North Dakota had about 1,330 of these jobs in 2024. The state projects about 1,490 in 2034, a change of +12%. That works out to about 160 openings a year, mostly from people who retire or change jobs.

Where people work: Human service zones and county agencies, nonprofits, tribal programs, shelters, and community action agencies.

Work setting: Offices, homes, and community settings. Some jobs include evenings.

Compare an offer: Ask about base pay, caseload, mileage pay, benefits, and help paying for school.

7Where This Can Lead

  • Case manager: Handle more complex cases.
  • Social worker: With a social work degree and license.
  • Program coordinator: Run a community program.
  • Specialist: Focus on youth, older adults, housing, or disability services.

Ask people in your agency which paths are open.

8The Real Talk

Things you might like:

  • Helping your own community.
  • Seeing problems get solved.
  • Many kinds of agencies to choose from.
  • Growing demand in North Dakota.

Things to ask about:

  • Heavy caseloads and paperwork.
  • Stress from people’s hard situations.
  • Pay that may start modest.
  • Rules that limit what you can do.

North Dakota projects about 12% growth in these jobs from 2024 to 2034, with about 160 openings a year.

A question for yourself: Do you enjoy solving practical problems so people get the help they need?
