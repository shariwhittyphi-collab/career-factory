Administrative Assistant Career Assessment

Keeping an office organized so everyone else can do their work

Section 1 of 11

1Welcome

Someone answers the phone. Someone knows where the file is. Someone set up the meeting and made sure everyone had what they needed. In many offices, that person is the administrative assistant.

This career mixes computer work, people skills, and organization. Explore what it feels like before deciding whether it interests you.

Go at your pace. The short questions at the end are for reflection. There are no right or wrong answers.

2What is an Administrative Assistant?

You help an office run smoothly. You answer phones and greet visitors, keep calendars, prepare documents, and keep files in order. You might work for one boss or for a whole team.

  • Answer phones, take messages, and send calls to the right person.
  • Greet visitors and help them find what they need.
  • Schedule and confirm appointments and meetings.
  • Type letters, notes, and short reports.
  • Enter information into computer systems and keep files organized.
  • Order supplies and arrange repairs for office equipment.

The real picture: The job changes a lot from one office to the next. A school office, a clinic, a farm cooperative, and a state agency all need administrative help, but the details and the pace are different.

3A Day in the Life

One possible day: Every office runs on its own schedule.

  • Morning: Check email and voicemail, then look at today's calendar.
  • Later: Greet a visitor, answer several calls, and fix a scheduling mix-up.
  • Midday: Print and put together packets for an afternoon meeting.
  • Afternoon: Enter information into a database and file the paperwork.
  • Before leaving: Write down what is unfinished and set up tomorrow's first tasks.

Some days are quiet. Others bring many interruptions at once. Learning to keep a list and come back to a task is part of the job.

4How You Learn the Job

A four-year degree is not the usual entry route. About half of the people in this job say a high school diploma or GED is what the job needs. Many learn the office's systems on the job. Some employers ask for an associate degree or office experience, so read the actual job ad.

  • Build basic skills first: Typing, email, a calendar, word processing, and a simple spreadsheet. The programs offices list most are Microsoft Outlook, Word, Excel, and PowerPoint.
  • Start nearby: A receptionist, front desk, or office clerk job can lead to administrative work.
  • On-the-job training: Each office teaches its own phone system, records, and rules.
  • Optional education: North Dakota schools offering administrative assistant certificates or associate degrees include Bismarck State College, Cankdeska Cikana Community College in Fort Totten, Dickinson State University, Dakota College at Bottineau, Williston State College, and Rasmussen University in Fargo. Compare cost, length, and whether classes are online.

Paying for training:

  1. Start with financial aid, scholarships, and grants. The school's financial aid office can help.
  2. Ask employers whether they help pay for training.
  3. Ask for the full cost, including books, fees, travel, and a computer.
  4. Ask your career counselor about other help, such as WIOA. WIOA is a federal program that can help some people pay for job training. Ask before you enroll.

Employer help with school may come with a promise to keep working there. Read the terms first.

5Training and Employer Requirements

No state license is needed for general administrative assistant work. Some specialized roles have their own rules. Requirements depend on the employer. Ask what you need to start.

  • Comfort with a computer, email, and common office programs.
  • Clear phone manners and careful spelling.
  • A background check for jobs in schools, clinics, banks, or government offices.
  • Training in the office's own systems, which the employer usually provides.
  • Optional office skills certificates; ask whether an employer actually wants one before you pay for it.

Before you begin: Ask who trains new staff, how long training lasts, and who you can go to with questions.

Ask about written instructions, an accessible workstation, extra practice time, or other support that helps you learn and do the work well.

6Pay and Job Outlook

North Dakota administrative assistant pay — May 2025:

  • Middle pay: $22.83 an hour ($47,480 a year). Half of workers earned more and half earned less.
  • Half of workers earned between $19.36 and $25.91 an hour.
  • 1 in 10 earned less than $16.79 an hour, and 1 in 10 earned more than $29.22.
  • These numbers show what workers earned. They are not a promise of what a new worker will start at.
  • North Dakota had about 6,490 of these jobs in 2024. The state projects about 6,640 in 2034, a change of +2%. That works out to about 720 openings a year, mostly from people who retire or change jobs.

Where people work: Schools, clinics, government offices, farm and energy companies, banks, churches, and almost every kind of business.

Work setting: Usually an office desk with a computer and phone. Many jobs are weekday, daytime hours. Some offices need part-time or early coverage.

Compare an offer: Ask about base pay, hours, benefits, paid time off, training, and whether the job is full time, part time, or temporary.

7Where This Can Lead

  • Experienced assistant: Handle more complicated scheduling, records, and projects.
  • Office manager: Lead the front office and other support staff as experience grows.
  • Specialized assistant: Medical, legal, or executive assistant roles may need extra training and often pay more.
  • A related direction: Bookkeeping, payroll, human resources, or customer service.

You can enjoy this work for a whole career without becoming a manager. Ask people in the office which paths are actually open.

8The Real Talk

Things you might like:

  • Helping people every day.
  • Indoor work with a regular schedule in many offices.
  • Jobs in almost every town and every kind of business.
  • Learning skills you can take to many other jobs.

Things to ask about:

  • Interruptions while you are trying to finish something.
  • Callers or visitors who are upset or in a hurry.
  • Long stretches of sitting and computer work.
  • Busy times with deadlines, such as the end of the month or a big meeting.

Across the country, the number of these jobs is projected to shrink. In North Dakota, the projection is close to flat with about 720 openings a year, mostly from people retiring or changing jobs.

A question for yourself: Do you like keeping things organized while helping the people around you?

For each activity, choose whether it sounds comfortable to try. You do not need these skills already, and you can ask for support.

1. Keeping track of several small tasks at once
2. Using email, a calendar, and word processing programs
3. Answering the phone and taking a clear message
4. Checking details like names, dates, and spelling
5. Staying polite when someone is upset or in a hurry
6. Keeping private information private
7. Working at a desk and computer for much of the day
8. Asking questions when you are not sure how something is done
