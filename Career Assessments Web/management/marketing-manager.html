Marketing Manager Career Assessment

Campaigns, budgets, and proving the numbers moved

Section 1 of 11

1Welcome

A marketing manager decides how a company tells people what it sells, who it tells, where, and for how much money. Campaigns, websites, email, social media, ads, trade shows, and the reports that say whether any of it worked.

This job pays well in North Dakota and there are not many of them. This page tells you what the work is really like, what it pays here, and how people get in — which is almost always by doing the marketing work first.

This takes about 15 minutes.

2What is a Marketing Manager?

A marketing manager owns a budget and a set of goals and has to connect the two. You figure out who the customer is and what they actually care about. You plan campaigns and decide what runs where — search ads, social media, email, radio, print, trade shows, the website. You manage the people who write the copy and build the graphics, or you manage the outside agency that does. You watch the numbers every week: how many people came, what it cost to get them, how many became customers. Then you sit in front of leadership and say what worked, what did not, and what you want to do next quarter. In a small ND company you are also the writer, the photographer, and the person who runs the booth at the trade show.

The Reality: Read this part twice. Marketing managers get promoted from marketing jobs. The normal road is marketing coordinator or specialist, then senior specialist, then manager, which takes about five to eight years. What gets you promoted is not creativity by itself — it is being able to show, with numbers, that what you did made money. A marketing degree helps and is common, but a portfolio of campaigns with real results attached beats a degree with nothing behind it. The second honest thing: in a small North Dakota company you will be the entire marketing department. The title says manager, and for a while the only person you manage is yourself, plus an agency and a printer.

Your Responsibilities:

  • Plan campaigns and decide what runs on which channel
  • Own the marketing budget and defend how it was spent
  • Research customers and competitors and decide who to aim at
  • Manage writers, designers, and outside agencies
  • Track results — traffic, leads, cost per lead, sales — and report them
  • Keep the website, email lists, and social media accounts current
  • Work with the sales team on what they need to close deals
  • Plan trade shows, sponsorships, and community events

You get the right message to the right people, and you prove it worked.

3A Day in the Life

8:00 AM — Open the dashboard. Last week’s campaign brought traffic but almost no leads. Find out why.

8:45 AM — Stand-up with your specialist and your designer. Move a deadline, kill a piece that is not working.

10:00 AM — Review ad copy and photos for the spring campaign. Send two back with notes.

11:00 AM — Meeting with sales. They want a one-page piece for a customer type you had not planned for.

12:00 PM — Lunch while you read a competitor’s new website.

1:00 PM — Call with the agency about search ad spending. Shift budget from the ads that are not converting.

2:30 PM — Build next quarter’s plan and budget. Every line has to have a number attached to it.

4:00 PM — Present last month’s results to leadership. Two things worked, one did not. Say so plainly.

Creative work with a spreadsheet attached to every piece of it.

4Training in North Dakota

Do the marketing work first. The manager title comes after you can show results. There is no license and no required certificate. A bachelor’s in marketing, business, or communication is the common path and most ND employers expect one — but what they actually buy is a portfolio: campaigns you ran, what they cost, and what they produced. That is why people get here by working, not by studying. The good news is that this is one of the few management jobs where you can build real proof on your own with a small budget and a few months.

Training Pathways:

  • Coordinator to specialist to manager (5 to 8 years): Start as a marketing coordinator, social media specialist, or digital marketing specialist. Learn to measure everything you do. Move up when you can show the numbers moved because of your work.
  • Bachelor’s degree while you work (4 years, often online): Marketing, business administration, management, or strategic communication. NDSU offers Marketing fully online and UND offers Marketing in its business college. This is the credential most ND job ads ask for.
  • Sideways in from a related job (2 to 5 years): Graphic designers, writers, salespeople, and event coordinators move into marketing all the time. Add the measurement side — analytics, email platforms, ad accounts — and you become a candidate.

Where to Train in North Dakota:

  • North Dakota State University (Fargo) — Marketing B.S., available 100% online, one of eight majors in the College of Business, along with Business Administration and Management which are also fully online (ndsu.edu/business/academic-programs/undergraduate)
  • University of North Dakota (Grand Forks) — Marketing major in the Nistler College of Business & Public Administration, along with Entrepreneurship, Business Analytics, and Management, plus a Marketing minor and an AACSB-accredited MBA (business.und.edu/academics)
  • North Dakota State University (Fargo) — Strategic Communication major, a B.A. or B.S. of at least 120 credits, with required coursework in Principles of Strategic Communication, Research for Strategic Communication, and an advertising or public relations capstone, plus a 3-credit field experience (catalog.ndsu.edu/curriculum/undergraduate/strategic-communication-major)
  • ND State College of Science (Wahpeton and Fargo) — Business Management AAS with a Marketing, Sales and Hospitality Services option, a two-year degree that costs far less than a university and transfers (catalog.ndscs.edu/business-management)
  • Bismarck State College (Bismarck) — Management AAS and Management certificate, online or on campus, stacking into the Bachelor of Applied Science in Organizational Management and Leadership (catalog.bismarckstate.edu/catalog/degrees/career-technical-education/management)
  • Minot State University (Minot) — Management B.S. and a Master of Science in Management (minotstateu.edu/business)
  • University of Mary (Bismarck) — B.S. in Business Administration, an online B.S. in Business Management, and an online MBA (umary.edu/academics/schools/gary-tharaldson-school-business)
  • Valley City State University (Valley City) and Dickinson State University (Dickinson) — business administration degrees, both available online (catalog.vcsu.edu/undergraduate-catalog/departments/business and dickinsonstate.edu/academics/fields-of-study/sobe)

Before You Start: Clear writing. Comfort with numbers, because every campaign gets measured. Basic skill with a website platform, an email tool, and an ad account — these can be learned free in a few months. A portfolio matters more here than in any other job in this series.

Tip: Start with financial aid, scholarships, and any help an employer offers — that is where the money starts. After that, ask a career counselor or Job Service North Dakota whether WIOA or other programs can help with what is left over, like training, tools, work boots, or exam fees. What can be paid for depends on the program and on your own situation, so ask before you sign up rather than after. Registered apprenticeships let you earn a paycheck while you learn — see jobsnd.com/job-seeker/apprenticeships for ND programs.

5Certification & Licensing

No license and no required certificate. Your campaign results are the credential. Nothing licenses a marketing manager, and no certificate will get you the job by itself. What helps is a bachelor’s degree, proof you can run the tools, and a portfolio that shows what your work produced. Platform certificates are free or cheap and are worth having on a resume mainly because they show you can operate the ad and analytics systems you will be responsible for.

What Helps in Marketing Management:

  • A bachelor’s in marketing, business, or strategic communication — what most ND job ads ask for, and available online from NDSU and others
  • A portfolio with numbers — three to five campaigns with what they cost and what they produced. This is the single most persuasive thing you can bring to an interview
  • Platform certificates — the free training and certificates offered by the major search, social, and email advertising platforms. They are not prestigious, they are inexpensive, and they prove you can run the accounts
  • Project Management Professional (PMP) — PMI’s credential, useful if you run large campaigns across many teams. With a bachelor’s it takes 3 years of project management experience plus 35 hours of training, and renews with 60 professional development units every 3 years (pmi.org)
  • An MBA — worth considering later if you want to move into a director or officer role at a larger company. Not needed to become a marketing manager

The Exams: There is no exam for this job. Platform certificates have short online tests. The PMP exam is 180 questions in 4 hours through PMI.

Cost: The platform certificates are generally free. A bachelor’s is the real cost, and a two-year ND college is the inexpensive door into it. PMI does not publish the PMP exam price on the pages linked from its certification page, so ask PMI. Many employers pay for tuition and for marketing conferences — ask before you enroll.

Keeping It Current: Nothing to renew. Platform certificates expire and get retaken, usually free. What keeps you employable is a current portfolio, because this field changes fast and five-year-old campaign results do not persuade anyone.

6Salary & Job Outlook

North Dakota Marketing Manager Pay (May 2025):

  • Starting out (bottom 10–25%): $37.66–$49.99/hour ($78,340–$103,980/year)
  • Middle (median — half earn more, half earn less): $60.38/hour ($125,580/year)
  • Experienced (top 25–10%): $75.88–$95.36/hour ($157,830–$198,340/year)

What that means: The middle marketing manager in North Dakota earns about $125,580 a year, against a national middle of about $166,790. That is a large gap — about $41,000 — and it is the biggest percentage gap of any job in this series. Marketing pay is concentrated in big cities and in industries North Dakota does not have much of. The ND bottom tenth is about $78,340 and the top tenth is about $198,340, which is still a very good living here. Two things to notice: the jump from the bottom tenth to the 25th percentile is about $25,600, which is larger than in most jobs, so the first promotion past coordinator pays off quickly. And this is one of the few jobs in this series where working remotely for an out-of-state employer is realistic, which is the honest way around the ND pay gap.

Jobs in North Dakota: About 260 people do this work in ND today. By 2034, that should be about 280 (about 7% growth). Expect about 20 job openings every year in ND from growth and people retiring or changing jobs. Across the U.S., the change is about 7% growth.

Outlook: Decent growth, very few seats. O*NET shows North Dakota going from about 260 marketing managers in 2024 to about 280 in 2034 — about 7% growth, with roughly 20 openings a year statewide. Nationally the job also grows about 7%, so North Dakota is keeping pace with the country. It is simply a small pool: 20 openings a year, most of them in Fargo, with Bismarck, Grand Forks, and Minot accounting for most of the rest. Compare that with about 40 a year for sales managers and about 100 for medical and health services managers. Marketing is also the job on this list where remote work is most realistic, so your actual market is bigger than 20 if you are willing to work for a company in Minneapolis or Denver from a desk in North Dakota — and their pay scales are higher.

Who Hires in ND: Banks and credit unions, health systems, insurance companies, ag and equipment manufacturers, food and specialty product companies, tourism and hospitality, colleges and universities, technology companies in Fargo, retailers and co-ops, marketing and advertising agencies, and non-profits with a development office.

Work Setting: An office or a home office, and a screen most of the day. Usually 40 to 50 hours a week, with hard weeks around a campaign launch or a big trade show. Travel is occasional — trade shows, photo and video shoots, conferences. This is the most remote-friendly job in this series by a wide margin.

Physical Demands: Low. Sitting, screens, and meetings. Trade shows and event days mean standing for eight hours and hauling boxes and booth pieces, and photo or video shoots can mean a long day on your feet outdoors. What wears on people here is deadline pressure and the fact that your results are visible to everyone in the company.

Sources: U.S. Bureau of Labor Statistics, Occupational Employment and Wage Statistics, North Dakota, May 2025 (SOC 11-2021); North Dakota and U.S. long-term employment projections 2024–2034, via O*NET OnLine. Numbers are rounded.

7Where This Leads

Path 1: Marketing Coordinator — You schedule, post, proofread, and help with everything. This is the doorway.

Path 2: Marketing or Digital Specialist — You own a channel — email, social media, search ads, or the website.

Path 3: Senior Specialist or Campaign Lead — You run whole campaigns and the budget that goes with them.

Path 4: Marketing Manager — You own the plan, the budget, and the people who carry it out.

Path 5: Marketing Director — Several managers, several product lines, and a seat where the strategy is set.

Path 6: Chief Marketing Officer or Agency Owner — You run marketing for the company, or you run the agency.

8The Real Talk

What's Great:

  • Strong ND pay, with a middle of about $125,580
  • The most remote-friendly job in this series, which lets you reach higher-paying markets
  • You can build real proof on your own with a small budget and a few months
  • Creative work that still gets measured, so good work is provable
  • Every industry needs it, so you are not locked to one kind of employer
  • The step up from coordinator to specialist pays off fast

What's Hard:

  • About 20 openings a year statewide
  • ND pay runs about $41,000 under the national middle — the widest gap in this series
  • In a small ND company you are the entire marketing department
  • Everything you do is measured, and the failures are visible to everyone
  • The tools and platforms change constantly and you have to keep up on your own time
  • Budgets get cut first when business is slow, and marketing jobs go with them

Be Honest: Can you accept that every campaign gets graded with a number? Can you defend a budget to someone who thinks marketing is an expense rather than an investment? Can you kill an idea you love because the data say it is not working? And are you comfortable being the whole department for a while, doing the writing and the posting yourself?
