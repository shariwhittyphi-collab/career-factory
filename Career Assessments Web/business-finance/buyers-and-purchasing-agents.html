Buyer and Purchasing Agent Career Assessment

Buying the parts, supplies, and services a business runs on

Section 1 of 11

1Welcome

Buyers and purchasing agents decide what a business buys, who it buys from, and what it pays. They find suppliers, compare prices, write orders, and make sure things arrive on time.

North Dakota publishes this job under the title "Purchasing Agents, Except Wholesale, Retail, and Farm Products." You will explore what the work is really like and whether it fits you.

This takes about 15 minutes.

2What is a Buyer and Purchasing Agent?

A buyer figures out what the company needs, asks several suppliers for prices, and picks the best deal. Best does not always mean cheapest. It means the right quality, at the right time, for a fair price. Buyers write purchase orders, which are the official paperwork that says what you are buying and what you agreed to pay. Many buyers also handle contracts, which are written agreements that spell out the rules both sides must follow.

The Reality: Almost nobody is hired straight into a buyer title. The normal door in is a job next to the buying: purchasing clerk, inventory clerk, parts counter, shipping and receiving, or storeroom. You learn the parts, the suppliers, and the software, and then you move up. If you already know a product line — truck parts, farm equipment, building materials, medical supplies — that knowledge is worth a lot here, and it can beat a degree that has no product knowledge behind it. City, county, and state buying jobs are different: they follow strict bid rules, and they usually ask for a degree or long experience.

Your Responsibilities:

  • Figure out what the company needs and when it needs it
  • Ask several suppliers for prices and delivery dates
  • Compare quotes on price, quality, and shipping time
  • Write purchase orders and track them
  • Negotiate prices and payment terms with suppliers
  • Check that what arrived matches what was ordered
  • Fix problems when a shipment is late, short, or wrong
  • Keep supplier records and contract files organized

You make sure the crew has what it needs before anyone notices it is missing.

3A Day in the Life

7:30 AM — Open your email. A supplier says a part is back-ordered three weeks.

8:00 AM — Call two other suppliers to see who can ship it sooner.

9:00 AM — Enter six purchase orders in the system for next month.

10:30 AM — Meet with the shop foreman about what he will need for a big job.

12:00 PM — Lunch.

12:45 PM — Review three quotes for a new forklift. Build a simple comparison sheet.

2:00 PM — Receiving calls: a pallet arrived damaged. Start the claim.

4:00 PM — Update the price list and file the signed supplier agreement.

Desk work with a phone glued to your ear. Deadlines move and you move with them.

4Training in North Dakota

Many buyers learn on the job. Larger employers and government offices ask for a degree. There is no license for this job. Small and mid-size North Dakota employers often promote a clerk or a parts person who already knows the products. Bigger companies, hospitals, and public agencies usually want a two-year or four-year business degree. A degree plus warehouse or parts experience is the strongest combination.

Training Pathways:

  • Start in a related job (right away): Take a purchasing clerk, parts counter, inventory, or shipping and receiving job. Learn the product line and the ordering software. Ask to help with quotes. This is how most North Dakota buyers actually got there.
  • Two-year degree (2 years): A business management or business administration associate degree, often finished while you keep working. Your credits transfer toward a bachelor's later.
  • Four-year degree (4 years): A supply chain management or business degree. This is what larger employers and public agencies ask for, and it is the faster route to a purchasing manager title.

Where to Train in North Dakota:

  • North Dakota State University (Fargo) — Supply Chain Management Major, B.S., minimum 120 credits. Required courses include Managing Inventory and Materials, Procurement & Sourcing, and Transportation & Distribution — catalog.ndsu.edu
  • University of North Dakota (Grand Forks) — Operations & Supply Chain Management, B.B.A., 120 credits, 4 years, on campus or online. UND lists buyer, purchasing manager, inventory control analyst, and material resource planner among the jobs it prepares you for, and the program is AACSB accredited — und.edu
  • Bismarck State College (Bismarck) — Management, AAS, 60 to 61 credits, 2 years. Required courses include Principles of Management, Organizational Behavior, Project Management, and a Cooperative Education internship — catalog.bismarckstate.edu
  • North Dakota State College of Science (Wahpeton and Fargo) — Business Management, AAS (Administration and Finance), 61 credits. Delivered face-to-face at Wahpeton or Fargo, with some classes online. Required courses include Principles of Management, Business in the Legal Environment, and Financial Statement Analysis — catalog.ndscs.edu
  • Minot State University (Minot) — Management, B.S., 120 credits, on campus or online. Supply Chain and Operations Management (BUS 420) is required of every Management major — minotstateu.edu and catalog.minotstateu.edu

Before You Start: High school diploma or GED. You need to be comfortable with everyday math, percentages, and spreadsheets. You need to be organized and willing to pick up the phone and push a supplier. No advanced math.

Tip: Start with financial aid, scholarships, and any help an employer offers — that is where the money starts. After that, ask a career counselor or Job Service North Dakota whether WIOA or other programs can help with what is left over, like training, tools, work boots, or exam fees. What can be paid for depends on the program and on your own situation, so ask before you sign up rather than after. Registered apprenticeships let you earn a paycheck while you learn — see jobsnd.com/job-seeker/apprenticeships for ND programs.

5Certification & Licensing

No license is required. Certificates are optional and mostly reward experience you already have. The main groups here are ASCM (the Association for Supply Chain Management, formerly APICS), ISM (the Institute for Supply Management), and the UPPCC (the Universal Public Procurement Certification Council) for government buying.

Credentials You Might Get:

  • CPIM (ASCM) — Certified in Planning and Inventory Management. Two exams covering demand management, supply planning, inventory management, scheduling, and distribution. You must pass both parts within three years of each other, and the certification is maintained every five years. ASCM's page does not list any degree or experience requirement to sit for it (ascm.org)
  • CSCP (ASCM) — Certified Supply Chain Professional. One exam, 150 questions, 3.5 hours, scored 200 to 350 with 300 to pass. ASCM suggests about 100 hours of study. Ask ASCM directly about eligibility, because the page does not spell it out (ascm.org)
  • CPSM (ISM) — Certified Professional in Supply Management. You need 3 years of professional supply management experience with a bachelor's degree, or 5 years without a degree. Three exams: Supply Management Core (180 questions, 3 hours), Supply Management Integration (165 questions, 2 hours 45 minutes), and Leadership and Transformation (165 questions, 2 hours 45 minutes). Renewed every three years with 60 continuing education hours (ismworld.org)
  • APSM (ISM) — the shorter entry-level option. It is one exam, the same Supply Management Core, and the score stays valid for 5 years (ismworld.org)
  • CPPB (UPPCC) — Certified Professional Public Buyer, for city, county, state, and tribal buying jobs. You need a 2-year degree, diploma, or certificate plus 3 years of procurement experience in the last 10 years, or 5 years of experience with no degree. Either way, at least half the experience must be public sector, and you need 72 contact hours of procurement training. The exam is 180 multiple-choice questions in 3.5 hours, given in two windows a year (uppcc.org)

The Exams: All of these are computer-based multiple-choice exams. None of them require a college degree if you have enough years of work, and the ASCM exams list no experience bar at all. The public-sector CPPB is the one that checks your years most strictly.

Cost: UPPCC lists the CPPB at a $390 application fee ($290 for NIGP or partner members) plus a $325 exam fee. ASCM sells its exams bundled with study material, so the CPIM and CSCP packages run well over a thousand dollars. Ask a career counselor whether exam or study fees can be covered.

Keeping It Current: CPIM is maintained every five years. CPSM renews every three years with 60 continuing education hours. Check each group's site for current rules before you pay.

6Salary & Job Outlook

North Dakota Buyer and Purchasing Agent Pay (May 2025):

  • Starting out (bottom 10–25%): $25.31–$29.34/hour ($52,630–$61,030/year)
  • Middle (median — half earn more, half earn less): $36.73/hour ($76,400/year)
  • Experienced (top 25–10%): $42.60–$51.00/hour ($88,610–$106,070/year)

What that means: The middle North Dakota buyer earns about $36.73 an hour, or about $76,400 a year. That is right at the national middle of $37.36, which is unusual for an office job in this state. But read the two ends honestly. The bottom tenth, near $25.31 an hour, is where you start, and a purchasing clerk or parts job you take to get in will start below that. The top tenth here is about $51.00 an hour, while the national top tenth is about $61.96 — North Dakota has fewer of the very large corporate buying jobs that pay at the top. This is a salaried office job, so do not count on overtime pay.

Jobs in North Dakota: About 890 people do this work in ND today. By 2034, that should be about 980 (about 11% growth). Expect about 100 job openings every year in ND from growth and people retiring or changing jobs. Across the U.S., the change is about 6% growth — faster than average.

Outlook: Good. North Dakota expects about 100 openings a year, and the state is growing this job faster than the country is. Most openings come from people retiring or moving up, not from brand-new positions.

Who Hires in ND: Hospitals and clinics, farm and grain co-ops, equipment dealers, oil and gas companies, manufacturers, construction firms, trucking companies, school districts, tribal governments, city and county offices, and North Dakota state government.

Work Setting: An office, usually inside the company you buy for. Mostly indoors at a desk with a phone and two screens. Some buyers walk out to the warehouse or shop several times a day. A few travel to see suppliers or attend trade shows.

Physical Demands: Low. Sitting, typing, and talking on the phone. The strain lands on your eyes, back, and wrists. A good chair, a sit-stand desk, a headset, and screen magnification or reading software are common requests employers say yes to.

Sources: U.S. Bureau of Labor Statistics, Occupational Employment and Wage Statistics, North Dakota, May 2025 (SOC 13-1023); North Dakota and U.S. long-term employment projections 2024–2034, via O*NET OnLine. Numbers are rounded.

7Where This Leads

Path 1: Purchasing or Inventory Clerk — The usual first job. Orders and receiving paperwork.

Path 2: Buyer — You own a product area and pick the suppliers.

Path 3: Senior or Lead Buyer — Bigger contracts and more money on the line.

Path 4: Contract Specialist — Focus on writing and managing agreements.

Path 5: Purchasing Manager — Run the department and supervise buyers.

Path 6: Supply Chain Manager — Oversee buying, inventory, and shipping together.

8The Real Talk

What's Great:

  • You can enter from a warehouse or parts job
  • Product knowledge beats a degree here
  • Steady year-round indoor work
  • Skills transfer to any industry
  • Clear ladder to manager pay
  • Pay sits at the national middle

What's Hard:

  • You rarely start with the buyer title
  • Late shipments become your problem
  • Suppliers will push back on you
  • Public buying has strict bid rules
  • Deadlines and shortages cause real stress
  • Sitting at a desk most of the day

Be Honest: Can you stay polite but firm when a supplier is giving you the runaround? Can you keep track of dozens of open orders without losing one? Are you willing to start in a clerk or parts job and work up?
