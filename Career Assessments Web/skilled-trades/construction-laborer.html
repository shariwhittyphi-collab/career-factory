Construction Laborer Career Assessment

Doing the hands-on work that gets every building project done

Section 1 of 11

1Welcome

Before a building goes up, someone digs, carries, levels, and cleans. That crew member is often a construction laborer.

This is physical, outdoor work on a team. Explore what it feels like before deciding whether it interests you.

Go at your pace. The short questions at the end are for reflection. There are no right or wrong answers.

2What is a Construction Laborer?

You do the hands-on work that keeps a construction project moving. You help skilled workers, run small machines, and keep the site safe and clean.

  • Load and unload materials and carry them where they are needed.
  • Dig, shovel, and level ground and gravel.
  • Help pour and finish concrete.
  • Run tools like compactors, saws, and jackhammers.
  • Signal equipment operators and direct traffic around the site.
  • Clean up to remove hazards.

The real picture: The work changes with each job. Roads, houses, farms, and oil field sites all need laborers, and in North Dakota much of the work is busiest from spring through fall.

3A Day in the Life

One possible day: Every crew and job site is different.

  • Early morning: Meet the crew, put on safety gear, and hear the plan for the day.
  • Morning: Unload a delivery of lumber and rebar and stack it where it goes.
  • Midday: Help set forms and spread gravel before a concrete pour.
  • Afternoon: Help finish the concrete and run a compactor.
  • Before leaving: Clean tools and pick up the site so it is safe overnight.

Weather changes plans. Some days start before sunrise to beat the heat, and some jobs pause when it storms.

4How You Learn the Job

Most people learn on the job. Many employers hire people with a high school diploma or GED, and some do not require one. Read the actual job ad.

  • On-the-job training: An experienced worker shows you the tools and safety rules.
  • Apprenticeship: Some contractors and the Laborers’ union offer construction craft laborer apprenticeships, where you are paid while you learn.
  • Safety training: Many employers ask for or provide OSHA 10-hour safety training.
  • Optional classes: North Dakota schools offering construction trades programs include Turtle Mountain College in Belcourt and Sitting Bull College in Fort Yates. Compare cost, length, and schedule.

Paying for training: In an apprenticeship you are paid while you learn, and pay usually rises as you complete hours. For classes or a certificate, start with financial aid, scholarships, and grants. Ask employers whether they help pay. Ask your career counselor about other help, such as WIOA, a federal program that can help some people pay for job training. Ask before you enroll.

5Training and Employer Requirements

North Dakota does not license construction laborers. Building contractors need a state contractor license, but that is the employer’s job, not yours. Ask each employer what you need to start.

  • Ability to lift and carry heavy materials, or ask about the job’s physical demands.
  • Steel-toed boots and some basic gear; ask what the employer provides.
  • A driver’s license and reliable ride to job sites that move.
  • Safety training, which many employers provide.
  • Drug testing for some employers.

Before you begin: Ask who trains new workers and what safety gear you will be given.

Ask about written instructions, a buddy on your first jobs, extra practice time, or other support that helps you learn and work safely.

6Pay and Job Outlook

North Dakota construction laborer pay — May 2025:

  • Middle pay: $23.59 an hour ($49,070 a year). Half of workers earned more and half earned less.
  • Half of workers earned between $21.96 and $29.17 an hour.
  • 1 in 10 earned less than $18.75 an hour, and 1 in 10 earned more than $34.23.
  • These numbers show what workers earned. They are not a promise of what a new worker will start at.
  • North Dakota had about 5,150 of these jobs in 2024. The state projects about 5,650 in 2034, a change of +10%. That works out to about 480 openings a year, mostly from people who retire or change jobs.

Where people work: Building contractors, road and bridge crews, oil field and energy projects, farms, and cities and counties.

Work setting: Outdoor job sites in all kinds of weather. Many jobs are full time in busy season, with early starts and some long days.

Compare an offer: Ask about base pay, overtime, winter work, travel pay, benefits, and what safety gear is provided.

7Where This Can Lead

  • Experienced laborer: Run more equipment and take on harder tasks.
  • A skilled trade: Many laborers move into carpentry, concrete finishing, pipe laying, or equipment operation.
  • Crew leader or foreman: Lead a crew as experience grows.
  • Construction manager: With experience and sometimes more school, plan and run whole projects.

Ask people on the crew which paths are open where you live.

8The Real Talk

Things you might like:

  • Seeing a project rise because of your work.
  • Working outside with a crew.
  • Starting without a degree and learning on the job.
  • Many paths into skilled trades.

Things to ask about:

  • Hard physical work that can be tough on your body over time.
  • Weather, including North Dakota winters.
  • Seasonal layoffs or long days in busy season.
  • Travel to job sites far from home.

North Dakota projects about 10% growth in these jobs from 2024 to 2034, with about 480 openings a year.

A question for yourself: Does physical work on a team, outside, sound like a good fit for you?

10Part 2: Things You Could Try

For each activity, choose whether it sounds comfortable to try. You do not need these skills already, and you can ask for support.

1. Lifting, carrying, and shoveling for much of a shift
2. Working outside in heat, cold, and wind
3. Following directions from a crew leader
4. Wearing a hard hat, safety glasses, and other gear all day
5. Learning to use tools like saws, compactors, and jackhammers
6. Getting up early and working long days in busy season
7. Measuring and marking where work goes
8. Speaking up when something looks unsafe
