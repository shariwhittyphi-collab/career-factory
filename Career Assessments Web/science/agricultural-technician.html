Agricultural Technician Career Assessment

Field plots, soil samples, and a pickup truck — the two-year door into ag science

Section 1 of 11

1Welcome

Agricultural technicians run the hands-on side of crop and livestock science. They plant research plots, pull soil and plant samples, scout fields for bugs and weeds, run tests, and write down numbers that somebody else will turn into a recommendation.

Pay attention to this one. Of the seven careers in this group, it is the only one you can reach with a two-year degree or less, and North Dakota is an agriculture state. That combination does not come along often.

This takes about 15 minutes.

2What is a Agricultural Technician?

An agricultural technician is the person who actually does the work in the field and in the lab. You lay out research plots and mark them. You run a tractor, a sprayer, a plot combine, or a seed drill. You pull soil cores and plant tissue samples and label every one so nobody loses track of where it came from. You count insects, weeds, and diseased plants. You run samples through lab equipment. You record every number carefully, because a whole season of work can be ruined by one row of bad data. Some technicians work on the animal side instead, collecting samples and keeping records on livestock. O*NET lists sample job titles like Agricultural Research Technician, Agronomy Research Technician, Field Assistant, Seed Analyst, and Lab Tech.

The Reality: This is a real job for people who do not want six more years of school, and that is worth being clear about. O*NET's survey of people doing this work reports 41% saying a high school diploma is what the job required, 13% saying an associate degree, and 18% saying a bachelor's degree. O*NET puts it in Job Zone Three, medium preparation. Now the honest part. North Dakota pay sits a little under the national middle: the ND middle is $46,960 a year while the national middle is $49,630. Minnesota pays a good deal better, with a middle of $57,310. And it is seasonal work in a state with a short season. Planting and harvest will eat your May and your September. Winter can be slow, or it can be lab and data work indoors, depending on the employer.

Your Responsibilities:

  • Lay out, plant, and mark research plots
  • Run tractors, sprayers, plot combines, and other field equipment
  • Collect soil, plant tissue, seed, water, and animal samples
  • Scout fields for insects, weeds, and plant diseases
  • Set up lab and field equipment and keep it calibrated
  • Run tests on samples and record the results
  • Keep careful written records of every plot, sample, and date
  • Help scientists and agronomists prepare reports and summaries

You are the person who makes sure the numbers are real.

3A Day in the Life

6:30 AM — Load the truck. Sample bags, cooler, flags, GPS unit, clipboard.

7:00 AM — Drive 40 minutes out to the research plots. Coffee on the dash.

7:45 AM — Walk the rows. Count aphids in ten spots and write down each count.

9:30 AM — Pull soil cores from the treated and untreated strips. Label every bag.

11:30 AM — Lunch in the truck by the field approach.

12:30 PM — Second farm. Check the sprayer calibration before an application.

3:00 PM — Back to the lab. Log samples into the system and start a moisture test.

4:30 PM — Enter the morning counts into the spreadsheet. Fix two that do not add up.

Early starts, dusty boots, and a lot of writing things down.

4Training in North Dakota

A two-year degree is the sweet spot here, and North Dakota has several. This is the one career in this group where you do not need a university degree to get in the door. Many technicians start with a high school diploma and farm experience. A two-year degree moves you up faster and opens the research and precision-ag jobs, and North Dakota's two-year colleges are built for exactly this. If you later want to become an agronomist or a soil scientist, these credits transfer toward a four-year degree, so nothing is wasted.

Training Pathways:

  • Two-year degree (about 2 years): An AAS in precision agriculture or agronomy. NDSCS in Wahpeton lists both a Precision Agronomy AAS and a Precision Agriculture Technician AAS. Lake Region State College in Devils Lake lists a Precision Agriculture AAS of 67 credits. Williston State College lists an Agriculture AAS of at least 62 credits with seven options to choose from.
  • Start working, add a certificate (under 1 year): Get hired as a field or lab helper with a high school diploma and farm background, then stack a short credential on top. NDSCS lists an Agriculture Applicator and Operations certificate. The North Dakota pesticide applicator certification through NDSU Extension is the one most employers actually ask about.
  • Two years, then transfer (4 years total): Finish the AAS or an AS, then move to NDSU. NDSU's Agricultural Technology BS is 120 credits and is heavy on precision agriculture, drones, sensors, soils, crop science, and GIS. NDSU also lists an Agricultural Sciences major offered on campus and online.

Where to Train in North Dakota:

  • North Dakota State College of Science (Wahpeton) — Precision Agronomy, AAS, and Precision Agriculture Technician, AAS. NDSCS also lists an Agriculture Applicator and Operations certificate and Farm and Ranch Management, AAS — ndscs.edu/academics/programs
  • Lake Region State College (Devils Lake) — Precision Agriculture, AAS, 67 total credit hours with 20 credits of general education. LRSC says graduates work as precision technicians at implement dealerships, agronomy centers, and independent precision service providers — lrsc.edu/academics/programs/precision-agriculture
  • Williston State College (Williston) — Agriculture, AAS, minimum 62 credits, with seven options including Farm Management, Ranch Management, Natural Resource Management, and Unmanned Aerial Systems. WSC lists Agricultural Technician among its career outcomes — willistonstate.edu/programs/Agriculture
  • Bismarck State College (Bismarck) — Agriculture Industry and Technology (Agronomy), plus Agriculture Management, Farm and Ranch Management, and Ranch Operations — bismarckstate.edu/academics/programs
  • Dickinson State University (Dickinson) — Agricultural Sales and Service, AS, a two-year degree, and Agricultural Studies, BS, if you decide to keep going — dickinsonstate.edu/academics/fields-of-study/soas/agriculture
  • North Dakota State University (Fargo) — Agricultural Technology, BS, 120 credits, in the Department of Agricultural and Biosystems Engineering. NDSU says the program uses drones, sensors, robotics, and big data, and lists Agricultural Technician and Precision Ag Specialist among early-career jobs — catalog.ndsu.edu and ndsu.edu/programs/undergraduate/agricultural-technology
  • United Tribes Technical College (Bismarck) — Sustainable Agriculture and Food Systems, and Environmental Science and Research — uttc.edu/academics

Before You Start: High school diploma or GED. Take biology, chemistry, and as much math as you can stand. A valid driver’s license matters more here than in almost any other science job, because you will drive to fields every day, and being able to pull a trailer helps. Farm or ranch experience is a real advantage and employers count it.

Tip: Start with financial aid and scholarships. Fill out the FAFSA, ask the school’s financial aid office what you qualify for, and ask an employer whether they help pay for school. That is where the money starts. After that, a career counselor or Job Service North Dakota may be able to help with what is left over, like tuition, books, a laptop, or exam fees. What can be paid for depends on the program and on your own situation, so ask before you enroll rather than after. Worth asking about specifically: two-year degrees that train you for a job, like civil engineering technology, land surveying, mechatronics, electronics, or instrumentation. North Dakota two-year colleges also cost far less than a university. Ask about paid summer internships too — engineering firms, plants, and survey crews hire students while they are still in school.

5Certification & Licensing

No license is required, but two certifications open doors. Nobody has to be licensed to work as an agricultural technician in North Dakota. But two credentials show up constantly in job ads, and one of them is nearly standard.

Certifications Worth Having:

  • North Dakota pesticide applicator certification — NDSU Extension runs certification and training for North Dakota, and the ND Department of Agriculture points all questions to them. There are two sides: a private applicator works land they own or rent, and a commercial or public applicator applies pesticides for hire or for a government agency (ndsu.edu ag-hub pesticide certification program)
  • How you get it — you pass a monitored exam given by NDSU Extension staff. Training sessions are offered statewide in fall, winter, and spring and are strongly recommended but not required. County extension offices have the study materials (ndsu.edu)
  • Commercial categories — North Dakota has 11 commercial categories, including agricultural pest control, ornamental and turf, greenhouse, right-of-way, public health, and wood preservatives. Commercial applicators pass a core exam plus a category exam (ndsu.edu)
  • Commercial financial responsibility — commercial applicators must show proof of financial responsibility of at least $100,000 (ndsu.edu)
  • Certified Crop Adviser (CCA) — the big one for people who want to advise growers. Run by the American Society of Agronomy. With a bachelor’s degree you need 2 years of documented crop advising experience; with a high school diploma you need 4 years after high school. Grower references are required (sciencesocieties.org)
  • CCA exams — two of them: an International CCA Exam covering crop management, nutrient management, soil and water management, and pest management, plus a Local Board Exam for your region. Exams run four times a year and you may now take them one section at a time, with up to five years to finish (sciencesocieties.org)

The Exams: The pesticide exam is monitored by NDSU Extension. The CCA International Exam is $280 for the full exam or a retest, or $105 for a single section. Local board exam prices are set by each local board and the price list I read did not show a North Dakota figure, so ask the North Dakota CCA board what theirs costs.

Cost: NDSU Extension does not publish the pesticide certification fee on its overview page, so call your county extension office and ask before you sign up. For the CCA, plan on the $280 international exam plus the local board exam, plus study materials.

Keeping It Current: North Dakota pesticide certification expires April 1 every three years. You renew by attending recertification training or by testing again through your local extension office. The CCA requires 40 hours of continuing education every two years.

6Salary & Job Outlook

North Dakota Agricultural Technician Pay (May 2025):

  • Starting out (bottom 10–25%): $17.62–$21.19/hour ($36,660–$44,070/year)
  • Middle (median — half earn more, half earn less): $22.58/hour ($46,960/year)
  • Experienced (top 25–10%): $28.64–$30.90/hour ($59,580–$64,270/year)

What that means: These are real North Dakota numbers from O*NET, not national estimates. The ND middle is $22.58 an hour, about $46,960 a year. Read the whole range, because the shape of it matters. The ND 25th percentile of $44,070 is actually ABOVE the national 25th percentile of $40,950, so the bottom end here is better than the bottom end nationally. But the ND middle of $46,960 is under the national middle of $49,630, and the ND top end falls further behind: ND 90th is $64,270 while the national 90th is $76,480. Minnesota pays noticeably more, with a middle of $57,310 and a 75th percentile of $82,300. In plain terms: North Dakota is a decent place to start in this job and a harder place to get rich in it. Also expect seasonal overtime. Planting and harvest are long days, and that is where a lot of the money is.

Jobs in North Dakota: About 140 people do this work in ND today. By 2034, that should be about 160 (about 12% growth). Expect about 20 job openings every year in ND from growth and people retiring or changing jobs. Across the U.S., the change is about 4% growth — average.

Outlook: Good, and better here than nationally. North Dakota is projected to go from about 140 of these jobs in 2024 to about 160 by 2034, roughly 12% growth, against about 4% nationally. That works out to about 20 openings a year. Twenty is a small number in plain terms, but measure it against the size of the field: this occupation only has about 140 people in it statewide, so 20 openings a year means the door opens often relative to how many are inside. Seed companies, co-ops, and the NDSU research centers around the state are the places to watch.

Who Hires in ND: Seed and crop input companies, farmer-owned co-ops and agronomy centers, implement dealerships with precision ag departments, the NDSU Research Extension Centers scattered across the state, the USDA Agricultural Research Service, the ND Department of Agriculture, crop consulting firms, sugar and edible bean processors, and large farms and ranches.

Work Setting: Outdoors most of the growing season and indoors most of the winter. Fields, research plots, greenhouses, seed labs, and a truck. Expect dust, sun, wind, mud, mosquitoes in June, and cold in October. Long drives between sites are normal, especially in the western half of the state.

Physical Demands: Medium to high. Walking plot rows for hours, kneeling, bending, carrying sample bags and coolers, lifting seed and fertilizer bags, and climbing on and off equipment. You will be on your feet outdoors in heat and cold. Good eyesight for spotting insects and plant symptoms helps a lot.

Sources: U.S. Bureau of Labor Statistics, Occupational Employment and Wage Statistics, North Dakota, May 2025 (SOC 19-4012); North Dakota and U.S. long-term employment projections 2024–2034, via O*NET OnLine. Numbers are rounded.

7Where This Leads

Path 1: Field or Lab Assistant — Entry level. You collect, label, and log. High school diploma plus farm sense gets you here.

Path 2: Agricultural Technician — You run plots and equipment on your own and own your data.

Path 3: Precision Ag Technician — Guidance systems, yield monitors, drones, and variable-rate maps. The AAS programs aim here.

Path 4: Research Technician or Lead Tech — You run a research station’s field season and train the seasonal crew.

Path 5: Certified Crop Adviser (CCA) — You advise growers directly. Needs the exams plus 2 to 4 years of experience.

Path 6: Agronomist or Soil Scientist — Requires a four-year degree, and often a master’s. Your AAS credits transfer toward it.

8The Real Talk

What's Great:

  • Two-year degree or less gets you in
  • North Dakota is an agriculture state
  • ND growth about 12%, faster than the US
  • ND bottom end pays above the US bottom end
  • Outdoors work that is not a desk
  • Credits transfer if you want to go further

What's Hard:

  • ND middle pay is under the US middle
  • Seasonal — long springs and falls, slow winters
  • Only about 140 of these jobs statewide
  • Minnesota pays a good deal more
  • Weather, dust, and mosquitoes are part of it
  • Careless record-keeping ruins a whole season

Be Honest: Can you be outdoors all day in North Dakota weather? Can you write down a number correctly the four-hundredth time? Are you all right with a job that runs hard in May and September and quiet in January?
