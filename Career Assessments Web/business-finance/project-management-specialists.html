Project Management Specialist Career Assessment

Keeping a project on schedule, on budget, and moving

Section 1 of 11

1Welcome

Project management specialists plan and track projects. They build the schedule, watch the budget, chase down the pieces that are late, and keep everyone pointed at the same finish line.

This is the best-paying job in this group in North Dakota. You will explore what the work is really like and whether it fits you.

This takes about 15 minutes.

2What is a Project Management Specialist?

A project is a piece of work with a start, a finish, and a budget — building a clinic, installing new software, moving a warehouse. A project management specialist builds the plan, tracks progress against it, and reports where things stand. They do not usually supervise the workers. They own the schedule, the budget, and the communication. That is why they are sometimes called a project coordinator or project analyst early on.

The Reality: Almost nobody is hired into this from outside. People reach it from inside an industry they already know. A nurse becomes a clinic project coordinator. A welder becomes a construction project coordinator. An IT support person becomes a software project coordinator. The industry knowledge is what makes you useful, because you have to know when someone is telling you a schedule that cannot happen. The realistic plan is to get into an industry, take on coordinating work, and add the CAPM certificate — which is the one credential here that needs no experience at all, only a high school diploma and 23 hours of training. Also know this: you carry the blame for delays you did not cause, and you have to push people who do not report to you.

Your Responsibilities:

  • Build the project schedule and keep it current
  • Track the budget and flag it early when it slips
  • Run meetings and write down what was decided
  • Chase down the tasks that are behind
  • Report status to managers and the customer
  • Spot risks before they become problems
  • Keep all the project files and paperwork in one place
  • Close the project out and record what went wrong and right

You are the person who knows where everything stands, and says so honestly.

3A Day in the Life

8:00 AM — Update the schedule. One task slipped three days over the weekend.

8:30 AM — Run the 15-minute standup. Ask each lead what is blocking them.

9:30 AM — Email the supplier whose delivery is holding up the whole next step.

10:30 AM — Update the budget sheet. Two invoices came in over the estimate.

12:00 PM — Lunch.

12:45 PM — Write the weekly status report. Say plainly what is at risk.

2:00 PM — Meet with the customer. Explain the delay and the new date.

4:00 PM — Log the decisions, update the risk list, and set tomorrow's agenda.

Meetings, messages, and spreadsheets. You switch between people and numbers all day.

4Training in North Dakota

Most postings ask for a bachelor's degree, but the CAPM certificate needs no degree and no experience. There is no license for this job. What employers really want is proof you can run a schedule and a budget in their industry. Two things get people in: taking coordinating work inside a job you already have, and the CAPM from PMI, which PMI says requires only a high school diploma or GED, being at least 18, and 23 hours of project management education. A degree still helps for corporate and government jobs.

Training Pathways:

  • Coordinate from inside your current job (1 to 3 years): Volunteer to track a project, run the meeting, or keep the schedule where you already work. Add the CAPM. This is the most realistic path, and your industry knowledge is the advantage.
  • Two-year degree plus CAPM (2 years): A business management associate degree that includes a project management course, paired with the CAPM certificate. It gets you into coordinator jobs, and the credits transfer toward a bachelor's.
  • Four-year degree (about 2.5 to 4 years): A project management or business bachelor's degree. This is what most postings ask for and what leads to the PMP and manager pay later.

Where to Train in North Dakota:

  • University of Mary (Bismarck) — Bachelor of Science in Project Management, online, finishable in as little as 2.5 years depending on transfer credits. University of Mary states it is an accredited affiliate of PMI and that students can take the CAPM exam and be certified before finishing the degree — online.umary.edu
  • University of Mary (Bismarck) — Project Management Graduate Certificate, 9 credits, 3 courses, about 21 weeks, online, for people who already hold a bachelor's degree. University of Mary states it is an Authorized Training Partner for PMI. A Master of Project Management is also offered — online.umary.edu
  • Bismarck State College (Bismarck) — Management, AAS, 60 to 61 credits, 2 years. Project Management is a required course, alongside Principles of Management, Organizational Behavior, and a Cooperative Education internship — catalog.bismarckstate.edu
  • North Dakota State College of Science (Wahpeton and Fargo) — Business Management, AAS (Administration and Finance), 61 credits, face-to-face or with some classes online. A practical two-year base that transfers — catalog.ndscs.edu
  • North Dakota State University (Fargo) — Construction Management Major, B.S.Cons.M., minimum 123 credits, for people who want to run construction projects specifically — catalog.ndsu.edu
  • University of North Dakota (Grand Forks) — Operations & Supply Chain Management, B.B.A., 120 credits, on campus or online. UND lists project management among the skill areas it covers — und.edu

Before You Start: High school diploma or GED. You need to write clearly, run a meeting, and be comfortable with spreadsheets and calendars. You need enough nerve to tell a manager bad news early instead of late. No advanced math.

Tip: Start with financial aid, scholarships, and any help an employer offers — that is where the money starts. After that, ask a career counselor or Job Service North Dakota whether WIOA or other programs can help with what is left over, like training, tools, work boots, or exam fees. What can be paid for depends on the program and on your own situation, so ask before you sign up rather than after. Registered apprenticeships let you earn a paycheck while you learn — see jobsnd.com/job-seeker/apprenticeships for ND programs.

5Certification & Licensing

No license is required. The CAPM is the rare credential that needs no work experience at all. PMI, the Project Management Institute, issues the two credentials that matter most here: the CAPM for people starting out and the PMP for experienced project managers.

Credentials You Might Get:

  • CAPM (PMI) — Certified Associate in Project Management. PMI requires a secondary degree such as a high school diploma, GED, or global equivalent, being at least 18 years old, and at least 23 hours of project management education completed before the exam. No work experience is required. This is the genuine on-ramp (pmi.org)
  • CAPM exam — 150 questions (135 scored, 15 unscored) in 180 minutes, covering Project Management Fundamentals and Core Concepts, predictive plan-based methods, agile frameworks, and business analysis frameworks. Renewed with 15 professional development units every 3 years (pmi.org)
  • PMP (PMI) — Project Management Professional, the big one. With a high school diploma or GED you need 5 years of managing projects. With an associate degree, 4 years. With a bachelor's degree or higher, 3 years. With a PMI GAC accredited degree, 2 years. All of that experience must be within the last 10 years (pmi.org)
  • PMP training requirement — 35 hours of project professional training, which can come from a self-paced course, an instructor-led class from an approved provider, or simply holding an active CAPM. That last option is worth knowing: the CAPM covers the PMP training requirement for you (pmi.org)
  • PMP exam — 180 questions in 240 minutes, split People 33%, Process 41%, Business Environment 26%, with two 10-minute breaks. Taken at a testing center or online with a proctor, up to three attempts in one year. Renewed with 60 professional development units every 3 years (pmi.org)

The Exams: Both exams are computer-based multiple choice and can be taken at a testing center or online with a proctor. The CAPM is 3 hours; the PMP is 4 hours. The CAPM is the one you can sit for right now with a high school diploma and a prep course — it does not ask about your work history at all.

Cost: PMI does not publish the exam fees on these pages, so check pmi.org or call PMI for current member and nonmember prices before you plan. PMI membership usually lowers the exam fee. Prep courses are sold separately and vary widely. Ask a career counselor whether exam and course fees can be covered.

Keeping It Current: CAPM holders need 15 professional development units in each 3-year cycle. PMP holders need 60 in each 3-year cycle. One unit is one hour of learning, teaching, presenting, reading, volunteering, or creating content, so ordinary training at work counts.

6Salary & Job Outlook

North Dakota Project Management Specialist Pay (May 2025):

  • Starting out (bottom 10–25%): $29.95–$37.02/hour ($62,300–$77,010/year)
  • Middle (median — half earn more, half earn less): $46.23/hour ($96,160/year)
  • Experienced (top 25–10%): $57.52–$66.98/hour ($119,630–$139,320/year)

What that means: The middle North Dakota project management specialist earns about $46.23 an hour, or about $96,160 a year. That is the highest pay in this group by a wide margin. The national middle is about $49.19, so North Dakota runs a little below, and the gap grows at the top — $66.98 here against about $80.76 nationally. Read the bottom number carefully too: the lowest tenth is about $29.95 an hour, or roughly $62,300 a year, and that is where a project coordinator starts. The spread from bottom to top is very wide because the same job title covers a person tracking one small project and a person running a multi-million-dollar build. This is salaried work, so long weeks near a deadline do not come with overtime pay.

Jobs in North Dakota: About 1,770 people do this work in ND today. By 2034, that should be about 1,900 (about 8% growth). Expect about 140 job openings every year in ND from growth and people retiring or changing jobs. Across the U.S., the change is about 6% growth — faster than average.

Outlook: Good. North Dakota expects about 140 openings a year and is growing this job faster than the country is. There are already about 1,770 of these jobs statewide, which makes it the largest and best-paying job in this group.

Who Hires in ND: Construction and engineering firms, hospitals and health systems, banks and insurance companies, energy and pipeline companies, software and IT departments, manufacturers, colleges, tribal governments, city and county offices, and North Dakota state government.

Work Setting: An office, or a trailer on a job site, or your home. Mostly indoors at a desk with a calendar, a spreadsheet, and a lot of meetings. Hybrid and remote jobs exist here more than in most of these careers. Construction project coordinators spend real time on site.

Physical Demands: Low. Sitting, typing, and talking, with a lot of screen time and video calls. The strain is on your eyes, back, and wrists. A good chair, a sit-stand desk, a headset, and screen magnification or reading software are common requests employers say yes to.

Sources: U.S. Bureau of Labor Statistics, Occupational Employment and Wage Statistics, North Dakota, May 2025 (SOC 13-1082); North Dakota and U.S. long-term employment projections 2024–2034, via O*NET OnLine. Numbers are rounded.

7Where This Leads

Path 1: Project Coordinator or Assistant — The usual first title. You keep the schedule and the files.

Path 2: Project Management Specialist — You own a project end to end.

Path 3: Senior Project Manager — Bigger budgets and higher stakes.

Path 4: Program Manager — Run several related projects at once.

Path 5: Portfolio or PMO Manager — Decide which projects the organization takes on.

Path 6: Operations or Department Director — Run a whole function, not just projects.

8The Real Talk

What's Great:

  • Best pay in this group by a wide margin
  • The CAPM needs no degree and no experience
  • About 140 ND openings a year
  • Your existing industry knowledge is the advantage
  • More remote and hybrid jobs than most office work
  • Skills work in every industry

What's Hard:

  • Rarely an entry-level job from outside
  • You get blamed for delays you did not cause
  • You must push people who do not report to you
  • Meetings eat a lot of your day
  • Deadline weeks are long and unpaid extra
  • Most postings still want a bachelor's

Be Honest: Can you tell a manager bad news early, before it gets worse? Can you get people to do things when you have no authority over them? Can you accept that the schedule is your responsibility even when someone else broke it?
