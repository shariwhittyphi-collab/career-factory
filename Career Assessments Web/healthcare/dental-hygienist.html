Is this advanced dental role right for your future?

1Welcome

This assessment will help you explore whether becoming a dental hygienist is a good fit for you.

You'll learn what the job really involves, the education required, career opportunities, and what it takes to succeed.

Heads up: Becoming a dental hygienist takes more education than becoming a dental assistant. We'll be honest about the commitment this requires.

This takes about 15 minutes.

2What is a Dental Hygienist?

A dental hygienist is a licensed healthcare professional who works with dentists to care for patients' teeth and gums.

Key Difference from Dental Assistant: Dental hygienists are licensed, can perform many procedures independently, and have higher education requirements. In ND they work under a dentist's supervision, but they do much of their patient care on their own.

Your Daily Responsibilities:

  • Clean teeth and remove tartar (scaling and root planing)
  • Take and analyze X-rays
  • Apply fluoride and sealants
  • Teach patients proper brushing and flossing (patient education is huge)
  • Screen for oral disease and cancer
  • Take patient history and vital signs
  • Work in hospitals, dental offices, public health clinics, schools
  • Give local anesthesia (in ND, with a board permit)
  • Perform periodontal (gum disease) treatment

You're clinical but also a teacher. You spend real time with patients, building relationships. You're also independent — you manage your own schedule within the practice.

3A Day in the Life

8:00 AM — Arrive. Review patient charts. Prepare treatment area. Check that all instruments are sterile.

8:30 AM — First patient: a 45-year-old with gum disease. Take her blood pressure. Review her health history. Discuss her flossing habits (she doesn't do it).

9:00 AM — Perform scaling on her lower teeth. Remove years of buildup. She feels better already.

9:45 AM — Teach her the correct way to floss. Give her tips on gum disease prevention. She actually listens to you.

10:00 AM — Clean instruments. Document treatment in the chart. Prepare for next patient.

10:15 AM — Second patient: a routine cleaning. You take X-rays. Everything looks good. You notice a small issue you show the dentist.

11:00 AM — More patient education. Answer questions about electric toothbrushes, water flossers, whitening.

12:00 PM — Lunch (usually a real break)

1:00 PM – 4:30 PM — More patients. Each one is different. Some have anxiety. You calm them. Some have great habits. You praise them.

4:30 PM — Clean and sterilize. Update charts. Schedule patients for their next appointment.

5:00 PM — Leave. Done for the day.

You work independently but collaboratively. You're the expert in preventive care. The dentist trusts your clinical judgment.

4Training in North Dakota

Dental Hygienist License Required. You cannot work as a hygienist in North Dakota without a license.

Education Path:

  • Associate Degree (about 3 years, including prerequisite classes): College program. Combines classroom, lab, and clinical practice. Most common path. Costs vary by school, so check with the program.
  • Bachelor's Degree: For hygienists who already have an associate degree. NDSCS offers an online Bachelor of Applied Science in Dental Hygiene. Helps if you want to teach.

North Dakota Schools (Accredited):

  • North Dakota State College of Science (Wahpeton) — AAS degree, CODA-accredited
  • Dakota College at Bottineau (Minot campus) — AAS degree, CODA initial accreditation
  • Bismarck State College (Bismarck) — new program, seeking CODA accreditation. You must graduate from a CODA-accredited program to get an ND license, so ask about its status.

Curriculum Includes: Anatomy, pharmacology, periodontics, oral pathology, radiology, nutrition, patient communication, ethics, business skills.

Clinical Practice: You'll work with real patients under supervision. This is required for your license.

5Licensing & National Boards

North Dakota Dental Hygiene License — Required to work. Maintained through continuing education.

National Board Dental Hygiene Examination (NBDHE)

  • Written exam: 350 multiple-choice questions on dental science, patient care, and safety
  • Cost: $600 (2026)
  • Clinical exam: A separate hands-on exam from a testing agency (like CRDTS or ADEX)

State Exam: You also pass North Dakota's online Jurisprudence & Ethics exam and a background check.

Continuing Education: Hygienists must complete 16 CE hours every 2 years to keep the license. Up to half can be self-study.

Why This Matters: The licensing requirements ensure quality. They protect patients. They also protect your credentials. If you move, you apply for a new state license, but your national exam scores help.

6Salary & Job Outlook

North Dakota Dental Hygienist Salary (BLS, May 2025):

  • Lower end (bottom 10%): about $40/hour ($82,890/year)
  • Median (middle): $43.53/hour ($90,530/year)
  • Top earners (top 10%): about $47/hour ($98,530/year)

Significantly Higher Than Dental Assistant. The extra education pays off.

Benefits: Health insurance, retirement plans, paid time off, dental coverage.

Job Outlook: Good. Gum disease is common. Preventive care is growing. BLS projects 8% growth nationally from 2025 to 2035 (much faster than average), with about 15,000 openings a year.

Work Environment: Typically Monday-Friday, 8am-5pm. Part-time and flexible schedules available.

7Where Does This Lead?

A dental hygiene license opens many doors.

Path 1: Private Dental Practice — Work for dentists, build a patient base, become a trusted team member. Many practices value experienced hygienists highly.

Path 2: Periodontal Specialist — Work in a gum-care (periodontal) office. Learn advanced gum treatment through extra courses.

Path 3: Public Health / School Dentistry — Work in underserved communities, schools, public health departments. Different schedule. Different patient population. Meaningful work.

Path 4: Teaching — Teach dental hygiene students. Need bachelor's degree, usually. Stable, rewarding, competitive salary.

Path 5: Corporate / Insurance Roles — Dental insurance companies, product companies. Clinical background + business skills = consulting, product management, education roles.

Path 6: Dentistry — Some hygienists go on to dental school. You understand the field. Dental school is 4 more years, usually after a bachelor's degree.

Path 7: Expanded Duties / Advanced Roles — In ND, hygienists can earn board permits for local anesthesia, nitrous oxide, and restorative care.

8The Real Talk

What's Great:

  • License = respect and autonomy
  • Build real relationships with patients
  • You make a difference in prevention (which actually matters)
  • Decent salary without 10 years of education
  • Job security
  • Flexible work arrangements
  • Skills in demand in every state (a new state means a new license)

What's Hard:

  • Hygiene school and licensing take about 3 years with prerequisites — that's real time and money
  • You're on your feet, using your wrists and hands a lot. Repetitive strain is real.
  • You are exposed to blood and saliva, so strict infection control is a must
  • Patients with anxiety, bad breath, poor hygiene — you deal with it all
  • Insurance and dental office politics. You have to navigate relationships.
  • Continuing education is mandatory and can get expensive
  • Some days you see people who don't follow your advice — frustrating

Be honest: Do you want to go back to school for 2-4 years? Can you handle the physical demands? Do you genuinely care about preventing disease (not just cleaning teeth)? If yes, this works.
