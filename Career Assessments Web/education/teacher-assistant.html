Teacher Assistant Career Assessment

Helping students learn, one small group or one child at a time

Section 1 of 11

1Welcome

A student is stuck on a math problem. A small group needs someone to read with them. A child needs a calm adult nearby. Teacher assistants, often called paraprofessionals or paras, help teachers give students that extra attention.

This career means working closely with students all day. Explore what it feels like before deciding whether it interests you.

Go at your pace. The short questions at the end are for reflection. There are no right or wrong answers.

2What is a Teacher Assistant?

You help a teacher in the classroom. You work with students one at a time or in small groups, help keep students safe and on task, and support the teacher's plan. Some assistants work with students who have disabilities.

  • Help students with lessons one at a time or in small groups.
  • Read aloud and practice skills the teacher has taught.
  • Watch students in classrooms, hallways, lunchrooms, and playgrounds.
  • Help students follow school rules and get along.
  • Get materials ready and help clean up.
  • Talk with the teacher about how students are doing.

The real picture: You are with students almost all day. The teacher plans the lessons, and you help carry them out.

3A Day in the Life

One possible day: Your day follows the school schedule.

  • Morning: Greet students and help them get settled.
  • Later: Lead a small reading group while the teacher works with others.
  • Midday: Watch students at lunch and recess.
  • Afternoon: Sit beside a student who needs extra help with math.
  • Before leaving: Tell the teacher what went well and what a student found hard.

Some days are calm. Others bring upset students or sudden changes. Staying patient is part of the job.

4How You Learn the Job

There is more than one way in. School districts set their own hiring rules, and some jobs have state requirements. Read the actual job ad.

  • Title I paraprofessionals: For jobs paid with Title I funds, North Dakota requires a high school diploma or equivalent plus one of these: two years of college (48 semester hours), an associate degree or higher, or passing a North Dakota-approved test of reading, math, and writing skills.
  • Special education paraprofessionals: North Dakota requires 20 hours of training within the first year on the job. Valley City State, Minot State, and Mayville State universities offer courses that meet this certificate.
  • Optional education: Dakota College at Bottineau and Sitting Bull College in Fort Yates offer teacher assistant associate degrees.
  • On-the-job training: Districts train assistants on school rules, safety, and how to help students.

Paying for training:

  1. Start with financial aid, scholarships, and grants. The school's financial aid office can help.
  2. Ask employers whether they help pay for training.
  3. Ask for the full cost, including books, fees, travel, and a computer.
  4. Ask your career counselor about other help, such as WIOA. WIOA is a federal program that can help some people pay for job training. Ask before you enroll.

Employer help with school may come with a promise to keep working there. Read the terms first.

5Training and Employer Requirements

Paraprofessional certificates are handled by the North Dakota Department of Public Instruction through NDTeach. Ask the school district which one the job needs.

  • A Title I or special education paraprofessional certificate, depending on the job.
  • A background check, which schools require.
  • Training on student privacy and safety.
  • Some districts ask for first aid or CPR.
  • A teaching license counts in place of the Title I paraprofessional certificate.

Before you begin: Ask what training the district provides and who you go to with questions about a student.

Ask about written instructions, an accessible workstation, extra practice time, or other support that helps you learn and do the work well.

6Pay and Job Outlook

North Dakota teacher assistant pay — May 2025 (yearly):

  • Middle pay: $38,240 a year. Half of workers earned more and half earned less.
  • Half of workers earned between $35,990 and $44,570 a year.
  • 1 in 10 earned less than $29,130, and 1 in 10 earned more than $46,260.
  • These numbers show what workers earned. They are not a promise of what a new worker will start at.
  • North Dakota had about 5,260 of these jobs in 2024. The state projects about 5,600 in 2034, a change of +7%. That works out to about 700 openings a year, mostly from people who retire or change jobs.

Where people work: Public and private schools, preschools, and special education programs across the state.

Work setting: Classrooms, hallways, lunchrooms, and playgrounds. You work close to students and move around much of the day.

Compare an offer: Ask about hourly or yearly pay, hours per day, whether summers are paid, benefits, and paid training.

7Where This Can Lead

  • Special education assistant: Work closely with students who have disabilities.
  • Special education technician: North Dakota has a technician credential for experienced special education paraprofessionals.
  • Teacher: Some assistants earn a bachelor's degree and a teaching license. Ask about programs for working paraprofessionals.
  • A related direction: Childcare, after-school programs, or youth work.

Working in a school is a good way to find out whether teaching is right for you.

8The Real Talk

Things you might like:

  • Helping students learn and grow.
  • A schedule that follows the school year in many jobs.
  • Schools in almost every North Dakota town.
  • A way to try out working in education.

Things to ask about:

  • Pay that is lower than many full-year jobs.
  • Summers that may be unpaid.
  • Students who are upset or have big needs.
  • Being on your feet and moving around all day.

North Dakota expects about 7% growth over ten years, with about 700 openings a year.

A question for yourself: Do you have the patience to help a student keep trying until they get it?
