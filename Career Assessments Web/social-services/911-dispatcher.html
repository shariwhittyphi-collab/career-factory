911 Dispatcher Career Assessment

Answering emergency calls and sending help where it is needed

Section 1 of 11

1Welcome

The first voice people hear in an emergency belongs to a 911 dispatcher.

This job mixes calm communication, quick thinking, and teamwork. Explore what it feels like before deciding whether it interests you.

Go at your pace. The short questions at the end are for reflection. There are no right or wrong answers.

2What is a 911 Dispatcher?

You answer emergency and non-emergency calls and send police, fire, and ambulance crews where they are needed.

  • Answer calls and find out where the caller is.
  • Ask questions to learn what is happening.
  • Decide which calls are most urgent.
  • Send the right crews by radio and computer.
  • Give callers instructions until help arrives.
  • Record details of every call.

The real picture: Many calls are not emergencies. Some are frightening. Dispatchers handle both, often at the same time.

3A Day in the Life

One possible day: Every center is different.

  • Start of shift: Get a briefing on what is happening in the area.
  • Morning: Handle a car crash call and send crews.
  • Midday: Answer non-emergency calls and track units by radio.
  • Afternoon: Give a caller medical instructions until the ambulance arrives.
  • End of shift: Finish records and brief the next dispatcher.

Quiet stretches can change in seconds.

4How You Learn the Job

Most centers hire people with a high school diploma or GED and train them.

  • Required training: North Dakota law requires each 911 center to give dispatchers initial and ongoing training.
  • Medical instructions: Dispatchers who give medical instructions before help arrives must finish an approved emergency medical dispatch course.
  • On the job: New dispatchers train with an experienced dispatcher before working alone.

Paying for training:

  1. Start with financial aid, scholarships, and grants. The school's financial aid office can help.
  2. Ask employers whether they help pay for training.
  3. Ask for the full cost, including books, fees, travel, and a computer.
  4. Ask your career counselor about other help, such as WIOA. WIOA is a federal program that can help some people pay for job training. Ask before you enroll.

Employer help with school may come with a promise to keep working there. Read the terms first.

5Training and Employer Requirements

North Dakota sets hiring rules for 911 centers. Ask each center for its full list.

  • Fingerprinting and a nationwide background check, with no felony conviction.
  • Drug screening and a hearing test before hiring.
  • Initial and ongoing training from the center.
  • An emergency medical dispatch course to give medical instructions.
  • Willingness to work rotating 24/7 shifts.

Before you begin: Ask how the center supports dispatchers after hard calls.

Ask about written instructions, an accessible workstation, extra practice time, or other support that helps you learn and do the work well.

6Pay and Job Outlook

North Dakota 911 dispatcher pay — May 2025:

  • Middle pay: $28.82 an hour ($59,940 a year). Half of workers earned more and half earned less.
  • Half of workers earned between $23.88 and $29.45 an hour.
  • 1 in 10 earned less than $22.97 an hour, and 1 in 10 earned more than $30.37.
  • These numbers show what workers earned. They are not a promise of what a new worker will start at.
  • North Dakota had about 300 of these jobs in 2024. The state projects about 320 in 2034, a change of +6%. That works out to about 30 openings a year, mostly from people who retire or change jobs.

Where people work: City and county 911 centers, state radio, and some hospitals and ambulance services.

Work setting: Dispatch centers with computers and radios, staffed 24 hours a day.

Compare an offer: Ask about base pay, shift pay, overtime, benefits, and support after hard calls.

7Where This Can Lead

  • Senior dispatcher or trainer: Train new dispatchers.
  • Shift supervisor: Lead a team.
  • 911 center manager: Run a dispatch center.
  • Related careers: EMT, firefighter, or police officer.

Ask dispatchers near you which paths are open.

8The Real Talk

Things you might like:

  • Helping people at the most important moment.
  • Teamwork with first responders.
  • Paid training and steady public jobs.
  • Fast-paced, never-boring work.

Things to ask about:

  • Stress and upsetting calls.
  • Nights, weekends, holidays, and overtime.
  • Long shifts at a console.
  • Support for dispatchers’ mental health.

North Dakota projects about 6% growth in these jobs from 2024 to 2034, with about 30 openings a year.

A question for yourself: Can you stay calm and clear when someone else is in crisis?
