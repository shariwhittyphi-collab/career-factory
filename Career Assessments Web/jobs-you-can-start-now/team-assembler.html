Team Assembler Career Assessment

The best-paying job in this area with no school required

Section 1 of 11

1Welcome

Team assemblers build finished products on a production floor, working as part of a crew where each person handles part of the build.

The North Dakota median is about $22.71 an hour — the highest in this area — with roughly 630 openings a year and no diploma required at most plants.

This takes about 15 minutes.

2What is a Team Assembler?

You assemble parts into a finished product to a specification, working as part of a team that rotates between stations rather than each person doing one thing forever. You read a work order or a build sheet, use hand and power tools, check your own work against the spec, and keep up with the rate the line is set to. North Dakota has real manufacturing — farm equipment, trailers, modular buildings, recreational vehicles, electronics, and food processing equipment — and these are the people who build it.

The Reality: If nobody assembles, nothing gets built. That is the plain version. The honest version of the work is this: the rate is set for you, and the quota almost always includes its own overhead — the setup, fetching your own parts, the quality checking — not just the building. People who have never done it hear the number and think it means only the assembly. It does not. You are also usually your own quality control, which means a defect is yours even when nobody allowed time to catch it. And the repetition is real: hours of the same motion leaves your wrists and arms aching in a way that does not show up on day one, it shows up in month four. Breaks exist, and they are often not long enough to be a break once you have walked to where you can sit down. All of that is true, and it still pays better than any other job in this area.

Your Responsibilities:

  • Assemble parts into a finished product to a written specification
  • Read build sheets, work orders, and simple drawings
  • Use hand tools, power tools, and jigs
  • Check your own work against the spec before it moves on
  • Rotate between stations as the team needs
  • Keep your station stocked with parts and materials
  • Record output and flag defects and shortages
  • Keep up with the line rate without cutting the quality checks
  • Follow lockout and machine safety rules

Assembly is a team job with an individual standard: the line sets the pace, and your name is still on what you built.

3A Day in the Life

6:00 AM — Clock in. Start-up meeting — what is building today and any changes.

6:15 AM — Set up your station, check parts and tools.

6:30 AM — Line starts. Build, check, pass on.

8:30 AM — Break.

8:45 AM — Back on. Rotate to a different station.

11:00 AM — Lunch.

11:30 AM — Changeover to a different model. Re-set the jigs.

1:30 PM — Break.

1:45 PM — Finish the run, log output, clean the station, hand over.

A fixed shift with a set rate, station rotation, and a tight changeover in the middle.

4Training in North Dakota

No school needed at most plants. Trained on the job over a few weeks. You learn each station from someone experienced and rotate through them as you pick them up. Plants that build more complex products take longer to train you, and they pay accordingly.

Training Pathways:

  • Direct hire into a plant: The main route. North Dakota manufacturers hire year-round, and many will take someone with no manufacturing background at all.
  • From packing, warehouse, or general labor: A very common path. If you are already inside a plant in another position, ask about assembly openings — internal applicants usually get them.
  • Through a staffing agency: Also common. Ask what the rate becomes when the employer hires you on directly, because the difference is often significant.
  • Into skilled trades: Welding, machining, maintenance, and industrial mechanics all recruit from assembly floors, and many plants will pay for that training.

Where to Train in North Dakota:

Before You Start: A high school diploma or equivalent is preferred by some plants but not required by all — ask rather than assume. You need steady hands, the ability to follow a written specification, and the stamina for a full shift of repetitive work. Most positions require you to be 18.

Tip: Most of these jobs need no school, so the cost of starting is not tuition — it is the things you need in the first month. Non-slip shoes, work clothes or a uniform, a way to get to work, and getting through the two or three weeks before the first paycheck arrives. Ask the employer first: many will supply a uniform, some give a shoe allowance, and some pay weekly rather than every two weeks, which matters a lot at the start. After that, ask a career counselor or Job Service North Dakota whether WIOA or other programs can help with what is left over, like work clothes, boots, tools, or getting to work. What can be paid for depends on the program and on your own situation, so ask before you start rather than after.

5Certification & Licensing

No license or certificate is required. You can be hired with nothing. Everything you need is trained inside the plant, and the certifications that raise your pay are normally employer-paid.

Worth having:

  • Forklift certification — employer-provided and free; useful on nearly any plant floor
  • OSHA 10 General Industry — a short safety course that plants value and often provide
  • Blueprint reading — short courses exist at North Dakota two-year colleges and move you toward better-paid work
  • Welding or machining certificates — the common step from assembly into a skilled trade, and plants often pay for them

The Exams: None required to start. Equipment certifications involve a short test and a hands-on check at work.

Cost: Nothing to start. Employer-provided training is the norm.

Keeping It Current: Forklift certification renews every three years; plant safety training is usually yearly.

6Salary & Job Outlook

North Dakota Team Assembler Pay (May 2025):

  • Starting out (bottom 10–25%): $18.42–$21.74/hour ($38,300–$45,210/year)
  • Middle (median — half earn more, half earn less): $22.71/hour ($47,250/year)
  • Experienced (top 25–10%): $28.31–$33.00/hour ($58,880–$68,630/year)

What that means: Note the bottom of this range: about $18.42 an hour to start is above the median of most jobs in this area. The upper end is experienced assemblers in more complex manufacturing, leads, and people who also run equipment. Shift premiums for evenings and nights are common, and overtime is frequent in North Dakota manufacturing — ask whether it is regular and whether it is required. If you come in through a staffing agency, ask what the direct-hire rate is.

Jobs in North Dakota: About 5,200 people do this work in ND today. By 2034, that should be about 5,670 (about 9% growth). Expect about 630 job openings every year in ND from growth and people retiring or changing jobs. Across the U.S., the change is little or no change nationally.

Outlook: Good in this state, and better than the national picture. North Dakota expects about 9% growth in assembly and fabrication work by 2034, from roughly 5,200 to 5,670 positions, with about 630 openings a year — while the national figure is essentially flat. Manufacturing here is tied to agriculture, energy, and equipment, and those are not going anywhere.

Who Hires in ND: Farm and agricultural equipment manufacturers, trailer and truck body builders, modular and manufactured housing plants, recreational vehicle makers, electronics manufacturers, and food processing equipment companies.

Work Setting: Indoors on a plant floor. Loud enough that hearing protection is standard. Safety glasses and steel-toe boots are normal. Many plants run two or three shifts.

Physical Demands: High in a repetitive way rather than a heavy way. Standing for a full shift, repeated hand and arm motion, some lifting, working at a set rate. Wrists, hands, shoulders, and lower back are what take the wear.

Sources: U.S. Bureau of Labor Statistics, Occupational Employment and Wage Statistics, North Dakota, May 2025 (SOC 51-2092); North Dakota and U.S. long-term employment projections 2024–2034, via O*NET OnLine. Numbers are rounded.

7Where This Leads

Path 1: Team Assembler — Where you start.

Path 2: Lead Assembler — Set the line, train new people, handle changeovers.

Path 3: Quality Control Inspector — Checking product formally, off the line.

Path 4: Machine Operator or Setup — Running and setting the equipment.

Path 5: Welder or Machinist — A skilled trade, often with the plant paying for the training.

Path 6: Industrial Maintenance — Keeping the plant running. One of the best-paid routes out of assembly.

Path 7: Production Supervisor — Running a shift and a crew.

8The Real Talk

What's Great:

  • The highest median pay in this area at about $22.71 an hour
  • Even the bottom of the range beats most jobs on these pages
  • About 9% growth in North Dakota, against flat nationally
  • No school required at most plants
  • Indoor, year-round, weather-independent work
  • Plants promote from inside and often pay for skilled-trade training
  • Overtime and shift premiums are common and can add a lot

What's Hard:

  • The rate is set for you, and the quota usually includes its own setup and checking
  • You are often your own quality control with no time allowed for it
  • Repetitive motion wears on wrists, arms, and shoulders over months, not days
  • Breaks are built around production, and they can be shorter in practice than on paper
  • Loud, and hearing protection is not optional
  • Shift work including nights at many plants
  • Standing on concrete for a full shift

Be Honest: Can you hold your quality at a pace somebody else set? Will you speak up about wrist or shoulder pain in month two instead of year two? And are you willing to ask about welding, maintenance, or quality control training, which is where the real money in a plant is?
