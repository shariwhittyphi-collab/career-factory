Maintenance and Repair Worker Career Assessment

Fixing a little of everything so buildings and machines keep working

Section 1 of 11

1Welcome

A leaky faucet, a broken door, a light that will not come on. Someone has to fix them, and in many buildings that person is the maintenance worker.

This job mixes many small trades. Explore what it feels like before deciding whether it interests you.

Go at your pace. The short questions at the end are for reflection. There are no right or wrong answers.

2What is a Maintenance and Repair Worker?

You keep buildings, equipment, and grounds in working order. You do routine upkeep and fix problems as they come up.

  • Do routine upkeep, like changing filters, belts, and fluids.
  • Find out why something stopped working.
  • Repair doors, locks, fixtures, and drywall.
  • Do small plumbing and painting jobs.
  • Order parts and supplies.
  • Keep records of the work you finished.

The real picture: A school job, an apartment job, and a factory job are very different. Some work is mostly building repairs; some is mostly machines.

3A Day in the Life

One possible day: Every building has its own list.

  • Morning: Check the work orders and plan the route.
  • Later: Replace a faucet part and fix a sticking door.
  • Midday: Change air filters in the heating system.
  • Afternoon: Answer an urgent call about a leak.
  • Before leaving: Write down what was finished and order a part for tomorrow.

Plans change when something urgent breaks. Some jobs include being on call.

4How You Learn the Job

Many people learn on the job, often after a high school diploma or GED. Many employers like to see a certificate or experience in a trade.

  • On-the-job training: An experienced worker teaches the building’s systems.
  • Apprenticeship: Building and industrial maintenance apprenticeships pay you while you learn.
  • Short classes: Courses in HVAC, plumbing basics, or electrical safety can help you get hired.
  • Start nearby: Janitor, groundskeeper, or helper jobs can lead to maintenance work.

Paying for training:

  1. Start with financial aid, scholarships, and grants. The school's financial aid office can help.
  2. Ask employers whether they help pay for training.
  3. Ask for the full cost, including books, fees, travel, and a computer.
  4. Ask your career counselor about other help, such as WIOA. WIOA is a federal program that can help some people pay for job training. Ask before you enroll.

Employer help with school may come with a promise to keep working there. Read the terms first.

5Training and Employer Requirements

There is no general state license for maintenance work. Some tasks are licensed: in North Dakota, electrical wiring work needs a state electrical license, and plumbing has its own rules. Ask exactly what your job will include.

  • Comfort with hand and power tools.
  • Ability to climb ladders, lift, and work in tight spaces, or ask about the physical demands.
  • A driver’s license for jobs that cover several buildings.
  • A background check for jobs in schools or health care.

Before you begin: Ask which repairs you will do yourself and which go to licensed trades.

Ask about written instructions, a buddy on your first jobs, extra practice time, or other support that helps you learn and work safely.

6Pay and Job Outlook

North Dakota maintenance and repair worker pay — May 2025:

  • Middle pay: $23.96 an hour ($49,840 a year). Half of workers earned more and half earned less.
  • Half of workers earned between $20.06 and $28.68 an hour.
  • 1 in 10 earned less than $17.44 an hour, and 1 in 10 earned more than $32.91.
  • These numbers show what workers earned. They are not a promise of what a new worker will start at.
  • North Dakota had about 3,680 of these jobs in 2024. The state projects about 3,980 in 2034, a change of +8%. That works out to about 380 openings a year, mostly from people who retire or change jobs.

Where people work: Schools, apartment buildings, hospitals and clinics, factories, hotels, and cities and counties.

Work setting: Inside and outside buildings, often moving from task to task. Some jobs include on-call or weekend duty.

Compare an offer: Ask about base pay, on-call pay, overtime, benefits, and whether the employer pays for classes.

7Where This Can Lead

  • Lead maintenance worker: Take on harder repairs and train others.
  • Facilities supervisor or manager: Run maintenance for a building or campus.
  • A licensed trade: Electrician, plumber, or HVAC technician through an apprenticeship.
  • Industrial maintenance: Specialize in factory machines.

Ask people where you work which paths are open.

8The Real Talk

Things you might like:

  • Fixing things and seeing them work again.
  • A different mix of tasks every day.
  • Jobs in almost every town.
  • Skills you can use at home, too.

Things to ask about:

  • On-call duty, nights, or weekend emergencies.
  • Dirty, cramped, or hot spaces.
  • Lifting and climbing.
  • Many people asking for help at once.

North Dakota projects about 8% growth in these jobs from 2024 to 2034, with about 380 openings a year.
