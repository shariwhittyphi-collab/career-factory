Butcher and Meat Cutter Career Assessment

Cutting, trimming, and packaging meat in stores and processing plants

Section 1 of 11

1Welcome

The steaks, roasts, and ground beef at a grocery store or a local locker plant were cut and wrapped by butchers and meat cutters.

This job mixes knife skills, food safety, and steady hands-on work. Explore what it feels like before deciding whether it interests you.

Go at your pace. The short questions at the end are for reflection. There are no right or wrong answers.

2What is a Butcher and Meat Cutter?

You cut, trim, and package meat so it is ready to sell and cook. You work at grocery store meat counters, local meat markets and locker plants, and processing plants.

  • Cut, trim, bone, and grind meat.
  • Wrap, weigh, and label packages.
  • Prepare special cuts that customers order.
  • Check meat when it arrives and store it properly.
  • Set up the display case so it looks good.
  • Clean and sanitize tools and work areas.

The real picture: A small-town meat market, a grocery store, and a large plant are very different jobs. Plants often have one task on a line; small shops do a bit of everything.

3A Day in the Life

One possible day: Every shop runs differently.

  • Morning: Put on protective gear, check deliveries, and plan the day’s cuts.
  • Later: Cut steaks and roasts and grind beef.
  • Midday: Wrap, weigh, and label packages for the case.
  • Afternoon: Prepare a custom order and help customers at the counter.
  • Before leaving: Clean and sanitize every surface and tool.

Fall hunting season and holidays can bring busy stretches.

4How You Learn the Job

Most people learn on the job. Many employers hire people with a high school diploma or GED. Training can take from a few weeks to about a year.

  • On-the-job training: An experienced cutter teaches knife safety, cuts, and food safety.
  • Short certificate: North Dakota State College of Science in Wahpeton and Dickinson State University offer meat cutting certificates.
  • Start nearby: A meat department clerk or packaging job can lead to cutting.

Paying for training:

  1. Start with financial aid, scholarships, and grants. The school's financial aid office can help.
  2. Ask employers whether they help pay for training.
  3. Ask for the full cost, including books, fees, travel, and a computer.
  4. Ask your career counselor about other help, such as WIOA. WIOA is a federal program that can help some people pay for job training. Ask before you enroll.

Employer help with school may come with a promise to keep working there. Read the terms first.

5Training and Employer Requirements

We did not find a North Dakota license for individual butchers. Meat processing businesses are licensed and inspected by the North Dakota Department of Agriculture. Ask each employer what you need to start.

  • Food safety training, which employers often provide.
  • Comfort working in cold rooms.
  • Ability to stand and lift for long periods, or ask about the physical demands.
  • Willingness to wear protective gear like mesh gloves.

Before you begin: Ask how the employer teaches knife safety and how much practice time you get.

Ask about written instructions, an accessible workstation, extra practice time, or other support that helps you learn and do the work well.

6Pay and Job Outlook

North Dakota butcher and meat cutter pay — May 2025:

  • Middle pay: $19.27 an hour ($40,080 a year). Half of workers earned more and half earned less.
  • Half of workers earned between $17.37 and $22.75 an hour.
  • 1 in 10 earned less than $10.76 an hour, and 1 in 10 earned more than $27.48.
  • These numbers show what workers earned. They are not a promise of what a new worker will start at.
  • North Dakota had about 590 of these jobs in 2024. The state projects about 590 in 2034, a change of +1%. That works out to about 70 openings a year, mostly from people who retire or change jobs.

The job counts are rounded to the nearest 10, so the small change does not show in them.

Where people work: Grocery stores, meat markets and locker plants, and meat processing plants.

Work setting: Cold, clean work rooms and store counters. Many jobs include weekends.

Compare an offer: Ask about base pay, shifts, benefits, and how the employer trains new cutters.

7Where This Can Lead

  • Experienced meat cutter: Handle custom and specialty cuts.
  • Meat department manager: Run a store’s meat counter.
  • Lead or supervisor: Lead a line in a processing plant.
  • Your own shop: Some butchers run a local meat market or locker plant.

Ask people in the trade which paths are open.

8The Real Talk

Things you might like:

  • Hands-on work with a clear result.
  • Learning a craft that people value.
  • Starting without a degree.
  • Jobs in many towns.

Things to ask about:

  • Cold work areas.
  • Sharp tools and the risk of cuts.
  • Repetitive motions that can strain hands and wrists.
  • Weekend and holiday hours.

North Dakota projects the number of these jobs to stay about the same from 2024 to 2034, with about 70 openings a year.

A question for yourself: Does careful, hands-on food work sound like a good fit for you?
