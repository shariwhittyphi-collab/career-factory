Paramedic Career Assessment

Emergency medical specialist

Section 1 of 11

1Welcome

Paramedics provide emergency medical care in the field, responding to 911 calls and transporting patients to hospitals. It's life-or-death work that requires quick thinking and courage.

You'll explore what this intense, demanding career is really like, and whether you have what it takes.

This takes about 15 minutes.

2What is a Paramedic?

A paramedic is an advanced emergency medical professional who responds to 911 calls, provides emergency medical care in the field, and transports patients to hospitals. Paramedics are the highest level of EMS training.

The Reality: You're often the first professional to arrive at a medical emergency. Your decisions and actions in the first minutes can mean the difference between life and death. No pressure.

Your Responsibilities:

  • Respond to 911 emergency calls
  • Assess patients quickly and accurately
  • Provide advanced life support (medications, defibrillation, airway management)
  • Treat trauma, cardiac events, strokes, respiratory failure, poisoning
  • Transport patients safely to appropriate hospitals
  • Work with EMT-Basics on your ambulance crew
  • Make split-second medical decisions
  • Maintain equipment and supplies
  • Document patient care thoroughly
  • Handle emotional and traumatic situations

You're medic, decision-maker, and first responder all at once.

3A Day in the Life

7:00 AM — Start shift at the station. Briefing. Review equipment. Check ambulance. Restock supplies from yesterday's calls.

8:00 AM — Routine patrol. Wait for calls. Familiarize yourself with your district. Review protocols.

9:15 AM — First call: chest pain. 67-year-old male. Possible cardiac event. Respond in 4 minutes. Assess. 12-lead EKG. IV access. Medications. Rapid transport to cardiac center. Patient stable.

10:30 AM — Document call. Clean ambulance. Restock. Back in service.

11:00 AM — Second call: car accident. Two vehicles. Three patients. Triage. One critical (head injury). Two minor. Call for additional ambulances. Manage critical patient. Prepare for transport.

12:30 PM — Hospital handoff. Paperwork. Ambulance back in service.

1:00 PM — Lunch (if time permits, station meal)

2:00 PM – 5:00 PM — More calls. Choking victim. Diabetic emergency. Fall with fracture. Alcohol intoxication. Varying acuity. Constant vigilance.

5:00 PM — End shift. Debrief. Mental/emotional processing.

High-stress, high-stakes, unpredictable, physically demanding work.

4Training in North Dakota

NREMT-Paramedic Certification Required. ND law requires state licensure. You cannot work as a paramedic without it.

Training Pathways:

  • Paramedic Program (about 1-2 years): Classroom + lab + clinical + field internship. EMT prerequisite (complete first). Certificate or 2-year associate degree (AAS). Costs vary by school.
  • Employer Help: Some ambulance services and fire departments help pay for paramedic school. Ask about any work commitment.

In North Dakota: EMT first (one semester or less), then Paramedic. CAAHEP-accredited programs: Bismarck State College (Bismarck), NDSCS with Sanford Health (Fargo), and Dakota College at Bottineau (Minot, with distance sites). Clinical rotations at hospitals and ambulance services.

Prerequisites: EMT certification or license first. CPR (BLS) card. Background check. You must be 18 to get a ND paramedic license.

Clinical Hours: Hundreds of hours in hospital clinicals and a field internship with working paramedics. Real calls. Real patients. Real responsibility.

5Certification & Licensing

NRP (Nationally Registered Paramedic). National Registry exam. Required for ND licensure.

North Dakota Requirements:

  • Get your EMT certification first (paramedic programs require it)
  • Complete CAAHEP-accredited paramedic program
  • Pass NREMT-Paramedic exam
  • Apply for ND state paramedic license (age 18+, linked to a ND ambulance service or ND medical director)

The Exam: Computer adaptive. 110-150 questions. Up to 3.5 hours. Mostly multiple choice, plus some newer question types and patient scenarios. Covers airway and breathing, heart care and CPR, trauma, medical and OB/GYN, and EMS operations. Child care questions are mixed in.

Cost: $175 each time you take the exam

Pass Rate: About 4 out of 5 pass on the first try (National Registry, recent years)

Continuing Education: Renew every 2 years. National Registry paramedics need 60 hours of CE per renewal. ND requires you to keep your National Registry current.

Reciprocity: Most states accept NREMT. You still apply for a license in each new state.

6Salary & Job Outlook

North Dakota Paramedic Salary (BLS, May 2025):

  • Lower pay (bottom 10%): $23.21/hour ($48,270/year)
  • Middle pay (median): $29.96/hour ($62,320/year)
  • Higher pay (top 10%): $36.27/hour ($75,440/year)

Higher pay in urban areas and specialized roles (flight paramedics, HEMS).

Benefits: Health insurance, retirement (pension in some departments), paid time off, hazard pay.

Job Outlook: Good. Jobs for EMTs and paramedics are expected to grow 6% nationally from 2025 to 2035 (BLS). Aging population. Moderate competition.

Work Environment: Ambulance services, fire departments, hospitals, critical care transport. Shift work: 12-24 hour shifts common. On-call possible.

Physical Demands: Heavy. Lifting patients (often obese, dead weight), running to scenes, carrying equipment, long hours standing in ambulance, sleeping at station.

7Where This Leads

Path 1: Fire Department — Some paramedics work for fire departments (firefighter-paramedic). Nationally, most paramedics work for local government or ambulance services (BLS).

Path 2: Flight Paramedic (HEMS) — Helicopter emergency medical services. Helicopter or fixed-wing. Highly specialized. Higher pay. More intense.

Path 3: Management — Station commander, EMS director, oversee paramedics and EMTs.

Path 4: Critical Care Transport — Interfacility transport of critically ill patients. More controlled than 911.

Path 5: Teaching — Teach paramedic program. Educate new providers.

Path 6: Nursing — Some paramedics go back to school to become an RN. Nurse anesthetist (CRNA) takes many more years after that. Medical background transfers.

Path 7: EMS Administration — Program development, quality assurance, policy.

8The Real Talk

What's Great:

  • Literally saving lives
  • Adrenaline and excitement
  • Respected profession
  • Good pay for education level
  • Strong camaraderie with crew
  • Diverse cases and patients
  • Career advancement options
  • Job security

What's Hard:

  • PTSD and trauma exposure (serious injuries, deaths, including children)
  • Burnout and compassion fatigue
  • Shift work (irregular sleep, health impacts)
  • Physical strain (back injuries, lifting)
  • Witnessing death and suffering daily
  • Moral/ethical dilemmas in the field
  • Difficult patients and families
  • Dealing with drug overdoses and addiction
  • Mental health crisis among paramedics
  • High stress can affect mental health and relationships. Peer support and counseling programs are common and worth using.

Be Honest: Can you handle life-or-death decisions? Can you witness trauma and not carry it home? Can you work shift work? Are you physically strong? Do you want to be a first responder?
