Bank Teller Career Assessment

Handling money carefully and helping customers at the counter

Section 1 of 11

1Welcome

A customer comes in to deposit a paycheck, take out cash, or ask a question about an account. The bank teller helps them and makes sure every dollar is counted correctly.

This job mixes customer service and careful money handling. Explore what it feels like before deciding whether it interests you.

Go at your pace. The short questions at the end are for reflection. There are no right or wrong answers.

2What is a Bank Teller?

You help customers with everyday banking at a counter or drive-up window. You take deposits, give out cash, cash checks, and answer questions. At the end of the day, your cash drawer has to match the computer exactly.

  • Take deposits and payments and give out cash.
  • Count money carefully and check it twice.
  • Use the bank's computer system for each transaction.
  • Check identification and follow security rules.
  • Answer questions and send customers to the right person for loans or new accounts.
  • Balance your cash drawer at the end of the day.

The real picture: Banks have strict rules, and tellers must follow them exactly. In small towns, tellers often get to know their customers well.

3A Day in the Life

One possible day: Every branch has its own rhythm.

  • Morning: Count your cash drawer with a coworker before the doors open.
  • Later: Help a line of customers with deposits and withdrawals.
  • Midday: A business brings in a large deposit to count and record.
  • Afternoon: Answer phone questions and help at the drive-up window.
  • Before leaving: Balance your drawer so it matches the computer to the penny.

Fridays, paydays, and the first of the month can be busy. Some branches are open Saturday mornings.

4How You Learn the Job

Most tellers learn on the job. About 7 in 10 people in this job say a high school diploma or GED is what new workers need. Many banks also want some cash handling or customer service experience. Read the actual job ad.

  • Build experience first: Cashier, retail, or customer service jobs teach counting money and helping customers.
  • Practice accuracy: Counting cash and checking your work matters more than speed.
  • On-the-job training: The bank teaches its computer system, security rules, and procedures, often for several weeks.
  • Optional education: Business or accounting classes can help if you want to move up in banking later.

Paying for training:

  1. Start with financial aid, scholarships, and grants. The school's financial aid office can help.
  2. Ask employers whether they help pay for training.
  3. Ask for the full cost, including books, fees, travel, and a computer.
  4. Ask your career counselor about other help, such as WIOA. WIOA is a federal program that can help some people pay for job training. Ask before you enroll.

Employer help with school may come with a promise to keep working there. Read the terms first.

5Training and Employer Requirements

No state license is needed to be a teller. Banks have their own requirements. Ask what you need to start.

  • Comfort counting money and using a computer.
  • A background check; ask each employer what it checks.
  • Training in security, privacy, and fraud rules.
  • Clear, friendly communication with customers.
  • A schedule that may include Saturday mornings; ask.

Before you begin: Ask how long training lasts and who helps you when your drawer does not balance.

Ask about written instructions, an accessible workstation, extra practice time, or other support that helps you learn and do the work well.

6Pay and Job Outlook

North Dakota teller pay — May 2025:

  • Middle pay: $18.90 an hour ($39,300 a year). Half of workers earned more and half earned less.
  • Half of workers earned between $18.27 and $22.04 an hour.
  • 1 in 10 earned less than $17.84 an hour, and 1 in 10 earned more than $22.97.
  • These numbers show what workers earned. They are not a promise of what a new worker will start at.
  • North Dakota had about 1,970 of these jobs in 2024. The state projects about 1,770 in 2034, a change of -10%. That works out to about 180 openings a year, mostly from people who retire or change jobs.

Where people work: Banks, credit unions, and other financial businesses in cities and small towns across the state.

Work setting: A teller counter or drive-up window with a computer and cash drawer. Many tellers spend about half the day standing. The work is indoors with set opening hours.

Compare an offer: Ask about base pay, hours, Saturday shifts, benefits, paid time off, training, and help paying for classes.

7Where This Can Lead

  • Head teller: Help other tellers and handle larger transactions.
  • Personal banker: Open accounts and help customers choose bank services.
  • Loan or operations work: Some tellers move into loan processing or back-office banking jobs.
  • A related direction: Bookkeeping, customer service, or office work.

Ask your employer whether they help pay for banking or business classes.

8The Real Talk

Things you might like:

  • Helping people with something important to them.
  • Clear rules for how things are done.
  • Mostly daytime hours at many branches.
  • A possible first step into banking.

Things to ask about:

  • Standing at a counter for long periods.
  • Pressure to get every dollar exactly right.
  • Customers who are frustrated about their money.
  • Fewer teller jobs over time.

North Dakota expects about 10% fewer teller jobs over ten years, with about 180 openings a year. Pay is close together from beginner to experienced, so moving up often means moving into another banking role.

A question for yourself: Do you like helping people while being very careful with details?
