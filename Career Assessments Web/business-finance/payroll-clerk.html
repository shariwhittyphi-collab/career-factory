Payroll Clerk Career Assessment

Making sure every worker is paid the right amount, on time

Section 1 of 11

1Welcome

Payday matters to everyone. Behind each paycheck, someone checked the hours, figured the taxes and deductions, and made sure the money went out on time. That is payroll.

This career mixes numbers, rules, and helping workers understand their pay. Explore what it feels like before deciding whether it interests you.

Go at your pace. The short questions at the end are for reflection. There are no right or wrong answers.

2What is a Payroll Clerk?

You make sure employees are paid correctly. You check hours and time sheets, enter pay changes, run payroll in software, and answer workers' questions about their paychecks.

  • Check hours worked, time off, and overtime.
  • Enter new workers and pay changes into the payroll system.
  • Figure pay, taxes, and deductions using payroll software.
  • Fix mistakes before paychecks go out.
  • Prepare reports and match payroll to bank records.
  • Answer workers' questions about their pay.

The real picture: Payroll runs on firm deadlines. A mistake affects a real person's paycheck, so careful checking matters every time.

3A Day in the Life

One possible day: The week follows the pay schedule.

  • Morning: Collect time sheets and check for missing hours.
  • Later: Enter a new hire, a raise, and a change to someone's tax form.
  • Midday: A worker stops by because their overtime looks wrong. Look it up and explain it.
  • Afternoon: Run a payroll preview and check it line by line.
  • Before leaving: List anything that must be fixed before payroll is final.

The days before payday are busy. The end of the year brings extra tax forms.

4How You Learn the Job

Many people come to payroll from other office work. Employers often look for bookkeeping, office, or human resources experience. Some ask for a college degree, so read the actual job ad.

  • Build basic skills first: Spreadsheets, careful data entry, and basic math with percentages.
  • Start nearby: Bookkeeping, office clerk, or human resources assistant jobs can lead to payroll.
  • Learn the software: Employers use payroll systems such as ADP, Workday, or Kronos. Each employer trains its own system.
  • Optional certification: PayrollOrg offers the Fundamental Payroll Certification for people new to payroll. Its website lists no experience requirement. Check the current cost and requirements on the PayrollOrg website.
  • Optional education: Accounting, business, or human resources classes at a North Dakota college can help.

Paying for training:

  1. Start with financial aid, scholarships, and grants. The school's financial aid office can help.
  2. Ask employers whether they help pay for training.
  3. Ask for the full cost, including books, fees, travel, and a computer.
  4. Ask your career counselor about other help, such as WIOA. WIOA is a federal program that can help some people pay for job training. Ask before you enroll.

Employer help with school may come with a promise to keep working there. Read the terms first.

5Training and Employer Requirements

No state license is needed to do payroll. Requirements depend on the employer. Ask what you need to start.

  • Comfort with spreadsheets and numbers.
  • Experience with payroll or bookkeeping software, or willingness to learn it.
  • A background check for many jobs.
  • Strict care with private pay and tax information.
  • Willingness to keep learning as tax and wage rules change.

Before you begin: Ask who checks your work at first and who you go to with a tax or rule question.

Ask about written instructions, an accessible workstation, extra practice time, or other support that helps you learn and do the work well.

6Pay and Job Outlook

North Dakota payroll clerk pay — May 2025:

  • Middle pay: $29.13 an hour ($60,600 a year). Half of workers earned more and half earned less.
  • Half of workers earned between $25.75 and $31.81 an hour.
  • 1 in 10 earned less than $22.54 an hour, and 1 in 10 earned more than $37.28.
  • These numbers show what workers earned. They are not a promise of what a new worker will start at.
  • North Dakota had about 300 of these jobs in 2024. The state projects about 280 in 2034, a change of -9%. That works out to about 30 openings a year, mostly from people who retire or change jobs.

Where people work: Hospitals, manufacturers, school districts, government offices, larger businesses, and payroll service companies.

Work setting: An office desk with a computer. Some jobs can be partly remote once you are trained.

Compare an offer: Ask about base pay, hours, benefits, paid time off, training, and help paying for a certification.

7Where This Can Lead

  • Payroll specialist: Handle more complex payroll, such as several states or many workers.
  • Payroll manager: Lead payroll for a larger employer.
  • Human resources: Payroll knowledge is useful in benefits and human resources jobs.
  • Accounting: More education can lead to accounting work.

Payroll companies also hire people to run payroll for many smaller businesses.

8The Real Talk

Things you might like:

  • Clear rules and a clear right answer.
  • Knowing that people count on your work.
  • Pay that is higher than many entry-level office jobs.
  • Skills that connect to human resources and accounting.

Things to ask about:

  • Firm deadlines every pay period.
  • Workers who are upset when a paycheck looks wrong.
  • Rules that change and need to be learned.
  • Very few jobs with this exact title in North Dakota.

North Dakota has only about 300 payroll clerk jobs and about 30 openings a year. Payroll skills are still useful, because many bookkeepers, office managers, and human resources workers run payroll as part of their job.

A question for yourself: Do you like careful number work where getting it right helps people directly?
