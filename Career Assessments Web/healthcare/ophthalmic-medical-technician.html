Ophthalmic Medical Technician Career Assessment

Eye care specialist

Section 1 of 11

1Welcome

Ophthalmic medical technicians work in eye care clinics and practices, helping diagnose vision problems and assisting with eye care procedures.

You'll explore what this patient-focused, detail-oriented career is really like, and whether it's right for you.

This takes about 15 minutes.

2What is an Ophthalmic Medical Technician?

An ophthalmic medical technician (OMT) works in eye care settings — private practices, clinics, or hospitals — assisting optometrists and ophthalmologists with vision testing, eye exams, and patient care.

Not a Technician in the Equipment Sense: You're primarily patient-focused, not equipment-focused. You're part patient care provider, part assistant, part technician.

Your Responsibilities:

  • Perform preliminary eye exams and vision screening
  • Operate specialized eye testing equipment (phoropter, tonometer, autorefractor)
  • Take patient histories and record information
  • Assist during eye exams and minor procedures
  • Prepare patients for testing
  • Educate patients about eye health and care
  • Handle patient scheduling and paperwork
  • Maintain equipment and ensure cleanliness
  • Manage diagnostic contact lenses
  • Keep patient records organized and confidential

You're the person patients interact with most. Calm, reassuring demeanor matters.

3A Day in the Life

8:00 AM — Arrive early. Check equipment. Make sure tonometer is calibrated, phoropter is clean, and testing rooms are ready.

8:15 AM — Review schedule for today. 15 appointments. Varies: routine exams, contacts, pre-surgical, pediatric cases.

8:30 AM — First patient arrives: 45-year-old needing routine exam. Greet warmly. Get history. Take visual acuity. Run automated refraction.

8:50 AM — Dr. Lopez comes in. You present findings. Assist with tonometry. Help with contact lens fitting. Patient pleased.

9:10 AM — Next patient: child with vision concerns. Gentle approach. Explain each step. Pediatric patients need patience.

9:40 AM — Third patient: pre-surgical evaluation (cataract removal). Extensive testing. Lots of measurements. Precise documentation.

10:30 AM — Phone time. Answer patient questions. Schedule follow-ups. Handle insurance inquiries.

11:00 AM — Contact lens fitting assistance. Demonstration. Proper insertion/removal training for patient.

12:00 PM — Lunch (in office, covering phone)

1:00 PM – 5:00 PM — Afternoon appointments. More exams. More fittings. Paperwork between patients. Equipment maintenance.

5:00 PM — Clean up. Sterilize equipment. File paperwork. Prepare for tomorrow.

Fast-paced, patient-focused, detail-oriented work.

4Training in North Dakota

No ND License Required, but many employers prefer certification.

Training Pathways:

  • Accredited Program: Certificate or degree program. You can take the COA exam right after you graduate. None in ND; costs vary by school.
  • On-the-Job Training: Some eye practices hire and train. You take a home-study course and work 1,000 hours under an ophthalmologist before the COA exam.

In North Dakota: There are no accredited ophthalmic programs in ND right now. The closest is Alexandria Technical & Community College (Alexandria, MN). Without a local program, you can train on the job in an ophthalmologist's office and take an approved home-study course to qualify for certification.

Real example: In August 2026, Sanford Health in Fargo posted an entry-level Associate Ophthalmic Assistant job. It required only Basic Life Support (CPR) certification. A high school diploma was preferred, and Sanford provides the training. Pay: $16.50–$26.50/hour.

Prerequisites: High school diploma/GED. Basic science and math helpful. Strong communication skills essential.

Key Skills Taught: Vision testing, patient communication, equipment operation, basic eye anatomy, patient record management.

5Certification & Licensing

COA (Certified Ophthalmic Assistant) — The entry-level certification. Offered by IJCAHPO (International Joint Commission on Allied Health Personnel in Ophthalmology). Higher levels are COT and COMT (Certified Ophthalmic Medical Technologist).

Requirements (COA, work route):

  • High school diploma/GED
  • Finish an approved home-study course (such as JCAT or AAO) and work 1,000 hours under an ophthalmologist (within 12 months)
  • Pass the COA exam (graduates of an accredited program can test right away)

The Exam: Written test. 200 multiple-choice questions. 3 hours. Covers eye anatomy, testing procedures, equipment, patient care, safety.

Cost: $300 ($150 when you apply, $150 after you pass)

Moving Up: COT and COMT take more experience or schooling and add hands-on skills tests.

Continuing Education: Renew every 3 years with CE credits (18 for COA). Keeps you current.

6Salary & Job Outlook

North Dakota Ophthalmic Medical Technician Salary (BLS, May 2025):

  • Lower end (bottom 10%): about $19/hour ($39,110/year)
  • Median (middle): $22.21/hour ($46,190/year)
  • Top earners (top 10%): about $29/hour ($59,920/year)

Good Pay for Education Level. Short training path, decent earning potential.

Benefits: Health insurance, retirement, paid time off, CE reimbursement.

Job Outlook: Very good. Eye care needs grow with an aging population. BLS projects 21% growth nationally from 2025 to 2035, one of the fastest-growing jobs.

Work Environment: Mostly Monday-Friday, office hours. Some Saturday clinics. Indoor, climate-controlled work. Low physical demands.

Portability: Skills transfer anywhere. Eye practices exist in every city.

7Where This Leads

Path 1: Specialize — Contact lenses, glaucoma, pediatric care. Build deeper expertise.

Path 2: Management — Run an eye clinic. Oversee staff. Handle scheduling and budgets.

Path 3: Education — Teach ophthalmic technician programs.

Path 4: Sales — Sell eyeglasses, contacts, or eye care equipment. Commission-based income.

Path 5: Optometry School — Use your experience as background for optometry school. The OD program takes 4 years, usually after a bachelor's degree.

Path 6: Orthoptics — Specialize in eye alignment and eye movement problems. Needs a bachelor's degree plus a 2-year orthoptic program and board exam.

Path 7: Other Healthcare — Transition to nursing, phlebotomy, or other patient-care roles. Your healthcare foundation transfers.

8The Real Talk

What's Great:

  • Quick entry to work (6 months - 2 years)
  • Patient interaction and meaningful help
  • Mostly clinic and office settings
  • Regular hours, work-life balance
  • Skills are portable nationwide
  • Upward career mobility options
  • Good pay for education level
  • Low physical demands

What's Hard:

  • Repetitive tasks day after day
  • Fast-paced clinic schedules
  • Difficult patients (anxious, impatient, complaining)
  • Insurance frustrations (denials, coverage questions)
  • Dealing with vision loss and medical issues
  • Equipment can be expensive if broken
  • Standing and computer work combined
  • Limited opportunities to advance without additional training

Be Honest: Do you like patient interaction? Can you stay positive and calm all day? Are you detail-oriented for records and measurements? Can you handle repetitive work?
