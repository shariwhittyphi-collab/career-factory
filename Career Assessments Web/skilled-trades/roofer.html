Roofer Career Assessment

Building and repairing the roofs that keep weather out

Section 1 of 11

1Welcome

Hail, wind, and snow are hard on roofs. Roofers build and repair them so homes and businesses stay dry.

This is physical work at heights, usually outside. Explore what it feels like before deciding whether it interests you.

Go at your pace. The short questions at the end are for reflection. There are no right or wrong answers.

2What is a Roofer?

You install, repair, and replace roofs on houses and other buildings.

  • Tear off old roofing and clear the work area.
  • Lay underlayment and waterproofing.
  • Install shingles, metal, or flat-roof systems.
  • Cut and fit shingles and flashing around chimneys and vents.
  • Inspect roofs for damage.
  • Clean up nails and debris.

The real picture: Work depends on the season and the weather. After a hailstorm, crews can be very busy.

3A Day in the Life

One possible day: Every crew works differently.

  • Early morning: Set up ladders, anchors, and harnesses before the heat.
  • Morning: Tear off old shingles and load them into a dumpster.
  • Midday: Lay new underlayment and flashing.
  • Afternoon: Nail down new shingles row by row.
  • Before leaving: Clean up nails with a magnet and cover any open areas.

Rain or high wind can stop work for the day.

4How You Learn the Job

Most roofers learn on the job. Many employers hire people with a high school diploma or GED, and some do not require one.

  • On-the-job training: An experienced roofer teaches tools, materials, and safe work at heights.
  • Apprenticeship: Roofer apprenticeships pay you while you learn.
  • Fall protection: Training in harnesses, anchors, and ladder safety is part of the job.

Paying for training: In an apprenticeship you are paid while you learn, and pay usually rises as you complete hours. For classes or a certificate, start with financial aid, scholarships, and grants. Ask employers whether they help pay. Ask your career counselor about other help, such as WIOA, a federal program that can help some people pay for job training. Ask before you enroll.

5Training and Employer Requirements

North Dakota does not license individual roofers. Roofing contractors need a state contractor license for jobs over $4,000, with proof of insurance and workers’ compensation coverage. State law also bars roofing contractors from promising to cover a customer’s insurance deductible.

  • Comfort working at heights.
  • Ability to lift and carry heavy bundles, or ask about the physical demands.
  • A driver’s license and a reliable ride to job sites.
  • Fall-protection training, which employers should provide.

Before you begin: Ask how the crew protects workers from falls.

Ask about written instructions, a buddy on your first jobs, extra practice time, or other support that helps you learn and work safely.

6Pay and Job Outlook

North Dakota roofer pay — May 2025:

  • Middle pay: $28.72 an hour ($59,740 a year). Half of workers earned more and half earned less.
  • Half of workers earned between $23.08 and $34.68 an hour.
  • 1 in 10 earned less than $22.14 an hour, and 1 in 10 earned more than $42.00.
  • These numbers show what workers earned. They are not a promise of what a new worker will start at.
  • North Dakota had about 350 of these jobs in 2024. The state projects about 380 in 2034, a change of +9%. That works out to about 30 openings a year, mostly from people who retire or change jobs.

Where people work: Roofing contractors, general contractors, and self-employment.

Work setting: Outdoors on roofs, in heat and wind. Work is seasonal, with long days after storms.

Compare an offer: Ask about base pay, season length, winter work, overtime, benefits, and safety training.

7Where This Can Lead

  • Experienced roofer: Work on metal, flat, or specialty roofs.
  • Crew leader or foreman: Run a crew.
  • Estimator or inspector: Measure roofs and plan jobs.
  • Your own business: With a contractor license and insurance.

Ask roofers near you which paths are open.

8The Real Talk

Things you might like:

  • Outdoor, active work with a crew.
  • Finishing a whole roof in a few days.
  • Starting without a degree.
  • Pay that rises with skill.

Things to ask about:

  • Heights and the risk of falls.
  • Hot sun and hard physical work.
  • Seasonal work and winter layoffs.
  • Busy stretches after storms.

North Dakota projects about 9% growth in these jobs from 2024 to 2034, with about 30 openings a year.

A question for yourself: Do heights and hard outdoor work sound like something you would enjoy?
