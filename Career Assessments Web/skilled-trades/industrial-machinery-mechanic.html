Industrial Machinery Mechanic Career Assessment

Keeping factory and plant machines running

Section 1 of 11

1Welcome

Industrial machinery mechanics (often called maintenance technicians or millwrights) keep factory and plant machines running.

You will explore what this hands-on, problem-solving trade is really like and whether it fits you.

This takes about 15 minutes.

2What is a Industrial Machinery Mechanic?

An industrial machinery mechanic maintains and repairs production equipment: conveyors, pumps, motors, compressors, robots, and packaging machines. When a line stops, they find the problem fast and get it running again.

The Reality: When a production line is down, the whole plant waits on you. Plants often run around the clock, so shift work and on-call are common.

Your Responsibilities:

  • Inspect and maintain production machines
  • Diagnose mechanical, electrical, and hydraulic problems
  • Repair or replace parts like bearings, belts, and gears
  • Read blueprints and manuals
  • Work with computer-controlled machines and sensors
  • Do preventive maintenance
  • Install new equipment
  • Follow safety lockout rules

You keep the plant running.

3A Day in the Life

6:00 AM — Shift starts at a food processing plant. Review the maintenance list.

6:30 AM — A conveyor stops. You lock it out safely, find a worn bearing, and replace it.

8:30 AM — Preventive maintenance: grease, check belts, and test sensors.

11:00 AM — A packaging machine is jamming. You adjust the timing.

12:00 PM — Lunch.

1:00 PM — Help install a new pump.

3:00 PM — Log repairs and order parts.

6:00 PM — Shift ends. You are on call this weekend.

Fast problem solving with a mix of mechanical, electrical, and computer skills.

4Training in North Dakota

No state license is required. Most mechanics complete a 1–2 year technical program or learn on the job.

Training Pathways:

  • Associate degree or certificate (1–2 years): Mechanical maintenance, robotics, or automation programs.
  • On-the-job training: Many plants train helpers and operators to become mechanics.
  • Military: Mechanical experience transfers well.

Where to Train in North Dakota:

  • Bismarck State College (Bismarck) — Mechanical Maintenance Technology, AAS (turbines, compressors, pumps, valves)
  • Bismarck State College — Instrumentation and Control Technology (online or on campus) and Industrial Automation & Robotics, AAS
  • ND State College of Science (Wahpeton) — Robotics, Automation & Mechatronics Technology, AAS (72 credits)
  • ND State College of Science — Precision Machining Technology (related)

Before You Start: High school diploma or GED. Mechanical ability, basic math, and reading technical manuals.

Tip: Start with financial aid, scholarships, and any help an employer offers — that is where the money starts. After that, ask a career counselor or Job Service North Dakota whether WIOA or other programs can help with what is left over, like training, tools, work boots, or exam fees. What can be paid for depends on the program and on your own situation, so ask before you sign up rather than after. Registered apprenticeships let you earn a paycheck while you learn — see jobsnd.com/job-seeker/apprenticeships for ND programs.

5Certification & Licensing

No license needed. Certifications are optional but can help you stand out.

Certifications Employers Look For:

  • Maintenance and reliability certifications (for example, SMRP CMRT)
  • Equipment maker and PLC training
  • Safety training (lockout/tagout, OSHA)

The Exams: Varies by certification.

Cost: Varies. Many employers pay for training.

Keeping It Current: Varies by certification.

6Salary & Job Outlook

North Dakota Industrial Machinery Mechanic Pay (May 2025):

  • Starting out (bottom 10–25%): $24.62–$29.04/hour ($51,210–$60,400/year)
  • Middle (median — half earn more, half earn less): $31.72/hour ($65,990/year)
  • Experienced (top 25–10%): $38.06–$50.17/hour ($79,170–$104,350/year)

What that means: ND pay is close to the national median. Power plant and refinery jobs often pay at the top of the range.

Jobs in North Dakota: About 2,840 people do this work in ND today. By 2034, that should be about 3,320 (about 17% growth). Expect about 300 job openings every year in ND from growth and people retiring or changing jobs. Across the U.S., the change is about 16% growth — much faster than average.

Outlook: Excellent. About 300 openings a year in ND and fast growth, as plants add more automated equipment.

Who Hires in ND: Food processing and ag equipment manufacturers, power plants, refineries, ethanol plants, and coal mines.

Work Setting: Inside plants. Shift work (days, nights, weekends) and on-call are common.

Physical Demands: Moderate to high. Lifting, climbing, crawling into machines, and working in noisy, hot, or cold plant areas.

Sources: U.S. Bureau of Labor Statistics, Occupational Employment and Wage Statistics, North Dakota, May 2025 (SOC 49-9041); North Dakota and U.S. long-term employment projections 2024–2034, via O*NET OnLine. Numbers are rounded.

7Where This Leads

Path 1: Maintenance Lead — Lead a maintenance team.

Path 2: Automation and Robotics Tech — Specialize in computer-controlled equipment.

Path 3: Millwright — Install and move large machinery.

Path 4: Reliability Technician — Prevent breakdowns before they happen.

Path 5: Maintenance Manager — Run a plant's maintenance department.

Path 6: Instrument Tech — Work on sensors and controls.

8The Real Talk

What's Great:

  • Fast-growing field
  • Many openings in ND
  • Mix of mechanical and tech skills
  • Steady plant jobs with benefits
  • Every day is different
  • Many ways to specialize

What's Hard:

  • Pressure when a line is down
  • Shift work and on-call
  • Noisy, hot, or cold plants
  • Safety risks around machines
  • Must keep learning new technology

Be Honest: Do you like solving problems under pressure? Are you OK with shift work? Do you enjoy both mechanical and tech work?
