Restaurant Cook Career Assessment

The fastest-growing job in this whole area

Section 1 of 11

1Welcome

Restaurant cooks prepare and cook food to order in restaurants, cafes, hotels, and bars.

North Dakota expects about 17% growth in this job by 2034 — the strongest growth of any job in this area — with roughly 770 openings a year. No school is required.

This takes about 15 minutes.

2What is a Restaurant Cook?

You work a station on a line, cooking food to order and sending it out at the right temperature at the right time. That means holding several tickets in your head at once, starting things in the order they will finish rather than the order they came in, and knowing without looking how long everything on your station takes. Most cooks learn this on the job, starting on the easiest station and working up.

The Reality: If nobody cooks, the dining room is just a room with tables in it. That is the honest measure of this job. It is hot, loud, and unforgiving on timing — a ticket that is thirty seconds late is late. Nights, weekends, and holidays are the busy times, which means they are your working times. Burns and knife nicks are routine, not dramatic. The pace during service is genuinely hard, and the quiet stretches before and after it are the only time you get to think.

Your Responsibilities:

  • Cook food to order at a station on the line
  • Read and hold several tickets at once
  • Time dishes so a whole table comes up together
  • Prep your station before service and break it down after
  • Keep food at safe temperatures and log them
  • Rotate stock and check dates
  • Keep your station clean through service, not just at the end
  • Cover another station when someone is down
  • Tell the server when something is going to run out

The skill is timing, and you cannot see it from the dining room — which is exactly why it gets underrated.

3A Day in the Life

2:00 PM — Clock in. Check the prep list and what came in on the delivery.

2:30 PM — Prep your station. Portion, label, stock, and taste.

4:30 PM — Line check. Temperatures logged, everything in reach.

5:00 PM — Service starts. Tickets begin coming in.

6:30 PM — Peak. Eight tickets up, three stations working together.

8:30 PM — It eases off. Restock what you burned through.

9:30 PM — Last tickets. Start breaking the station down.

10:30 PM — Clean, wrap, label, and set tomorrow up.

A long slow build, two hours of flat-out, then a methodical shutdown.

4Training in North Dakota

No school needed. Nearly every cook in North Dakota learned on the job. You start on the simplest station — usually fryer, salads, or pantry — and move up as you prove you can hold the timing. Culinary school exists, but it is not how most people in this state got into kitchens, and it is not required by employers here.

Training Pathways:

  • Start as a dishwasher or prep cook: This is the most common door in. Kitchens promote from the dish pit constantly, because they can already see whether you show up and keep up.
  • Hired directly onto the line: If you have cooked anywhere before, including fast food, say so. Speed and food safety transfer.
  • Two-year culinary programs: North Dakota has them, and they help if you want to run a kitchen eventually. They are optional, not a requirement, and no employer here will turn you away for not having one.

Where to Train in North Dakota:

Before You Start: No diploma or experience required at most kitchens. You need to stand for a long shift, work in heat, and handle knives and hot surfaces safely. Food handler training is usually arranged by the employer.

Tip: Most of these jobs need no school, so the cost of starting is not tuition — it is the things you need in the first month. Non-slip shoes, work clothes or a uniform, a way to get to work, and getting through the two or three weeks before the first paycheck arrives. Ask the employer first: many will supply a uniform, some give a shoe allowance, and some pay weekly rather than every two weeks, which matters a lot at the start. After that, ask a career counselor or Job Service North Dakota whether WIOA or other programs can help with what is left over, like work clothes, boots, tools, or getting to work. What can be paid for depends on the program and on your own situation, so ask before you start rather than after.

5Certification & Licensing

No license is required to cook. You can be hired and working without any certificate. Food safety training is usually provided, and the manager-level certificate becomes relevant only if you move up.

Worth having:

  • Food handler card — short online course, often paid for by the employer
  • ServSafe Food Handler — the more widely recognized version, useful when you change jobs
  • ServSafe Manager — needed for kitchen lead and chef roles; employers usually pay for it
  • Allergen training — increasingly expected, and a genuine safety issue in a kitchen

The Exams: Food handler courses end in a short online multiple-choice test. The Manager certificate is a longer proctored exam.

Cost: Food handler cards are usually under $25 and often employer-paid. Ask before you pay for it yourself.

Keeping It Current: Food handler cards typically last two to three years; ServSafe Manager lasts five.

6Salary & Job Outlook

North Dakota Restaurant Cook Pay (May 2025):

  • Starting out (bottom 10–25%): $13.87–$14.62/hour ($28,850–$30,410/year)
  • Middle (median — half earn more, half earn less): $17.53/hour ($36,470/year)
  • Experienced (top 25–10%): $18.32–$21.95/hour ($38,110–$45,650/year)

What that means: The bottom of this range is a new cook on the easiest station. The top is a lead cook or someone running a kitchen in a busy place. Moving from fast food to a restaurant line is worth about $3 an hour straight away, for work you already partly know how to do.

Jobs in North Dakota: About 4,420 people do this work in ND today. By 2034, that should be about 5,160 (about 17% growth). Expect about 770 job openings every year in ND from growth and people retiring or changing jobs. Across the U.S., the change is growing much faster than average.

Outlook: The strongest growth of any job in this area — about 17% in North Dakota by 2034, with roughly 770 openings a year. Restaurants struggle to find cooks, and that is reflected in the wage.

Who Hires in ND: Restaurants, hotels, bars and taverns, casinos, truck stops, hospitals, nursing homes, schools, and camp kitchens in the oil country.

Work Setting: Indoors, hot, loud, and tight on space. Evenings, weekends, and holidays are the busiest shifts. Some institutional kitchens run days with no nights at all.

Physical Demands: High. Standing the whole shift, lifting stock, hot surfaces and oil, sharp knives, wet floors, and working at speed in heat.

Sources: U.S. Bureau of Labor Statistics, Occupational Employment and Wage Statistics, North Dakota, May 2025 (SOC 35-2014); North Dakota and U.S. long-term employment projections 2024–2034, via O*NET OnLine. Numbers are rounded.

7Where This Leads

Path 1: Prep Cook — Portioning and prepping before service. A common starting point.

Path 2: Line Cook — One station during service. Where most cooks spend years.

Path 3: Lead or Sous Cook — Run the line, set the prep list, train the new people.

Path 4: Kitchen Manager or Chef — Ordering, costing, scheduling, and hiring. Usually salaried.

Path 5: Institutional Cook — Hospitals, schools, and care homes — day shifts, no nights, steadier hours.

Path 6: Food Service Supervisor — Running a kitchen operation rather than cooking in it.

8The Real Talk

What's Great:

  • Fastest-growing job in this area at about 17%
  • Pays about $3 an hour more than fast food
  • No school or diploma required
  • Real skill that you can take to any town in the country
  • Kitchens promote from inside almost without exception
  • Hospital and school kitchens offer day shifts if nights stop working for you

What's Hard:

  • Hot, loud, and physically demanding
  • Nights, weekends, and holidays are the busy shifts
  • Burns and cuts are routine
  • The timing pressure during service is real and does not let up
  • Kitchen culture can be rough, and it varies a lot by employer
  • Standing on a hard floor for ten hours adds up over years

Be Honest: Can you hold several things in your head at once while it is loud and hot? Can you work the nights and weekends other people have off? Can you take criticism quickly in the middle of service and keep going?
