Parts Counter Salesperson Career Assessment

Finding the right part and helping keep customers moving

Section 1 of 11

1Welcome

A farmer needs a part. A mechanic is waiting to finish a repair. The parts counter salesperson helps find the right item and keeps track of the order.

This career can combine customer service, computer work, and hands-on tasks. Explore what it feels like before deciding whether it interests you.

Go at your pace. The short questions at the end are for reflection. There are no right or wrong answers.

2What is a Parts Counter Salesperson?

You help customers and shop workers find replacement parts. You ask which machine or vehicle they have, check a catalog, find the part, and explain price and availability. A farm-equipment dealership is one setting; auto and truck parts stores are others.

  • Listen to the customer and confirm the machine model or part details.
  • Use a computer catalog to match the right item.
  • Check stock, prices, orders, and delivery information.
  • Bring smaller items to the counter and arrange help for larger ones.
  • Record sales, orders, or returns carefully.
  • Keep parts organized and work with the service shop.

The real picture: You do not need to memorize every part. You need to learn how to find accurate information and when to ask for help. Some openings are for beginners; others require experience.

3A Day in the Life

One possible day: The schedule changes with the store and the season.

  • Morning: Check a shipment and put parts in the right bins.
  • Later: A customer brings a worn part. Ask about the machine, check the catalog, and compare the replacement.
  • Midday: Take a phone order and tell the shop when a back-ordered item is expected.
  • Afternoon: Help several customers, handle a return, and check the stock count.
  • Before leaving: Record unfinished orders and pass important details to the next coworker.

Planting, harvest, or shop deadlines can make the counter busy. Some jobs include Saturdays, early hours, or delivery work. Ask about the schedule at the location you are considering.

4How You Learn the Job

A four-year degree is not the usual entry route. Many parts workers learn through employer training. A store may ask for a diploma, related experience, computer skills, or knowledge of equipment. Read the actual job ad.

  • Start with a visit: Observe a parts department and see how workers use the catalog and talk with customers.
  • Student experience: A high school co-op or internship may let you explore parts along with other departments. The local placement and duties need to be confirmed.
  • On-the-job training: Learn the store’s catalog, order system, products, stockroom, and customer-service process from coworkers.
  • Optional education: Business, automotive, agriculture, or technical courses can build useful skills. Compare cost and relevance before choosing a program.

See Student opportunities near home for Gooseneck, RDO, Titan, and Butler routes. Being in a student program does not guarantee a permanent parts job.

Helpful practice: Read model numbers, compare similar items, use a spreadsheet, practice clear phone messages, and check simple prices and quantities. You can learn these skills gradually.

Paying for training: Start with financial aid, scholarships, grants, and employer help. Ask the school and employer for the full cost, including tools, travel, a computer, and housing. Then ask your career counselor about other help, including WIOA. Ask before you enroll. Employer tuition help may come with a work commitment; read the terms first.

5Training and Employer Requirements

A parts-sales job is different from a licensed mechanic role. Requirements depend on the store and the work assigned. Ask the employer what you need to start.

  • Training in the catalog, sales system, and equipment brands.
  • Safe lifting and moving procedures for the actual stockroom.
  • Extra training and authorization if the role uses powered handling equipment.
  • A driver’s license or other driving requirements if deliveries are part of the job.
  • Product or professional training if the employer uses it; ask whether it is required or optional.

Before you begin: Ask who provides training, whether there is a cost, and how beginners get help. Do not pay for a credential just because it sounds useful.

Ask about written instructions, an accessible workstation, lifting help, extra practice, or other support that helps you learn and work safely.

6Pay and Job Outlook

North Dakota parts-sales pay — May 2025: These figures include parts sellers in several industries, not only farm-equipment dealerships.

  • Middle pay: $23.37 an hour / $48,600 a year. Half earned more and half earned less.
  • Lower 10th–25th percentiles: $16.77–$20.23 an hour. Upper 75th–90th percentiles: $28.52–$33.00 an hour. These are pay distributions, not promises about starting or experienced pay.
  • ND employment is projected from 1,670 in 2024 to 1,750 in 2034: 5% growth and about 190 openings a year, including replacements.

Where people work: Farm-equipment, truck, and auto dealerships; parts stores; and equipment businesses. Gooseneck had a Parts Counter Salesperson opening in Dickinson and a Shop Parts Counterperson opening in Harvey when checked on October 4, 2026.

Work setting: A counter, desk, and stockroom. The role may include standing, walking, reaching, handling parts, and phone or computer work. Ask about actual lifting tasks, equipment, and available support.

Compare an offer: Ask about base pay, commissions or bonuses if any, hours, benefits, training, and delivery tasks. A student placement may have different pay and duties from a full-time job.

7Where This Can Lead

  • Experienced parts worker: Handle more difficult catalog and customer questions.
  • Inventory or ordering: Focus on stock counts, purchasing, or keeping the shop supplied.
  • Parts department leadership: Guide coworkers and manage the department as experience grows.
  • A related direction: Customer service, sales, service scheduling, or equipment repair. Moving into repair may require technical training.

You can enjoy the parts counter as a career without aiming to manage a department. Ask people in the store what paths are actually available.

8The Real Talk

Things you might like:

  • Helping someone get back to work.
  • Learning about equipment without doing every repair yourself.
  • A mix of people, computers, and hands-on tasks.
  • Possible work in a nearby rural dealership or parts store.

Things to ask about:

  • Busy counters, phone calls, and several requests at once.
  • Customers who are frustrated about a breakdown or delay.
  • Similar-looking parts that require careful checking.
  • Standing, lifting, seasonal hours, and delivery duties.

A question for yourself: Do you like helping people while checking details carefully? A short job shadow can help you find out.
