Industrial Production Manager Career Assessment

Running the plant floor — hearing protection, safety boots, and a daily output number

Section 1 of 11

1Welcome

An industrial production manager runs the making of things. You are on a plant floor, in a processing facility, or in a shop, and your job is to get the right amount out the door, on time, at the right quality, without anyone getting hurt.

This is the most hands-on of the office-titled manager jobs and the one with the clearest scoreboard: you hit the number or you did not. This page tells you what that is really like in North Dakota.

This takes about 15 minutes.

2What is a Industrial Production Manager?

An industrial production manager is responsible for what a plant produces. You set the production schedule and you adjust it every time a machine goes down or a material does not arrive. You manage supervisors and operators. You own quality — if the product is out of spec, that is yours. You own safety, and on a plant floor that is not paperwork, it is whether someone goes home with all their fingers. You watch cost per unit, downtime, scrap, and output, and you report on all four. In ND that means sugar and food processing, oil and gas processing, farm equipment and machinery, ethanol and energy plants, and manufacturing shops of every size.

The Reality: You come up from the floor. Almost every industrial production manager was an operator, a technician, a maintenance tech, or a line supervisor first, and the ones who do well are the ones whose crews know they have actually run the equipment. A degree helps, especially at bigger plants, but if you cannot walk the floor and tell what is wrong by sound, the crew will know within a week. The other reality is the environment. This is not a desk job with a plant attached. It is noise, hearing protection, steel-toed boots, shift work, and a phone that rings at 3 AM when the line goes down. Many plants run around the clock, and the plant does not care that it is Sunday.

Your Responsibilities:

  • Set the production schedule and rebuild it every time something goes wrong
  • Supervise shift supervisors, operators, and support staff
  • Own product quality and deal with it when the product is out of spec
  • Run the safety program and enforce it on the floor, every shift
  • Track output, downtime, scrap, and cost per unit, and explain all four
  • Work with maintenance to schedule repairs around production
  • Coordinate with purchasing and shipping so materials arrive and product leaves
  • Lead the fix when a machine goes down and the line stops

You are the reason the line runs and the reason everyone goes home whole.

3A Day in the Life

5:45 AM — Shift change. Get the handoff from night shift: the number two line ran rough at 2 AM.

6:15 AM — Walk the floor with hearing protection on. Listen. Talk to four operators.

7:30 AM — Production meeting. Yesterday’s output missed by 6%. Say why, and say the plan.

9:00 AM — A bearing fails on the number two line. Everything stops. Get maintenance and reroute the schedule.

11:00 AM — Quality flags a batch out of spec. Hold it, find the cause, decide what happens to it.

12:30 PM — Eat fast. Review the downtime and scrap numbers.

2:00 PM — Safety walk with the supervisor. Two things need fixing before the next shift.

4:00 PM — Brief the incoming shift supervisor, then go home. Your phone is on all night.

Loud, fast, measured by a number, and never fully off the clock.

4Training in North Dakota

Almost everyone comes up from the floor. A degree speeds it up at bigger plants. There is no license for this job. What earns it is years on production equipment plus proof you can run a shift. At larger plants an engineering or technical degree moves you faster and is sometimes required. In North Dakota there are strong two-year technical programs that put you on the floor quickly and a four-year engineering road for the bigger plants.

Training Pathways:

  • Operator to supervisor to manager (6 to 12 years): Run the equipment, become a lead, then a shift supervisor, then production manager. This is the most common road in ND plants and the one that earns the most credibility on the floor. Take the lead job when it is offered even if the pay bump is small.
  • Two-year technical degree, then up (2 years plus 5 to 8 years): An AAS in process technology, mechatronics, robotics and automation, or precision machining gets you onto a plant floor with skills that pay immediately, and moves you toward supervision sooner than starting green.
  • Engineering degree (4 years plus 3 to 6 years): A bachelor’s in industrial, manufacturing, or mechanical engineering is the fast road at large plants. NDSU offers Industrial Engineering and Management, Manufacturing Engineering, and Robotics and Automation, and has accelerated pathways that pair a bachelor’s with an MBA or a Master of Engineering in about five years.

Where to Train in North Dakota:

  • North Dakota State University (Fargo) — Industrial Engineering and Management, Manufacturing Engineering, and Robotics and Automation majors; graduate programs in Industrial and Manufacturing Engineering and in Project Management; accelerated B.S.-plus-MBA and B.S.-plus-Master of Engineering pathways in about five years (ndsu.edu/ime)
  • ND State College of Science (Wahpeton and Fargo) — Robotics, Automation and Mechatronics Technology, Precision Machining Technology, Welding Technology, Electrical Technology, and Diesel Technology; these are the AAS programs that put you on a plant floor (catalog.ndscs.edu)
  • ND State College of Science — Business Management AAS, Business Technology Management option: a third year for people who already hold a two-year technical degree and want the supervision and business side (catalog.ndscs.edu/business-management/associate-in-applied-science/business-management-business-technology-management)
  • Bismarck State College (Bismarck) — Power Process Technology, Electric Power Technology, Petroleum Production Technology, Nuclear Power Technology, and Energy Services & Renewable Technician, plus a Bachelor of Applied Science in Mechatronics Engineering Technology and one in Operations Management (bismarckstate.edu/academics/programs and catalog.bismarckstate.edu/catalog/degrees/bas)
  • Bismarck State College (Bismarck) — Management AAS and Leadership certificate for the supervision side, online or on campus (catalog.bismarckstate.edu/catalog/degrees/career-technical-education/management)
  • University of North Dakota (Grand Forks) — Operations & Supply Chain Management B.B.A. (business.und.edu/academics)
  • North Dakota State University (Fargo) — Supply Chain Management B.S. (ndsu.edu/business/academic-programs/undergraduate)

Before You Start: A high school diploma or GED. Real experience on production equipment. Solid practical math — rates, percentages, tolerances, and yields. The ability to work shifts, including nights and weekends, at least early on. A body that tolerates noise, standing, and a plant floor.

Tip: Start with financial aid, scholarships, and any help an employer offers — that is where the money starts. After that, ask a career counselor or Job Service North Dakota whether WIOA or other programs can help with what is left over, like training, tools, work boots, or exam fees. What can be paid for depends on the program and on your own situation, so ask before you sign up rather than after. Registered apprenticeships let you earn a paycheck while you learn — see jobsnd.com/job-seeker/apprenticeships for ND programs.

5Certification & Licensing

No license, but CPIM and CQE are the two credentials plant employers recognize. Nothing is legally required. Two credentials genuinely help, and they point at different halves of the job: CPIM for planning and inventory, CQE for quality. Safety credentials sit underneath both, because on a plant floor safety is not a side topic.

Credentials That Carry Weight in a Plant:

  • CPIM (Certified in Planning and Inventory Management), ASCM — the planning and inventory credential. ASCM says you must pass two exams, CPIM Part 1 and CPIM Part 2, within three years of each other, and submit a certification maintenance application every five years to keep it (ascm.org)
  • CPIM cost — ASCM publishes member-with-certification-upgrade pricing of $435 for the Part 1 self-study prep and $545 for Part 2, exam vouchers at $545 per part, and a bundle of both parts’ learning systems, exams, and a year of membership at $1,860. ASCM does not show non-member pricing on that page, so ask ASCM for the non-member rate before you budget (ascm.org)
  • CQE (Certified Quality Engineer), ASQ — the quality credential. ASQ requires eight years of on-the-job experience in the CQE body of knowledge, with three of those years in a decision-making position. Education waives part of it: a technical or trade school diploma waives one year, an associate degree two years, a bachelor’s four years, and a master’s or doctorate five years (asq.org/cert/quality-engineer)
  • CQE cost — ASQ publishes a $550 initial exam fee and a $350 retake fee, with a $100 discount on the initial exam for ASQ members, bringing it to $450 (asq.org)
  • Safety credentials (OSHA 30 and above) — short and inexpensive, and many ND plants expect a production manager to hold one before putting them over a crew

The Exams: CPIM: two exams, Part 1 and Part 2, passed within three years of each other. CQE: one exam, with an eight-year experience requirement that education partly waives.

Cost: CPIM: about $435 to $545 per part for prep, $545 per exam voucher, or $1,860 for the full bundle at ASCM member-with-upgrade pricing; ask ASCM for non-member rates. CQE: $550 initial, $450 for ASQ members, $350 to retake. Many ND plants pay for these outright because the plant gets the benefit. Ask your employer first.

Keeping It Current: ASCM requires a CPIM certification maintenance application every five years. ASQ requires recertification for the CQE on its own published cycle — ask ASQ for the current requirement and fee rather than assuming.

6Salary & Job Outlook

North Dakota Industrial Production Manager Pay (May 2025):

  • Starting out (bottom 10–25%): $36.62–$46.81/hour ($76,180–$97,360/year)
  • Middle (median — half earn more, half earn less): $55.80/hour ($116,050/year)
  • Experienced (top 25–10%): $70.54–$87.27/hour ($146,710–$181,530/year)

What that means: The middle North Dakota industrial production manager earns about $116,050 a year, against a national middle of about $126,060 — a gap of about $10,000, which is on the smaller side for this series. The ND 25th percentile of about $97,360 is very close to the national $98,160, and the ND 10th of about $76,180 is just under the national $78,000. Read it this way: at the entry and lower-middle end, North Dakota pays about what the country pays for this job. The distance opens at the top, where the ND 90th percentile of about $181,530 trails the national $205,520. Shift differential, overtime, and production bonuses are common in ND plants and are not fully captured in these figures, so the real take-home at a plant running around the clock is often higher than the table suggests. Ask about bonus structure specifically — at some plants it is a large part of the pay.

Jobs in North Dakota: About 460 people do this work in ND today. By 2034, that should be about 500 (about 7% growth). Expect about 40 job openings every year in ND from growth and people retiring or changing jobs. Across the U.S., the change is about 2% growth — slower than average.

Outlook: Good, and notably better in North Dakota than in the country. O*NET shows ND going from about 460 industrial production managers in 2024 to about 500 in 2034, roughly 7% growth, with about 40 openings a year. Nationally this job grows only about 2%, which O*NET flags as slower than average, with 17,100 openings a year. So North Dakota is growing this job more than three times as fast as the nation is, at a time when American manufacturing employment overall is flat. The reason is what North Dakota makes: food and sugar processing, ethanol and energy, agricultural equipment, and oil and gas processing, all of which are anchored here and not going elsewhere. Forty openings a year is modest, but it is more than facilities, administrative services, HR, or training managers get, and it is real work that cannot be done remotely or offshore.

Who Hires in ND: Food and sugar processing plants, ethanol and biofuel plants, oil and gas processing and refining, agricultural equipment and machinery manufacturers, metal fabrication shops, building products manufacturers, and packaging plants. The Red River Valley, the Fargo and Grand Forks areas, and the western oil country are where most of these jobs are.

Work Setting: A plant floor and the office attached to it. Noise, hearing protection, safety glasses, steel-toed boots, and whatever else the site requires. Many plants run three shifts or around the clock, so expect shift work early in your career and a phone that rings at night later on. Hot in summer near the process, cold near the loading doors in winter.

Physical Demands: Moderate to high. Standing and walking the floor for hours, climbing stairs and platforms, and working around loud machinery and moving equipment. You are not doing the heaviest lifting, but you are in the environment all day and you need hearing protection to mean it. Shift work is hard on sleep and it is fair to count that as a physical demand.

Sources: U.S. Bureau of Labor Statistics, Occupational Employment and Wage Statistics, North Dakota, May 2025 (SOC 11-3051); North Dakota and U.S. long-term employment projections 2024–2034, via O*NET OnLine. Numbers are rounded.

7Where This Leads

Path 1: Operator or Technician — Learn the equipment with your hands. This is where credibility starts.

Path 2: Lead Operator — Run a line and the people on it for a shift.

Path 3: Shift Supervisor — Own a whole shift, its output, and its safety.

Path 4: Production Manager — Own the plant floor, the schedule, and the numbers.

Path 5: Plant Manager — Own the whole site, including maintenance, quality, and shipping.

Path 6: Operations or Continuous Improvement Director — Run several plants, or make all of them better.

8The Real Talk

What's Great:

  • Your hands-on experience is the qualification — floor credibility beats a resume here
  • ND grows this job more than three times as fast as the nation does
  • Middle pay of about $116,050 with a top tenth near $181,530
  • Shift differential, overtime, and production bonuses often add to the published pay
  • Clear scoreboard — you know every day whether you did the job
  • The work cannot be sent offshore or done from a laptop somewhere else

What's Hard:

  • Noise, shift work, and a plant floor environment every single day
  • The 3 AM call when the line goes down is part of the job, not an exception
  • You own safety, and that means you own it when someone gets hurt
  • ND pay trails the country by about $10,000 at the middle and by about $24,000 at the top
  • About 40 openings a year statewide, and most of them are in the east or the oil country
  • Six to twelve years on the floor before the manager job, in most cases

Be Honest: Can you work in noise, in safety gear, and on shifts? Will you get out of bed at 3 AM and drive to the plant when the line stops? Can you shut a line down over a safety problem when shutting it down costs real money and somebody is going to be angry about it? And can you spend years on the floor first, because that is the only way the crew will respect you when you get the title?
