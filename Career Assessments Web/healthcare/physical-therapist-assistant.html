Physical Therapist Assistant Career Assessment

Rehabilitation healthcare specialist

Section 1 of 11

1Welcome

Physical Therapist Assistants (PTAs) help people recover from injuries, manage chronic pain, and regain mobility. You'll work with patients who've had strokes, back injuries, joint surgeries, or neurological conditions.

It's rewarding work where you see real progress and improvement. Patients get better—and you're part of that recovery.

This takes about 15 minutes.

2What is a Physical Therapist Assistant?

A Physical Therapist Assistant (PTA) works under the supervision of a licensed Physical Therapist (PT). You implement treatment plans created by the PT, work directly with patients, and report progress.

The Reality: You're a hands-on healthcare provider. You spend time with patients, guide them through exercises, adjust treatments based on how they respond, and celebrate their wins. It's people-facing work that matters.

Your Responsibilities:

  • Implement PT-designed treatment plans
  • Guide patients through therapeutic exercises
  • Perform hands-on techniques (massage, stretching, mobilization)
  • Use modalities (heat, ice, ultrasound, electrical stimulation)
  • Monitor patient response and modify exercises safely
  • Help patients regain mobility and function
  • Document patient progress in medical records
  • Educate patients on home exercise programs
  • Work with stroke, orthopedic, neurological patients
  • Provide encouragement and motivation during recovery

Therapists lead. You execute and adjust. It's partnership with the PT and direct connection with patients.

3A Day in the Life

7:00 AM — Arrive at clinic. Review patient schedule. Check PT treatment plans.

8:00 AM — First patient: post-knee surgery. 3 weeks post-op. Implement PT's plan. Range of motion exercises. Ice therapy. Teach home program.

8:45 AM — Second patient: stroke patient, partial paralysis. Help with gait training (walking). Teach compensation strategies. Encourage effort.

9:30 AM — Third patient: chronic back pain. Manual therapy (massage, mobilization). Core strengthening exercises. Pain management techniques.

10:15 AM — Documentation. Update charts. Note patient progress.

10:45 AM — Fourth patient: post-hip fracture. Functional training (sit to stand, stairs, walking). Safety priority. Slow progress but real improvement.

11:30 AM — Fifth patient: shoulder injury. Range of motion work. Strengthening exercises. Rotator cuff protocol.

12:00 PM — Lunch break

1:00 PM – 4:00 PM — Afternoon schedule. More patients. More exercises. More progress. PT checks in periodically. You do the hands-on work.

4:30 PM — End of day. Clean equipment. Chart final notes. Organize tomorrow's schedule.

Direct patient care. Real physical progress. Measurable results.

4Training in North Dakota

CAPTE Accreditation Required. North Dakota requires PT Assistant licensure. You must graduate from a CAPTE-accredited program.

Training Pathway:

  • PTA Program (2 years): Associate degree. Classroom + lab + clinical internships. Cost varies by school. Check with the program. Community and technical colleges.

In North Dakota: There is no accredited PTA program in North Dakota right now. The closest is Northland Community & Technical College in East Grand Forks, MN (across the river from Grand Forks). It offers a CAPTE-accredited AAS degree. Other programs are in Minnesota and South Dakota. Two-year associate degree minimum.

Prerequisites: High school diploma/GED. Anatomy/physiology coursework preferred. Some programs require pre-requisites.

Clinical Internships: At least 520 hours in clinical settings, including at least 12 weeks full-time. Real patients. Real therapy. Supervised practice.

Curriculum: Anatomy, physiology, kinesiology, therapeutic exercise, manual therapy, modalities, pathology, medical conditions, documentation, ethics, patient interaction.

5Certification & Licensing

NPTE-PTA (National Physical Therapy Exam for PTAs). Required for ND licensure. You must pass this exam.

North Dakota Requirements:

  • Graduate from CAPTE-accredited PTA program
  • Pass the NPTE-PTA exam
  • Pass the ND jurisprudence (law) exam
  • Apply for ND PTA license from the ND Board of Physical Therapy
  • Maintain license with continuing education

The Exam: 180 questions. Multiple choice. 4 hours. Covers patient care, therapeutic exercises, modalities, anatomy, ethics, documentation, safety.

Cost: $485 exam fee plus about $92 testing center fee. ND license application is $200 (plus background check).

Pass Rate: Northland (East Grand Forks) reports 88% passing on the first try (2024-2025). Ask any program you are considering for its pass rate.

Continuing Education: Renew your ND license every year. You need 25 continuing competence units every 2 years.

Reciprocity: Your exam score can be used in other states, but each state issues its own license. ND is in the PT Compact, so you can apply for privileges to work in other member states.

6Salary & Job Outlook

North Dakota PTA Salary (BLS, May 2025):

  • Lower-paid workers (bottom 25%): about $24.54/hour ($51,030/year) or less
  • Median (middle): $30.54/hour ($63,520/year)
  • Top-paid workers (top 10%): about $37.31/hour ($77,600/year) or more

Benefits: Health insurance, retirement, paid time off, continuing education support. Some facilities offer tuition reimbursement.

Job Outlook: Excellent. Aging population. Increased rehabilitation needs. BLS projects PTA jobs to grow 23% nationally from 2025 to 2035.

Work Environment: Outpatient clinics, hospitals, nursing homes, rehabilitation centers, sports medicine, home health. Mostly daytime hours. Some weekend availability.

Physical Demands: Moderate-high. Standing much of the day. Lifting and moving patients, often with help or equipment. Repetitive hand movements. Bending, squatting.

Growth Potential: Specialize (sports, neuro, pediatrics). Supervisor/manager role. Teach. Work in a private practice clinic.

7Where This Leads

Path 1: Specialization — Sports PT, neurological PT, pediatric PT, orthopedic PT. Advanced certifications available.

Path 2: Advancement — Senior PTA, clinical supervisor, therapy manager, director of rehabilitation services.

Path 3: Different Setting — Move from outpatient clinic to hospital, home health, nursing home, sports team, or military.

Path 4: Education — Teach PTA students. Clinical instructor. Program development.

Path 5: Physical Therapist — Some PTAs pursue a PT degree (DPT). You need about 3-4 years of college first (most programs want a bachelor's degree), then a 3-year DPT. In ND: UND (Grand Forks) and University of Mary (Bismarck). ND physical therapists earn a median of about $85,330/year (BLS, May 2025).

Path 6: Hybrid Role — Combine PTA work with fitness coaching, personal training, wellness, or business ownership.

Path 7: Home Health — Flexible scheduling. One-on-one patient care in home settings.

8The Real Talk

What's Great:

  • Visible patient progress and improvement
  • Hands-on, rewarding work
  • Strong job security and growth
  • Good pay for education level
  • Normal work hours (mostly daytime)
  • Physical activity (keeps you moving)
  • Positive patient relationships
  • Team-based environment
  • Multiple career paths
  • Work-life balance possible

What's Hard:

  • Physical demands on your own body (back strain, repetitive injury risk)
  • Patient pain and frustration (emotional toll)
  • Slow recovery progress (some patients plateau)
  • Difficult patients (non-compliant, demanding)
  • Documentation requirements
  • Insurance authorization denials
  • Standing for long hours
  • Occasional evening/weekend hours depending on facility
  • Lifting and transferring patients
  • Responsibility (patient safety depends on you)

Be Honest: Are you patient and encouraging? Can you handle people's pain and frustration? Can you stand and do manual work for 8 hours? Are you detail-oriented? Do you like seeing patients improve?
