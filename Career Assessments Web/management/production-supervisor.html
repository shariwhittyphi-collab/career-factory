Production Supervisor Career Assessment

Leading the people on a factory or plant floor to work safely and well

Section 1 of 11

1Welcome

When a plant runs well, someone is making sure the right people are on the right jobs, safely. That person is the production supervisor.

This job mixes leadership, problem solving, and the plant floor. Explore what it feels like before deciding whether it interests you.

Go at your pace. The short questions at the end are for reflection. There are no right or wrong answers.

2What is a Production Supervisor?

You lead a team of production workers in a factory or plant. You plan the work, keep people safe, and solve problems during the shift.

  • Plan schedules and assign jobs to meet production goals.
  • Enforce safety and cleanliness rules.
  • Check products and equipment for problems.
  • Keep records of hours, output, and quality.
  • Train new workers and give feedback.
  • Work with maintenance, shipping, and other departments.

The real picture: You are responsible for both people and numbers. Some days are calm; others bring breakdowns and short staffing.

3A Day in the Life

One possible day: Every plant runs on its own schedule.

  • Start of shift: Lead a short huddle about safety, goals, and staffing.
  • Morning: Walk the floor and check that each line is running.
  • Midday: Work with maintenance to fix a machine that went down.
  • Afternoon: Coach a new worker and review quality checks.
  • End of shift: Record output and pass notes to the next supervisor.

Plants that run around the clock need supervisors on every shift.

4How You Learn the Job

Many supervisors start on the floor. About a third of people in this job say a high school diploma is what it needs, along with experience. Some employers want college classes or a degree.

  • Grow from within: Machine operators and assemblers often become leads, then supervisors.
  • Leadership training: Many employers offer supervisor training.
  • Optional education: Bismarck State College and the University of North Dakota offer operations management and supervision programs.

Paying for training:

  1. Start with financial aid, scholarships, and grants. The school's financial aid office can help.
  2. Ask employers whether they help pay for training.
  3. Ask for the full cost, including books, fees, travel, and a computer.
  4. Ask your career counselor about other help, such as WIOA. WIOA is a federal program that can help some people pay for job training. Ask before you enroll.

Employer help with school may come with a promise to keep working there. Read the terms first.

5Training and Employer Requirements

No state license is needed. Requirements depend on the employer. Ask what you need.

  • Experience in production or a related job.
  • Knowledge of safety rules for the plant.
  • Comfort with computers for schedules and reports.
  • Willingness to work shifts, which may include nights or weekends.

Before you begin: Ask what leadership training and support new supervisors get.

Ask about written instructions, an accessible workstation, extra practice time, or other support that helps you learn and do the work well.

6Pay and Job Outlook

North Dakota production supervisor pay — May 2025:

  • Middle pay: $36.71 an hour ($76,350 a year). Half of workers earned more and half earned less.
  • Half of workers earned between $29.18 and $42.94 an hour.
  • 1 in 10 earned less than $25.21 an hour, and 1 in 10 earned more than $62.11.
  • These numbers show what workers earned. They are not a promise of what a new worker will start at.
  • North Dakota had about 1,730 of these jobs in 2024. The state projects about 1,840 in 2034, a change of +7%. That works out to about 180 openings a year, mostly from people who retire or change jobs.

Where people work: Food processing plants, equipment manufacturers, energy and ethanol plants, and other factories.

Work setting: Plant floors and offices. Shifts may include nights and weekends.

Compare an offer: Ask about base pay, shift pay, bonuses, benefits, and leadership training.

7Where This Can Lead

  • Senior supervisor: Lead several lines or a whole shift.
  • Production or plant manager: Run the whole operation.
  • Safety or quality specialist: Focus on one area you enjoy.
  • Training: Teach new workers and leads.

Ask leaders where you work which paths are open.

8The Real Talk

Things you might like:

  • Leading and helping people grow.
  • Solving problems on the spot.
  • Strong pay that grows with experience.
  • A path from the floor into management.

Things to ask about:

  • Pressure from goals and deadlines.
  • Handling conflicts and discipline.
  • Night, weekend, or rotating shifts.
  • Being called when something breaks.

North Dakota projects about 7% growth in these jobs from 2024 to 2034, with about 180 openings a year.

A question for yourself: Does leading a team on a busy plant floor sound good to you?
