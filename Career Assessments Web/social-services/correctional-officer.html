Correctional Officer Career Assessment

Keeping jails and prisons safe for staff and the people who live there

Section 1 of 11

1Welcome

Inside every jail and prison, officers keep things safe and running, around the clock. They also shape the daily lives of the people who live there.

This job mixes security, structure, and people skills. Explore what it feels like before deciding whether it interests you.

Go at your pace. The short questions at the end are for reflection. There are no right or wrong answers.

2What is a Correctional Officer?

You keep order and safety in a jail or prison. You supervise residents, follow security procedures, and respond when something goes wrong.

  • Supervise residents during daily activities.
  • Count residents and check locks, doors, and gates.
  • Search areas for items that are not allowed.
  • Screen visitors and control entrances.
  • Write reports about incidents and daily activity.
  • Help residents get to programs, meals, and appointments.

The real picture: Most of the job is talking, watching, and following routines. Being fair and consistent prevents many problems.

3A Day in the Life

One possible day: Every facility runs on its own schedule.

  • Start of shift: Get a briefing from the officers going off duty.
  • Morning: Do a count and walk a housing unit.
  • Midday: Supervise meals and movement to programs.
  • Afternoon: Watch cameras at a control station and screen visitors.
  • End of shift: Write reports and brief the next shift.

Most shifts are routine, and some are tense. Officers work as a team and call for help when needed.

4How You Learn the Job

A high school diploma or GED is what most jobs ask for. The North Dakota Department of Corrections and Rehabilitation says it trains new officers and pays them while they train.

  • Paid training: New officers learn security, communication, first aid, and how to calm tense situations.
  • County jails: North Dakota law requires jail staff who work directly with residents to complete training approved by the state Peace Officer Standards and Training board.
  • Optional education: Minot State University offers a corrections and criminal justice program.

Paying for training:

  1. Start with financial aid, scholarships, and grants. The school's financial aid office can help.
  2. Ask employers whether they help pay for training.
  3. Ask for the full cost, including books, fees, travel, and a computer.
  4. Ask your career counselor about other help, such as WIOA. WIOA is a federal program that can help some people pay for job training. Ask before you enroll.

Employer help with school may come with a promise to keep working there. Read the terms first.

5Training and Employer Requirements

Requirements depend on the employer. Ask each one what you need.

  • A high school diploma or GED.
  • A minimum age; county jails often require age 18, and some employers require 21.
  • A background check, drug test, and sometimes a physical or fitness test.
  • Completion of the employer’s training program.
  • Willingness to work rotating shifts, including nights and holidays.

Before you begin: Ask how long training lasts and what support new officers get, including help after a hard shift.

Ask about written instructions, an accessible workstation, extra practice time, or other support that helps you learn and do the work well.

6Pay and Job Outlook

North Dakota correctional officer pay — May 2025:

  • Middle pay: $25.22 an hour ($52,460 a year). Half of workers earned more and half earned less.
  • Half of workers earned between $23.77 and $29.39 an hour.
  • 1 in 10 earned less than $22.88 an hour, and 1 in 10 earned more than $30.23.
  • These numbers show what workers earned. They are not a promise of what a new worker will start at.
  • North Dakota had about 1,070 of these jobs in 2024. The state projects about 1,000 in 2034, a change of -6%. That works out to about 90 openings a year, mostly from people who retire or change jobs.

Where people work: State prisons run by the ND Department of Corrections and Rehabilitation, county and regional jails, and juvenile facilities.

Work setting: Inside secure facilities, with rotating shifts around the clock.

Compare an offer: Ask about base pay, shift pay, overtime, benefits, retirement, and paid training.

7Where This Can Lead

  • Senior officer or trainer: Train new staff.
  • Sergeant, lieutenant, or supervisor: Lead a shift.
  • Case manager or programs: Help residents prepare to return to the community.
  • Related careers: Probation officer, police officer, or 911 dispatcher.

Ask officers where you would work which paths are open.

8The Real Talk

Things you might like:

  • Paid training and steady public-sector benefits.
  • Clear rules and teamwork.
  • Starting with a high school diploma.
  • A chance to treat people with dignity in a hard place.

Things to ask about:

  • Stress and exposure to conflict.
  • Nights, weekends, holidays, and mandatory overtime.
  • The emotional weight of the work.
  • Support for officers’ mental health.

North Dakota projects about a 6% drop in these jobs from 2024 to 2034, but there are still about 90 openings a year as people retire or change jobs.

A question for yourself: Can you see yourself staying calm, fair, and steady in a structured, sometimes tense place?

10Part 2: Things You Could Try

For each activity, choose whether it sounds comfortable to try. You do not need these skills already, and you can ask for support.

1. Following strict rules and procedures every time
2. Staying calm when someone is angry or upset
3. Talking respectfully with people from many backgrounds
4. Watching closely and noticing small changes
5. Writing clear reports about what happened
6. Working nights, weekends, or holidays
7. Standing and walking for much of a shift
8. Calling for backup instead of handling everything alone
