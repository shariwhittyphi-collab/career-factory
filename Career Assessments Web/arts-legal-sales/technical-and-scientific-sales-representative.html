Technical and Scientific Sales Representative Career Assessment

Selling equipment and products you have to understand first

Section 1 of 11

1Welcome

Technical and scientific sales representatives sell products that are complicated enough that the customer needs someone who truly understands them — a combine, a downhole tool, a surgical instrument, an agronomy program, a compressor.

The official title is Sales Representatives, Wholesale and Manufacturing, Technical and Scientific Products. It is a driving job, a teaching job, and a quota job all at once. You will explore whether it fits you.

This takes about 15 minutes.

2What is a Technical and Scientific Sales Representative?

A technical sales representative owns a territory — a set of counties, plants, farms, clinics, or shops — and is responsible for what gets sold in it. You drive to customers, walk their operation, figure out what problem they actually have, and explain how your product solves it, in terms they can check. You do demonstrations. You quote prices and write orders. Then you go back after the sale, because if the machine does not perform, you are the person they call. A quota is the sales number you are expected to hit, usually every quarter and every year, and much of your pay depends on it.

The Reality: Two hard truths. First, the first year is usually lean. You are learning the product and building a territory from nothing, and commission comes from sales you have not made yet. Many employers give a salary, a guarantee, or a draw for the first year — and a draw is often an advance you pay back out of later commissions. Ask exactly how it works before you accept. Second, this is a driving job. Two hundred miles in a day is normal in North Dakota, in February as well as in June, and dinner with a customer is work, not free time.

Your Responsibilities:

  • Drive your territory and visit customers where they work
  • Learn the product deeply enough to answer hard questions
  • Walk a customer’s plant, farm, or shop and find the real problem
  • Run demonstrations and hands-on trials
  • Prepare quotes, write orders, and track them through delivery
  • Follow up after the sale and handle it when something fails
  • Keep the customer records in the sales software current
  • Hit a quota and report your numbers

You are the reason a customer trusts a machine they have not bought yet.

3A Day in the Life

6:45 AM — Coffee and the route. Three stops today, about 190 miles of driving.

8:00 AM — First stop, a farm shop. The producer is upset about downtime. Listen first, sell later.

9:30 AM — Windshield time. Call your service manager hands-free and get a part expedited.

11:00 AM — Second stop, a processing plant. Walk the line with the maintenance lead.

12:30 PM — Lunch in the truck while you build a quote on your laptop.

2:00 PM — Third stop, a demonstration. Let the customer run it themselves. That is what sells it.

4:30 PM — Drive home. Log every visit in the sales software while it is fresh.

6:30 PM — Dinner with a customer who only has evenings. This counts as work.

Long days, a lot of windshield time, and a number you are measured on every quarter.

4Training in North Dakota

No license is required. What you need is product knowledge, a clean driving record, and the ability to hear no. Employers hire two kinds of people into technical sales. Some hire a four-year business or technical degree and teach the product. Others promote from inside — the parts counter, the service bay, the agronomy crew, the shop floor — because that person already knows the product cold and the customers already trust them. Both routes are real in North Dakota, and the second one costs you nothing in tuition. Whichever route you take, subject knowledge is the part you cannot fake.

Training Pathways:

  • Four-year degree plus a sales credential (4 years): A business, marketing, agriculture, or technical degree, plus a professional selling minor or certificate. This is the fastest way into a company’s formal sales training program and the corporate accounts.
  • Two-year technical degree, then move into sales (2 years plus experience): Get an ag, diesel, electrical, machining, or health technology associate degree and take a technician, parts, or service job. Learn the product and the customers for a few years, then apply for the territory when it opens. Employers like this path because you already know what the machine does at 2 a.m.
  • Come in through the counter or the field (varies): Parts counter, service writer, agronomy scout, or inside sales. Many North Dakota outside sales reps started here. Ask your manager directly what they need to see from you before they hand you a territory.

Where to Train in North Dakota:

  • NDSU (Fargo) — Professional Selling minor and undergraduate certificate, open to students in any major. Home of the Center for Professional Selling and Sales Technology, the only dedicated academic sales center in North Dakota’s eleven-campus university system. Role-plays, internships, a Sales Club, and networking events. NDSU reports about 95% of recent graduates employed or continuing education — ndsu.edu
  • University of North Dakota (Grand Forks) — Professional Sales minor, 16 to 18 credits, on campus or online. Courses are Professional Selling, Negotiations for Sales and Relationship Management, Customer Relationship Management platforms, Sales Management, Personal Marketing, and a sales internship. UND points the coursework at North Dakota industries including energy, unmanned aircraft, and healthcare, and lists paid internships — und.edu
  • ND State College of Science (Wahpeton) — agriculture, diesel, precision machining, electrical, and mechanical AAS programs. This is the product-knowledge route into equipment and industrial sales — catalog.ndscs.edu
  • Bismarck State College (Bismarck) — energy, process, instrumentation, and business programs, plus online options. Strong fit for anyone aiming at energy services or industrial supply sales — bismarckstate.edu
  • Lake Region State College (Devils Lake) and Williston State College (Williston) — business and technical associate degrees close to the ag and Bakken territories where these jobs actually are — lrsc.edu, willistonstate.edu
  • Your employer — most equipment, energy, and device companies run their own paid product training for new reps. It is often the best training available and they pay you to take it. Ask about it in the interview

Before You Start: High school diploma or GED. A valid driver’s license and a clean driving record are not optional — you will be insured to drive a company vehicle. You need to be able to do quick math in front of a customer, learn technical material fast, and hear no without taking it personally. A background check and a drug screen are standard.

Tip: Start with financial aid, scholarships, and any help an employer offers — that is where the money starts. After that, ask a career counselor or Job Service North Dakota whether WIOA or other programs can help with what is left over, like training, tools, work boots, or exam fees. What can be paid for depends on the program and on your own situation, so ask before you sign up rather than after. Registered apprenticeships let you earn a paycheck while you learn — see jobsnd.com/job-seeker/apprenticeships for ND programs.

5Certification & Licensing

No license and no required certification. Product and industry credentials are what carry weight here. Nobody licenses technical salespeople. What employers and customers care about is whether you actually know the product, and in a few North Dakota industries there is a credential that proves you do. The manufacturer’s own certification is usually the most valuable one, and it is usually free because they want you to have it.

What Actually Counts:

  • No state license — North Dakota does not license sales representatives
  • Driver’s license and clean record — the one true requirement. You cannot hold this job if you cannot be insured to drive
  • Manufacturer product certifications — equipment, instrument, and device makers run their own training and certify reps on their product lines. Usually paid for by your employer. Ask what lines you would be certified on
  • Certified Crop Adviser (CCA) — issued by the American Society of Agronomy, and the credential to have for agronomy, seed, and crop-input sales. It requires exams, an education and experience standard, and ongoing continuing education units. There is a North Dakota-specific set of performance objectives for the local exam, so you are tested on conditions here — agronomy.org
  • Hospital vendor credentialing — if you sell medical devices or lab equipment you will have to be credentialed to enter clinical areas, which usually means background checks, immunization records, and training. Each health system sets its own rules, so ask the employer what theirs involves
  • Safety training for energy and industrial sites — site-specific safety orientation is commonly required before you can walk a plant, a rig, or a compressor station. Employers arrange it

The Exams: There is no exam to enter this work. The Certified Crop Adviser credential does have exams — a broad one plus a local exam written to North Dakota performance objectives — and manufacturer certifications usually end with a product test. Check agronomy.org for current CCA exam dates and requirements.

Cost: Getting into the job costs you nothing but a driver’s license. Certified Crop Adviser fees are set by the American Society of Agronomy — check agronomy.org for current amounts. Manufacturer training is normally paid by the employer. Ask in the interview who pays for training, travel, fuel, and a phone.

Keeping It Current: The Certified Crop Adviser credential requires ongoing continuing education units. Manufacturer certifications are refreshed whenever the product line changes, which in equipment is often every year or two.

6Salary & Job Outlook

North Dakota Technical and Scientific Sales Representative Pay (May 2025):

  • Starting out (bottom 10–25%): $28.85–$35.86/hour ($60,010–$74,590/year)
  • Middle (median — half earn more, half earn less): $49.38/hour ($102,720/year)
  • Experienced (top 25–10%): $66.00–$76.92/hour ($137,280–$160,000/year)

What that means: Read the middle number with real caution. The published North Dakota middle is about $49.38 an hour, or about $102,720 a year — the highest in this group. But that figure blends base salary, commission, and bonus into one number, and commission does not spread evenly. In the same territory, in the same year, one rep can finish near the bottom tenth and another near the top. The bottom tenth here, about $60,010, is often exactly what a first-year rep earns while the territory is still being built. And be careful about the top: the top tenth in North Dakota reaches about $160,000 a year, while the top tenth nationally reaches about $200,440. The big commission years are more common where the accounts are bigger. Before you sign anything, ask four questions in writing: what is the base, how is commission calculated, is there a guarantee or a draw in year one, and if it is a draw, do I have to pay it back.

Jobs in North Dakota: About 440 people do this work in ND today. By 2034, that should be about 440 (about 1% growth — essentially flat). Expect about 40 job openings every year in ND from growth and people retiring or changing jobs. Across the U.S., the change is about 2% growth — slower than average.

Outlook: Small and flat, but the pay is the highest here. About 440 people hold these jobs in North Dakota and the state projects the same number in 2034, with roughly 40 openings a year. Openings come from people retiring or moving, not from new positions. Worth knowing: the non-technical version of this job — wholesale and manufacturing sales of everything that is not technical or scientific — is about ten times bigger in North Dakota, with about 4,320 people and roughly 390 openings a year. The pay is lower, but the door is much wider, and it is a very common place to start before moving to the technical side.

Who Hires in ND: Agricultural and construction equipment dealers are the biggest group — Titan Machinery, headquartered in West Fargo, Butler Machinery in Bismarck and Fargo, RDO Equipment Co. in Fargo, and the Bobcat dealer network. Also: energy service and supply companies across the Bakken in Williston, Dickinson, and Minot; agronomy, seed, and crop-input companies and co-ops; industrial and electrical distributors such as Border States in Fargo; medical device and laboratory suppliers selling into Sanford, Essentia, Altru, and the critical access hospitals; and manufacturers selling their own equipment direct.

Work Setting: Your truck, mostly. Plus customers’ farms, shops, plants, rigs, and clinics, a dealership or branch office, and a home office for quotes and paperwork. Trade shows and dealer meetings. Overnight travel in the bigger territories, and weather you drive in anyway.

Physical Demands: Moderate. Long hours behind the wheel, which is harder on your back than it sounds. Climbing on and around equipment, walking plants and fields, lifting demo gear and sample cases. Outdoors in North Dakota weather. Steel toes and hearing protection for plant and site visits.

Sources: U.S. Bureau of Labor Statistics, Occupational Employment and Wage Statistics, North Dakota, May 2025 (SOC 41-4011); North Dakota and U.S. long-term employment projections 2024–2034, via O*NET OnLine. Numbers are rounded.

7Where This Leads

Path 1: Inside Sales or Parts Counter — A common first job. Learn the product and the customers.

Path 2: Territory Sales Representative — Your own territory and your own quota.

Path 3: Senior or Key Account Representative — The biggest accounts and the most complex sales.

Path 4: Product Specialist — Deep expertise in one line; you get called in to help other reps.

Path 5: Sales Manager — Run a team of reps and their territories.

Path 6: Regional or Branch Manager — Run a whole branch or region, including service and parts.

8The Real Talk

What's Great:

  • Highest published pay in this group
  • You are out among people, not at a desk
  • You become a genuine product expert
  • Strong performance pays quickly
  • North Dakota ag and energy need this role
  • Clear ladder into management

What's Hard:

  • A quota every quarter, every year
  • The first year is often lean
  • A lot of windshield time in bad weather
  • Evenings and overnights away from home
  • Commission means uneven paychecks
  • You hear no far more than yes

Be Honest: Can you hear no ten times and make the eleventh call? Can you live with a paycheck that changes month to month, and a first year that may be thin? Can you drive long distances in winter and still be good company at the other end? And are you willing to learn a product so well that a customer who has run it for twenty years respects your answer?
