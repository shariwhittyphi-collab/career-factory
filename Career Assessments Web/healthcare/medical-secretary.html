Medical Secretary Career Assessment

Keeping a clinic's schedule, records, and front desk running

Section 1 of 11

1Welcome

A patient calls to make an appointment. Another walks in and needs to check in. A doctor needs a record sent to a specialist. The medical secretary helps keep all of it moving.

This career is a way to work in healthcare from the office side. Explore what it feels like before deciding whether it interests you.

Go at your pace. The short questions at the end are for reflection. There are no right or wrong answers.

2What is a Medical Secretary?

You handle the office work of a clinic, hospital department, or dental office. You schedule appointments, check patients in, handle insurance information, and keep medical records organized and private.

  • Answer phones and schedule appointments, tests, and procedures.
  • Greet and check in patients.
  • Collect and enter insurance information.
  • Send and receive medical records and test results.
  • Help patients fill out forms.
  • Keep patient information private and secure.

The real picture: You will see people who are sick, hurting, or worried. A calm, kind voice is part of the job.

3A Day in the Life

One possible day: Clinics are busiest when the doors open.

  • Morning: Check in a line of patients and scan their insurance cards.
  • Later: Answer calls and fit a sick patient into a full schedule.
  • Midday: Send records to a specialist and file test results.
  • Afternoon: Call patients to confirm tomorrow's appointments.
  • Before leaving: Check that tomorrow's schedule and paperwork are ready.

Some clinics have early, evening, or weekend hours.

4How You Learn the Job

There is more than one way in. About half the people in this job say a high school diploma or GED is what new workers need. Others say an associate degree or a college certificate. Read the actual job ad.

  • Build basic skills first: Typing, email, scheduling, and clear phone manners.
  • Learn medical words: A medical terminology class helps you understand what doctors and nurses write.
  • Optional education: North Dakota schools with medical office programs include Turtle Mountain College in Belcourt, Dakota College at Bottineau, Bismarck State College, Williston State College, and Rasmussen University in Fargo.
  • On-the-job training: Each clinic trains staff on its own scheduling and records system.

Paying for training:

  1. Start with financial aid, scholarships, and grants. The school's financial aid office can help.
  2. Ask employers whether they help pay for training.
  3. Ask for the full cost, including books, fees, travel, and a computer.
  4. Ask your career counselor about other help, such as WIOA. WIOA is a federal program that can help some people pay for job training. Ask before you enroll.

Employer help with school may come with a promise to keep working there. Read the terms first.

5Training and Employer Requirements

No state license is needed for this job. Employers set their own requirements. Ask what you need to start.

  • Comfort with a computer, phone, and scheduling software.
  • Training on patient privacy rules, which employers provide.
  • A background check, which many healthcare employers require.
  • Some employers ask for proof of certain vaccinations.
  • Optional medical office certifications; ask whether an employer wants one before you pay for it.

Before you begin: Ask how long training lasts and who helps with insurance and billing questions.

Ask about written instructions, an accessible workstation, extra practice time, or other support that helps you learn and do the work well.

6Pay and Job Outlook

North Dakota medical secretary pay — May 2025:

  • Middle pay: $21.82 an hour ($45,390 a year). Half of workers earned more and half earned less.
  • Half of workers earned between $18.96 and $24.14 an hour.
  • 1 in 10 earned less than $18.00 an hour, and 1 in 10 earned more than $27.95.
  • These numbers show what workers earned. They are not a promise of what a new worker will start at.
  • North Dakota had about 970 of these jobs in 2024. The state projects about 1,030 in 2034, a change of +6%. That works out to about 100 openings a year, mostly from people who retire or change jobs.

Where people work: Clinics, hospitals, dental offices, nursing homes, and other healthcare offices.

Work setting: A front desk or back office with a computer and phone, in a clean indoor setting.

Compare an offer: Ask about base pay, hours, weekend or evening shifts, benefits, paid time off, training, and help paying for classes.

7Where This Can Lead

  • Lead front office worker: Train new staff and handle harder scheduling problems.
  • Medical records or billing: More training can lead to medical records specialist or billing jobs.
  • Office manager: Run a clinic's front office as experience grows.
  • Patient care: Some people decide they want hands-on care and train as a medical assistant or nurse.

Ask your employer whether they help pay for healthcare classes.

8The Real Talk

Things you might like:

  • Being part of healthcare without hands-on care.
  • Helping people when they need it.
  • Clinics and hospitals in many North Dakota towns.
  • A field that is expected to grow.

Things to ask about:

  • Busy phones and several requests at once.
  • Patients who are scared, in pain, or frustrated.
  • Long stretches of sitting and computer work.
  • Insurance rules that take time to learn.

North Dakota expects about 6% growth over ten years, with about 100 openings a year.

A question for yourself: Do you like staying organized and kind when things get busy?
