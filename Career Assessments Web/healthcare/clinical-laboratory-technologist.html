Clinical Laboratory Technologist Career Assessment

Medical lab testing specialist

Section 1 of 11

1Welcome

Clinical laboratory technologists and technicians test blood, urine, and tissue samples to help doctors find and treat disease. About 7 out of 10 medical decisions depend on lab results.

You will explore what this behind-the-scenes healthcare career is really like and whether it fits you.

This takes about 15 minutes.

2What is a Clinical Laboratory Technologist?

Lab professionals run tests on patient samples using microscopes and advanced machines. There are two main levels: Medical Laboratory Technicians (MLT) have a 2-year degree. Medical Laboratory Scientists (MLS) have a 4-year degree and handle more complex tests.

The Reality: Patients never see you, but doctors depend on you. One wrong result can lead to the wrong treatment. Accuracy is everything.

Your Responsibilities:

  • Run blood tests (blood counts, blood sugar, cholesterol)
  • Look at blood and cells under a microscope
  • Grow and identify bacteria to find infections
  • Match blood for transfusions (blood bank)
  • Run and check high-tech lab machines
  • Do quality checks so results are correct
  • Call doctors and nurses with critical results
  • Sometimes draw blood from patients

You are the detective behind the diagnosis.

3A Day in the Life

6:00 AM — Arrive. Morning blood samples from the hospital floors are already coming in.

6:15 AM — Run quality control on the machines to make sure they are accurate before testing patients.

7:00 AM — Load dozens of blood tubes. Check results as they come out. Flag anything unusual.

8:30 AM — A result shows dangerously low potassium. You call the nurse right away.

10:00 AM — Look at a blood smear under the microscope. Some cells look abnormal. You send it for the pathologist to review.

11:30 AM — Emergency: a trauma patient needs blood now. You match blood for a transfusion, fast and carefully.

12:30 PM — Lunch.

1:00 PM — Microbiology: check bacteria growing on plates from yesterday. Identify an infection and test which antibiotics will work.

2:30 PM — Clean, restock, and record everything. Hand off to the evening shift.

Precise, fast-paced, and mostly behind the scenes. Hospitals run labs 24/7.

4Training in North Dakota

North Dakota requires a state license. You need a college program plus a national certification exam, then an ND license. There are two levels.

Training Pathways:

  • MLT — Medical Laboratory Technician (2 years): Associate degree with a clinical rotation in real labs.
  • MLS — Medical Laboratory Scientist (4 years): Bachelor's degree including a clinical year. Higher pay and more complex work.
  • MLT to MLS: Work as an MLT, then finish your bachelor's later. UND offers this fully online.

Where to Train in North Dakota:

  • Bismarck State College (Bismarck) — Medical Laboratory Technician AAS; NAACLS-accredited; 22-week clinical rotation; 97% first-time exam pass rate since 1978
  • University of North Dakota (Grand Forks or online) — B.S. in Medical Laboratory Science; also online MLT-to-MLS
  • North Dakota State University (Fargo) — Medical Laboratory Science major
  • Minot State University (Minot) — B.S. in Medical Laboratory Science
  • University of Jamestown (Jamestown) — Medical Laboratory Science, with a clinical year at an affiliated site

Before You Start: High school diploma or GED. High school biology, chemistry, and algebra are strongly recommended. Background check, immunizations, and drug screen are required before clinicals. Normal color vision is important for reading tests.

Tip: Start with financial aid, scholarships, and any help an employer offers — that is where the money starts. After that, ask a career counselor or Job Service North Dakota whether WIOA or other programs can help with what is left over, like tuition, exam fees, scrubs, or books. What can be paid for depends on the program and on your own situation, so ask before you sign up rather than after. Some ND hospitals also help pay for training if you agree to work for them afterward.

5Certification & Licensing

State license required in North Dakota. North Dakota is one of the few states that licenses lab professionals. The ND Board of Clinical Laboratory Practice issues the license after you pass a national exam.

What You Need:

  • National certification: ASCP Board of Certification — MLT(ASCP) or MLS(ASCP) (other board-approved exams also accepted)
  • ND license: Medical Laboratory Technician (MLT) or Medical Technologist / Clinical Laboratory Scientist (bachelor's required)
  • Specialist license: For people with a science bachelor's who certify in one area (like microbiology)

The Exams: The ASCP exam is computer-based and adaptive (100 questions, about 2.5 hours). It covers blood bank, chemistry, hematology, microbiology, urinalysis, and lab operations.

Cost: About $225–$250 for the ASCP exam, plus the ND license fee. Prices change, so check before you buy.

Keeping It Current: ND licenses renew every 2 years (by July 1 of even-numbered years) with continuing education. ASCP certification renews every 3 years.

6Salary & Job Outlook

North Dakota Clinical Laboratory Technologist and Technician Pay (May 2025):

  • Starting out (bottom 10–25%): $17.93–$23.20/hour ($37,300–$48,260/year)
  • Middle (median — half earn more, half earn less): $30.14/hour ($62,680/year)
  • Experienced (top 25–10%): $37.20–$40.35/hour ($77,370–$83,920/year)

What that means: MLTs (2-year degree) usually earn in the lower-to-middle range. MLS (4-year degree) earn in the middle-to-upper range. Night shifts often pay extra.

Jobs in North Dakota: About 1,180 people do this work in ND today. By 2034, that should be about 1,260 (about 6% growth). Expect about 80 job openings every year in ND from growth and people retiring or changing jobs. Across the U.S., the change is about 2% growth.

Outlook: Good. ND is growing faster than the U.S., with about 80 openings a year. Many lab workers are near retirement, and rural hospitals often have trouble filling these jobs.

Who Hires in ND: Hospitals of all sizes (including small rural hospitals), clinics, reference labs, public health labs, blood banks, and research labs across ND.

Work Setting: Hospital and clinic labs. Hospitals run 24/7, so evening, night, weekend, and holiday shifts are common, especially for new hires. Small rural labs may have you on call.

Physical Demands: Moderate. On your feet for much of the shift. Fine hand work with small tubes and pipettes. Long periods at a microscope. You work around blood and infectious samples, using safety gear.

Sources: U.S. Bureau of Labor Statistics, Occupational Employment and Wage Statistics, North Dakota, May 2025 (SOC 29-2010); North Dakota and U.S. long-term employment projections 2024–2034, via O*NET OnLine. Numbers are rounded.

7Where This Leads

Path 1: MLT to MLS — Earn your bachelor's (online options) for higher pay and more responsibility.

Path 2: Specialist — Focus on blood bank, microbiology, chemistry, or molecular testing.

Path 3: Lab Supervisor or Manager — Run a hospital lab.

Path 4: Rural Lab Lead — Be the go-to lab expert at a small ND hospital.

Path 5: Public Health or Research — Work in state public health labs or research.

Path 6: Other Careers — Physician assistant, pathologist assistant, or industry and sales roles.

8The Real Talk

What's Great:

  • Important work: most medical decisions depend on the lab
  • Steady demand across all of ND, including rural areas
  • Good pay for a 2-year degree
  • Great for people who like science and detail
  • Less direct patient contact
  • Clear path from MLT to MLS

What's Hard:

  • Nights, weekends, and holidays, especially at first
  • High pressure to be perfectly accurate
  • Critical results and emergencies (like trauma transfusions)
  • Working with blood and infectious samples
  • Behind the scenes, so you get little recognition
  • State license and national exam required
  • Lots of science and chemistry in school
  • Repetitive tasks

Be Honest: Do you enjoy science and precise, careful work? Can you handle working with blood? Are you OK with night or weekend shifts? Can you stay accurate under pressure?
