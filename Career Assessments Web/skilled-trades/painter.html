Painter Career Assessment

Preparing and painting walls, buildings, and structures with care

Section 1 of 11

1Welcome

Fresh paint changes how a room or building feels. Behind every clean edge is a painter who did careful prep work first.

This job mixes detail, patience, and physical work. Explore what it feels like before deciding whether it interests you.

Go at your pace. The short questions at the end are for reflection. There are no right or wrong answers.

2What is a Painter?

You prepare and paint walls, ceilings, trim, and outside surfaces of homes and buildings.

  • Cover floors and furniture and tape off edges.
  • Fill cracks and holes, then sand and scrape.
  • Apply primer, paint, and stain with brushes, rollers, or sprayers.
  • Set up ladders and scaffolding safely.
  • Read work orders and match colors.
  • Clean up tools and the work area.

The real picture: Much of the job is preparation. Painting outside is seasonal in North Dakota, while inside work goes on all year.

3A Day in the Life

One possible day: Every job is different.

  • Morning: Move furniture, lay drop cloths, and tape trim.
  • Later: Patch holes and sand the walls smooth.
  • Midday: Prime the patched spots.
  • Afternoon: Cut in edges with a brush and roll the walls.
  • Before leaving: Clean brushes and rollers and check the work in good light.

Some days are spent high on a ladder outside. Others are quiet indoor work.

4How You Learn the Job

Most painters learn on the job. Many employers hire people with a high school diploma or GED, and some do not require one.

  • On-the-job training: An experienced painter teaches prep, tools, and safety.
  • Apprenticeship: Painter apprenticeships pay you while you learn.
  • Safety training: Ladder, scaffold, and respirator training are common.
  • Lead-safe training: Work on homes built before 1978 follows federal lead-safe rules, and the company must be certified. Workers are trained in lead-safe work practices.

Paying for training: In an apprenticeship you are paid while you learn, and pay usually rises as you complete hours. For classes or a certificate, start with financial aid, scholarships, and grants. Ask employers whether they help pay. Ask your career counselor about other help, such as WIOA, a federal program that can help some people pay for job training. Ask before you enroll.

5Training and Employer Requirements

North Dakota does not license painters. Painting contractors need a state contractor license for larger jobs, and that is the employer’s responsibility. Ask what you need to start.

  • Ability to climb ladders, stand, and reach for long periods, or ask about the physical demands.
  • Comfort wearing a mask or respirator when needed.
  • A driver’s license for travel to job sites.
  • Lead-safe training if the employer works on older homes.

Before you begin: Ask who trains new painters and what safety gear is provided.

Ask about written instructions, a buddy on your first jobs, extra practice time, or other support that helps you learn and work safely.

6Pay and Job Outlook

North Dakota painter pay — May 2025:

  • Middle pay: $26.38 an hour ($54,870 a year). Half of workers earned more and half earned less.
  • Half of workers earned between $22.75 and $29.16 an hour.
  • 1 in 10 earned less than $17.71 an hour, and 1 in 10 earned more than $37.64.
  • These numbers show what workers earned. They are not a promise of what a new worker will start at.
  • North Dakota had about 610 of these jobs in 2024. The state projects about 640 in 2034, a change of +5%. That works out to about 50 openings a year, mostly from people who retire or change jobs.

Where people work: Painting contractors, building contractors, schools and hospitals, property managers, and self-employment.

Work setting: Inside homes and buildings, and outside on ladders and scaffolding. Outdoor work is seasonal.

Compare an offer: Ask about base pay, winter work, overtime, benefits, and what safety gear is provided.

7Where This Can Lead

  • Experienced painter: Take on spraying, special finishes, or commercial jobs.
  • Crew leader: Lead a painting crew.
  • Estimator: Measure jobs and figure costs.
  • Your own business: Some painters start their own company, which needs a contractor license and insurance.

Ask painters near you which paths are open.

8The Real Talk

Things you might like:

  • A clear before and after.
  • Careful, detailed hands-on work.
  • Starting without a degree.
  • Inside work in winter and outside work in summer.

Things to ask about:

  • Ladders and heights.
  • Paint fumes, dust, and old lead paint.
  • Slow seasons and layoffs.
  • Standing and reaching all day.

North Dakota projects about 5% growth in these jobs from 2024 to 2034, with about 50 openings a year.

A question for yourself: Does patient, careful work that looks good when it is done sound like you?
