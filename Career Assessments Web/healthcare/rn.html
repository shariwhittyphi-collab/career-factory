Registered Nurse (RN)

Career Assessment Tool — North Dakota

This tool will help you decide if becoming a Registered Nurse is the right choice for you.

You will learn what RNs really do, how to get trained in North Dakota, and what comes next in your nursing career.

Important: Answer all questions honestly. This is just for you—there are no wrong answers.

What is a Registered Nurse?

A Registered Nurse, or RN, provides comprehensive patient care, makes independent nursing judgments, and leads healthcare teams. RNs work in hospitals, clinics, schools, home health, public health, and many other settings. RNs have more independence than LPNs and CNAs. RNs assess patients, make treatment decisions, give medications, teach patients, and manage their own cases. RNs are often leaders in healthcare who supervise other nurses and healthcare workers.

What Will You Actually Be Doing Every Day?

Here are the real tasks RNs do:

  • Assess patients — ask questions, listen, examine, review their history
  • Make nursing diagnoses and treatment plans
  • Administer and monitor medications (IV, oral, injections)
  • Start and monitor IVs and catheters
  • Monitor vital signs and recognize when something is wrong
  • Perform wound care and dressing changes
  • Help patients with bathing, dressing, toileting, mobility
  • Chart and communicate patient information clearly
  • Work with doctors to modify treatment plans
  • Teach patients about their medications and condition
  • Comfort patients and families during difficult times
  • Supervise and lead other nurses and CNAs
  • Make decisions about patient safety and emergency care
What You Need to Know: RN work is the most complex role in nursing. You are responsible for patient assessments and treatment decisions. You must think critically and act quickly in emergencies. You will work with doctors, therapists, social workers, and other specialists. You lead a team. You work nights, weekends, and holidays. The work is physically and emotionally demanding. You make life-and-death decisions. You must be comfortable with medications, anatomy, physiology, and complex medical situations. You need strong communication skills.

What Training Do You Need in North Dakota?

Two Paths to Become an RN:

Path 1: Associate Degree in Nursing (ADN/AAS) — Faster

  • About 2 years of nursing school (Dakota Nursing Program: 3 semesters for the PN year, then 2 more semesters for the RN), plus any required prerequisite classes
  • Offered at community colleges and state colleges throughout North Dakota
  • Combines classroom and clinical experience
  • Cost: about $17,700 to $29,000 for both years in the Dakota Nursing Program (2025-26 estimate; includes tuition, fees, books, and supplies). Costs vary by school.

Path 2: Bachelor of Science in Nursing (BSN) — More Advanced

  • 4 years full-time
  • Offered at universities in North Dakota
  • More in-depth study of science, research, and leadership
  • Cost: varies by school. Example: at NDSU, ND residents pay about $12,200 per year in tuition and fees, and about $14,200 per year once in the nursing program (2026-27). Housing and books are extra.
  • Many employers now prefer BSN graduates for advancement

RN Training Requirements in North Dakota:

  • Complete an approved nursing program (ADN or BSN)
  • Pass the NCLEX-RN exam (National Council Licensure Examination for Registered Nurses)
  • Apply for licensure with the North Dakota Board of Nursing
  • Pass a background check
  • Health screening requirements

Prerequisites:

  • High school diploma or GED
  • College prerequisites before nursing courses (anatomy, physiology, chemistry, microbiology, etc.)
  • Most schools require a minimum GPA (for example, 2.5 for the Dakota Nursing Program)
  • CPR certification before entering program
  • Good grades in science courses help you get accepted

What is the NCLEX-RN Exam?

After you finish your RN program (ADN or BSN), you must pass the NCLEX-RN (National Council Licensure Examination for Registered Nurses). This computerized exam tests your knowledge to safely practice as an RN. Here is what it covers:

What the Exam Tests:

  • Safe and effective nursing care for individuals and groups
  • How to assess and diagnose patient problems
  • How to plan and implement nursing care
  • Medications and medication safety
  • Communication and therapeutic relationships with patients
  • Teaching patients and families
  • How to work with the healthcare team
  • Leadership and delegation skills
  • Anatomy, physiology, pathophysiology, and pharmacology
  • Legal and ethical responsibilities
  • How to recognize and manage medical emergencies
  • Critical thinking and clinical judgment

Exam Format:

  • Computer-based adaptive testing
  • Between 85 and 150 questions (varies per test taker)
  • Time limit: 5 hours total (includes tutorial and breaks)
  • Questions get harder as you answer correctly; easier if you answer incorrectly
  • You must reach a certain level of competency to pass
  • Official results come from the Board of Nursing, usually within 6 weeks (unofficial quick results may be bought after 2 business days)
About the Exam: The NCLEX-RN is harder than the NCLEX-PN because RNs make more complex decisions. You can usually take it within weeks of graduation. The exam fee is $200. If you do not pass, you must wait at least 45 days to try again. Your RN program will prepare you. Many programs include NCLEX prep courses. You must pass to become licensed.

What Comes After RN?

Being an RN opens many doors for your nursing career:

Path 1: Advanced Practice Nursing

Return to school for advanced degrees and certifications:

  • Nurse Practitioner (NP) — diagnose and treat patients independently in some settings
  • Clinical Nurse Specialist (CNS) — expert in a clinical area
  • Certified Nurse Midwife (CNM) — deliver babies and manage pregnancy care
  • Certified Nurse Anesthetist (CRNA) — administer anesthesia in surgery
  • Most take 2 to 4 more years of graduate school (master’s or doctorate)

Path 2: Specialization Within RN

Become an expert in a specific area without a new degree:

  • Critical care (ICU nursing)
  • Operating room (surgery)
  • Emergency department
  • Pediatrics (children)
  • Obstetrics (pregnancy and birth)
  • Oncology (cancer care)
  • Cardiac care (heart and blood vessels)
  • Home health nursing
  • Public health nursing
  • School nursing

Path 3: Leadership and Management

Move into supervisory and management roles:

  • Charge nurse or shift supervisor
  • Nursing manager or unit manager
  • Director of Nursing
  • Chief Nursing Officer
  • Nurse educator or trainer
  • Quality improvement leader

Path 4: Teaching and Research

Work in education or research settings:

  • Nursing school instructor or professor
  • Clinical educator
  • Nursing research
  • Hospital-based education programs

Path 5: Stay as an RN and Build Expertise

Many RNs have fulfilling, well-paying careers without advancing to advanced practice. You develop deep expertise, are trusted by patients and doctors, and become a mentor to newer nurses.

Should You Become an RN?

RNs are the backbone of healthcare. You will work with patients every day and directly impact their health, healing, and sometimes their survival.

RN is a good choice if:

  • You want to make a real difference in people's lives
  • You are ready for 2-4 years of focused study and clinical practice
  • You are strong in science and can handle complex material
  • You can think quickly and make good decisions under pressure
  • You want to lead and work independently in healthcare
  • You are interested in specialization or advancement
  • You want earning potential of about $65,000-$103,000 per year
  • You can work nights, weekends, and holidays
  • You handle stress and difficult situations well
  • You communicate clearly and work well with teams
  • You can handle emotional situations with patients and families

Think twice if:

  • You do not like taking responsibility for decisions
  • You cannot go back to school for 2-4 years
  • You struggle with science and math
  • You do not handle stress or pressure well
  • You cannot work flexible shifts including nights and weekends
  • You do not want to supervise or lead others
  • The cost of RN training (even with financial help) worries you
  • You are afraid of medications or making mistakes
  • You do not want to deal with life-and-death situations
The Choice is Yours: RN training is longer and more complex than LPN training, but offers more pay, independence, and career options. RNs lead healthcare teams. Take this assessment honestly. Research programs. Talk to working RNs. Then decide what's right for you.
