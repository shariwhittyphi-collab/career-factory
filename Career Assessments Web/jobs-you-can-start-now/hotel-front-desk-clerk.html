Hotel Front Desk Clerk Career Assessment

Indoor, seated, and growing about 8%

Section 1 of 11

1Welcome

Hotel front desk clerks check guests in and out, handle payments and problems, and run the front of a hotel.

North Dakota expects about 8% growth by 2034. It needs no school, and it is one of the few jobs in this area that is indoor, mostly seated, and open to people with no experience at all.

This takes about 15 minutes.

2What is a Hotel Front Desk Clerk?

You check guests in and out, take payment, assign rooms, answer the phone, handle reservations and cancellations, sort out problems with rooms and bills, and keep the desk and lobby in order. On a night shift you also run the end-of-day accounting and are often the only staff member in the building.

The Reality: If nobody is at the desk, a building full of clean rooms is useless to anybody. The work itself is steady rather than hard, and it has two real catches. The first is the pay range, and it is unusual: the tenth percentile is $14.69 and the ninetieth is $17.91. That is almost no spread. There is very little room to earn more in this job without moving into management, and you should know that before you plan around it. The second is the shifts. Hotels run twenty-four hours, so nights, weekends, and holidays are part of it, and the night audit shift means being alone in a building with whoever walks in at three in the morning.

Your Responsibilities:

  • Check guests in and out
  • Take payment and handle billing disputes
  • Assign rooms and manage the reservation system
  • Answer the phone and take bookings
  • Handle complaints about rooms, noise, and charges
  • Coordinate with housekeeping and maintenance
  • Run the night audit and end-of-day accounting
  • Keep the lobby and desk area in order
  • Follow security procedures, especially overnight

Most of the job is sorting out a problem before the guest decides it ruined their trip.

3A Day in the Life

3:00 PM — Take over from the day shift. Read the notes on who is arriving.

3:30 PM — Check-ins begin. Steady stream for two hours.

5:30 PM — A room is not ready. Call housekeeping, offer the guest an alternative.

6:30 PM — Phone bookings and a walk-in.

8:00 PM — Quieter. Catch up on reservations and tomorrow’s arrivals.

9:30 PM — Noise complaint on the second floor. Handle it calmly.

10:30 PM — Last arrivals. Lock the side doors.

11:00 PM — Hand over to the night auditor, or start the audit yourself.

Hotels run around the clock, so shifts include evenings, nights, weekends, and holidays.

4Training in North Dakota

No school needed. Trained on the job in about a week. You learn the property management system, the room types, and the hotel’s procedures on shift, usually alongside someone experienced for the first few days.

Training Pathways:

  • Direct hire: Hotels across North Dakota hire year-round and more heavily before summer and during construction and energy activity.
  • Night audit: Usually pays a premium and is quieter. It suits some people completely. Be honest with yourself about working alone overnight.
  • Into hotel management: Almost every hotel manager in this state started at a desk or in housekeeping. This is a genuine route, and it is where the money in hotels is.
  • Into other front-desk work: Medical, dental, and office reception pays about $2.40 an hour more and runs weekday hours.

Where to Train in North Dakota:

Before You Start: No diploma or experience required at most hotels. You need to be reliable, calm with complaints, and comfortable with a computer system. Some positions require you to be 18, especially for night audit.

Tip: Most of these jobs need no school, so the cost of starting is not tuition — it is the things you need in the first month. Non-slip shoes, work clothes or a uniform, a way to get to work, and getting through the two or three weeks before the first paycheck arrives. Ask the employer first: many will supply a uniform, some give a shoe allowance, and some pay weekly rather than every two weeks, which matters a lot at the start. After that, ask a career counselor or Job Service North Dakota whether WIOA or other programs can help with what is left over, like work clothes, boots, tools, or getting to work. What can be paid for depends on the program and on your own situation, so ask before you start rather than after.

5Certification & Licensing

No license or certificate is required. Nothing is needed to start. Industry certificates exist and matter only if you are heading toward management.

Worth knowing about:

  • Property management system training — provided by the hotel, and specific to the brand
  • Certified Front Desk Representative (AHLEI) — optional industry certificate; some brands pay for it
  • Certified Hotel Administrator — relevant only if you are aiming at management
  • Basic first aid and CPR — some hotels provide it, and it is useful when you are the only staff on site

The Exams: None to start. Industry certificates involve an online exam.

Cost: Nothing to start. Ask the employer before paying for any hotel certificate yourself.

Keeping It Current: Brand system training is refreshed when the software changes.

6Salary & Job Outlook

North Dakota Hotel Front Desk Clerk Pay (May 2025):

  • Starting out (bottom 10–25%): $14.69–$16.16/hour ($30,550–$33,610/year)
  • Middle (median — half earn more, half earn less): $16.60/hour ($34,530/year)
  • Experienced (top 25–10%): $17.03–$17.91/hour ($35,430–$37,250/year)

What that means: Look closely at this range — it is the narrowest of any job in this area. From the tenth percentile to the ninetieth is only about $3.20 an hour. That means experience alone will not move your pay much here. The ways up are a night audit premium, a larger or busier property, or moving into supervising and management. Ask about the night differential, because it is a real part of the money in this job.

Jobs in North Dakota: About 1,640 people do this work in ND today. By 2034, that should be about 1,770 (about 8% growth). Expect about 280 job openings every year in ND from growth and people retiring or changing jobs. Across the U.S., the change is growing faster than average.

Outlook: Good growth — about 8% in North Dakota by 2034, with roughly 280 openings a year. Travel, construction, and energy activity all keep hotel occupancy up in this state.

Who Hires in ND: Hotels and motels of every size, extended-stay properties, casinos, resorts, and crew housing operations in the oil counties.

Work Setting: Indoors at a desk in a lobby, mostly seated. Hotels operate twenty-four hours, so expect evenings, nights, weekends, and holidays. Night audit means working alone.

Physical Demands: Low. Mostly standing or sitting at a desk, occasional lifting of luggage and supplies.

Sources: U.S. Bureau of Labor Statistics, Occupational Employment and Wage Statistics, North Dakota, May 2025 (SOC 43-4081); North Dakota and U.S. long-term employment projections 2024–2034, via O*NET OnLine. Numbers are rounded.

7Where This Leads

Path 1: Front Desk Clerk — Where you start.

Path 2: Night Auditor — Usually a shift premium, and quieter work.

Path 3: Front Desk Supervisor — Running the desk and the schedule.

Path 4: Front Office Manager — Running the whole front of house.

Path 5: Assistant General Manager or General Manager — Where the pay in hotels actually is, and most came up from the desk.

Path 6: Office or Medical Reception — About $2.40 an hour more, with weekday hours — see that page.

8The Real Talk

What's Great:

  • Indoor, mostly seated, climate-controlled work
  • About 8% growth in North Dakota
  • No school, diploma, or experience needed
  • Night audit shifts pay a premium and are quiet
  • A genuine route into hotel management
  • Quiet stretches on most shifts, unlike most jobs in this area

What's Hard:

  • Almost no pay range — experience alone will not raise your wage much
  • Nights, weekends, and holidays
  • Night audit means being alone in the building overnight
  • You take the complaint for anything wrong with the room, the bill, or the noise
  • Hotel work in parts of this state is seasonal, so hours can drop
  • Tied to the desk for the whole shift

Be Honest: Are you all right with a job where more experience will not mean much more money unless you move up? Can you work nights, weekends, and holidays? And would being the only person in a hotel at three in the morning bother you?
