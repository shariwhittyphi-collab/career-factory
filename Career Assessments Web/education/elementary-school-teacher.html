Elementary School Teacher Career Assessment

One class, every subject, a whole year of growth

Section 1 of 11

1Welcome

Elementary school teachers teach children roughly six to eleven years old — the grades between kindergarten and middle school. You get one group of about twenty-two to twenty-six children and you keep them all day, every day, all year.

You do not teach one subject. You teach all of them. This page will show you what that really means, what it pays in North Dakota, and whether it fits you.

This takes about 15 minutes.

2What is a Elementary School Teacher?

An elementary teacher teaches reading, writing, math, science, and social studies to the same class every day. You plan five subjects, not one. In the morning you are a reading teacher running three different small groups because your class reads at three different levels. By ten you are a math teacher. By one you are teaching how a plant grows or how a bill becomes a law. You also grade the work, track how every child is doing, write report cards, meet with parents, sit in team meetings about the children who are struggling, and cover lunch and recess duty. In North Dakota the elementary license covers grades 1 through 8, so "elementary teacher" here can mean a first grade classroom or an eighth grade classroom.

The Reality: Say this part out loud: the work does not fit in the school day. Your contract may say 8:00 to 4:00, but planning five subjects, grading, and answering parent emails happens at night and on Sunday. Most elementary teachers get one prep period a day, about 45 minutes, and half of it goes to copying, meetings, or a phone call home. The first two years are the hardest, because you are building every single lesson from scratch for the first time. Teachers who last learn to stop chasing perfect and reuse what worked.

Your Responsibilities:

  • Teach reading, writing, math, science, and social studies to one class
  • Run small reading and math groups for children at different levels
  • Plan five subjects’ worth of lessons every week
  • Grade daily work, tests, and projects and write report cards
  • Track test data and figure out which children need more help
  • Handle behavior, conflict, and friendship problems in your room
  • Meet with parents at conferences and by email and phone all year
  • Sit in team meetings about students who need extra support or a special education plan

You get one class for one year, and you can see the difference you made in June.

3A Day in the Life

7:30 AM — In early. Copies for the day, math materials out, and the morning message on the board. Answer two parent emails from last night.

8:15 AM — Students arrive. Morning work, attendance, lunch count. One student tells you their dog died last night, so you adjust the whole morning in your head.

8:45 AM — Reading block. Whole-group lesson, then three small groups while the rest work at stations. You keep notes on each group as you go.

10:15 AM — Math. Whole group, then partner work. Two students are lost, so you pull them to the back table.

11:30 AM — Lunch and recess duty. You eat in twenty minutes, standing, while you sort out an argument about a soccer game.

12:30 PM — Your prep period — except today it is a meeting about a student who needs a support plan. No prep.

1:15 PM — Science, then social studies. Group work, which is loud, and that is fine.

3:30 PM — Dismissal, then two hours of grading, planning tomorrow, and calling a parent. You take the rest home.

You are on your feet and talking almost non-stop from the first bell, and the real planning happens before and after.

4Training in North Dakota

A four-year degree in elementary education and a North Dakota teaching license. The North Dakota elementary license covers grades 1 through 8. Note both ends of that. It does not include kindergarten unless you add a Kindergarten Endorsement or hold the birth to grade 3 early childhood license. It does include middle school grades, which is why an elementary degree in this state opens more doors than people expect. This is by far the biggest teaching job in North Dakota, so if you are choosing between the four careers in this series, this is the one with the most openings.

Training Pathways:

  • Straight to a four-year school (4 years): Start as an elementary education major. You will do field hours in real classrooms starting early, then a full-time student teaching semester near the end. VCSU, for example, requires over 150 hours of field experience before a twelve-week student teaching placement.
  • Two years here, then transfer (4 years total): Take general education and early education credits at a North Dakota two-year college or tribal college, then move to a four-year school for the teaching courses and student teaching. This costs much less at the start. NDSCS built an Associate in Arts specifically for this.
  • Already have a bachelor’s in something else: You have two real options. One is the ESPB Alternate Access License, which lets a district hire you in a documented shortage area for up to three years while you finish coursework. The other is a master’s-level licensure program — NDSU offers an M.Ed. teacher licensure option and Dickinson State offers a Master of Education in Elementary Education.

Where to Train in North Dakota:

  • University of North Dakota (Grand Forks) — Elementary Education, B.S.Ed., 125 credits, four years, on campus or 100% online with no campus visit required. UND says graduates are licensed for grades 1 to 8 in North Dakota (grades 1 to 6 in Minnesota) and offers minors in Early Childhood Education, Middle Level Education, Literacy Education, Special Education, and English Language Learner/Bilingual Education — und.edu/programs/elementary-education-bsed
  • Valley City State University (Valley City) — Elementary Education, B.S., 120 to 135 credits, grades 1 to 8. VCSU requires a second major, minor, or content option, and lists Kindergarten, English Learners, STEM Education, Special Education, Reading, Middle School, and Early Childhood as the add-ons. Over 150 hours of field experience before twelve weeks of student teaching — catalog.vcsu.edu/undergraduate-catalog/programs/majors/elementary-education
  • Dickinson State University (Dickinson) — Elementary Education, with Middle School Education, Early Childhood Education, and Reading available as concentrations. DSU states that elementary licensure entitles the holder to teach in the regular elementary classroom for grades one through eight — dickinsonstate.edu/academics/fields-of-study/soe/education/teacher-education-program/concentrations-licensures
  • University of Mary (Bismarck) — Elementary Education, B.A. or B.S., grades 1 to 8, accredited by the ND Education Standards and Practices Board, available fully online. U-Mary also lists a tuition-free Elementary Education Academy that it says lets qualifying students finish the bachelor’s in 2.6 years, and allows a double major with early childhood or special education — umary.edu/academics/undergraduate-programs/elementary-education
  • Mayville State University (Mayville) — Elementary Education, B.S.Ed., built for working adults with evening and online coursework. Mayville State says plainly that it is not entirely online: some classes meet live by desktop conferencing — mayvillestate.edu/msu-online/programs/bsed-elementary-education
  • Minot State University (Minot) — Elementary Education, B.S.Ed. — minotstateu.edu/enroll/programs/elementary-education.shtml
  • Turtle Mountain College (Belcourt) — Elementary Education, B.S. TMC also offers a Master of Education — tm.edu/academics
  • United Tribes Technical College (Bismarck) — Elementary Education, A.S. and Elementary Education, B.S. — ted.uttc.edu/degrees
  • North Dakota State College of Science (Wahpeton) — Liberal Arts (Elementary Education Transfer), Associate in Arts, 61 credits. NDSCS says students finish here and then transfer to take their education courses and student teach — catalog.ndscs.edu/liberal-arts/associate-in-arts/liberal-arts-elementary-education-transfer
  • Bismarck State College (Bismarck) — Education Foundations: Early Childhood and Elementary, a program certificate. It comes with a Title I Paraprofessional Certificate and a North Dakota Substitute Teacher License, and stacks into BSC’s Bachelor of Applied Science in Foundations of Teaching, which then leads into a licensure program at a partner school — bismarckstate.edu/academics/programs/educationfoundationsearlychildhoodelementary
  • Dickinson State University (Dickinson) — Master of Education in Elementary Education, for people who already hold a bachelor’s degree — dickinsonstate.edu/academics/fields-of-study/graduate-studies/master-of-education-in-elementary-education
  • North Dakota State University (Fargo) — M.Ed. Teacher Licensure Option. Note that NDSU does not list an undergraduate elementary education licensure major; its undergraduate teacher education is secondary (grades 5-12) and K-12 in art, music, and physical education — ndsu.edu/education/teacher-education

Before You Start: A high school diploma or GED to start. You need to read a lot and write clearly. You also have to be comfortable enough with elementary math to teach it well, which is different from being good at math — you have to explain why it works, not just get the answer. Teacher preparation programs have a GPA gate partway through; UND asks for at least 2.75. A fingerprint background check is required before you can be licensed or placed in a classroom.

Tip: Start with financial aid and scholarships. Fill out the FAFSA, ask the school’s financial aid office what you qualify for, and ask an employer whether they help pay for school. That is where the money starts. After that, a career counselor or Job Service North Dakota may be able to help with what is left over, like tuition, books, a laptop, or exam fees. What can be paid for depends on the program and on your own situation, so ask before you enroll rather than after. Worth asking about specifically: two-year degrees that train you for a job, like civil engineering technology, land surveying, mechatronics, electronics, or instrumentation. North Dakota two-year colleges also cost far less than a university. Ask about paid summer internships too — engineering firms, plants, and survey crews hire students while they are still in school.

5Certification & Licensing

North Dakota licenses you personally, through the Education Standards and Practices Board. ESPB issues and renews every North Dakota teaching license. An elementary license covers grades 1 through 8. Endorsements let you add kindergarten, reading, English learners, or special education on top.

The License Ladder in North Dakota:

  • Initial In-State License (two years) — ESPB says it is issued to first-time applicants who have met all of the ND requirements for licensure: a bachelor’s degree from an approved teacher education program, the required Praxis tests, and a background check (nd.gov/espb)
  • Two-Year Renewal License — for an educator with less than 18 months of contracted teaching in North Dakota, or who has completed probationary requirements (nd.gov/espb)
  • First Five-Year License — granted after you teach successfully for 18 months full-time equivalent in North Dakota (nd.gov/espb)
  • Five-Year Renewal License — requires at least 30 days under contract and six semester hours of continuing education (nd.gov/espb)
  • Endorsements ($80 each) — added through your ND Teach account. The Kindergarten Endorsement extends an elementary license down to kindergarten. ESPB also lists English Learner, Out-of-Field, and Major Equivalency Praxis endorsements (nd.gov/espb)
  • Alternate Access License (one year at a time, up to three years) — the door for someone who already holds a bachelor’s degree in another field. It is for documented shortage areas. ESPB asks for a degree in the content area or at least 30 credits in that area or a closely related one, plus a plan to finish. It renews only if you show progress and the school asks in writing (nd.gov/espb)
  • Initial Interim Substitute License (two years) — ESPB issues this with a minimum of 48 semester hours of college coursework. Substituting is the cheapest honest way to find out if you like this work before you commit four years (nd.gov/espb)
  • 25-Year Life License — for teachers who have held a North Dakota license for 25 years (nd.gov/espb)

The Exams: North Dakota requires all three Praxis Core Academic Skills for Educators tests: Reading (5713, qualifying score 156, minimum 149), Writing (5723, qualifying score 160, minimum 153), and Math (5733, qualifying score 150, minimum 143). ESPB also accepts a composite of 466 as long as no single score falls below that test’s minimum. You also take a subject test for your licensure area. ESPB does not list the subject test codes and cut scores on its testing page, so confirm the exact elementary education test and required score with ESPB before you register (nd.gov/espb/licensure/testing-information).

Cost: From the ESPB fee list: initial application $30, fingerprinting $44.50, Initial In-State two-year license $85, two-year renewal $65, five-year license $140, endorsements $80 each, Alternate Access License $165, Interim Substitute License $95, transcript review $175, and a $100 late fee if a license lapses. A $5 maintenance charge is added to new applications. The four-year degree is the real expense.

Keeping It Current: ESPB renews the five-year license when you have at least 30 days under contract and six semester hours of continuing education. Districts also pay more for graduate credits and a master’s degree, which is why so many teachers take summer classes — it covers renewal and moves you up the pay scale at the same time.

6Salary & Job Outlook

North Dakota Elementary School Teacher Pay (May 2025) — yearly contract pay:

  • Starting out (bottom 10–25%): $46,530–$49,360 a year
  • Middle (median — half earn more, half earn less): $60,030 a year
  • Experienced (top 25–10%): $61,970–$75,990 a year

What that means: Teachers are paid on a yearly contract, not by the hour, so the numbers above are yearly pay. Your district’s salary schedule is what tells you exactly what each step and each year of experience pays. The middle North Dakota elementary teacher earns about $60,030 a year, which is below the national middle of $63,970. So yes, this state pays less than the country does. The bigger gap is at the top: the top tenth in ND earn about $75,990 while the top tenth nationally earn about $104,340. In plain words, North Dakota starting pay is close to the national floor, but the ceiling here is much lower. Pay rises with years taught and with graduate credits, and district pay scales are public, so you can look up exactly what any ND district pays before you apply. Remember too that this is pay for a school-year contract, not twelve months.

Jobs in North Dakota: About 4,110 people do this work in ND today. By 2034, that should be about 4,350 (about 6% growth). Expect about 310 job openings every year in ND from growth and people retiring or changing jobs. Across the U.S., the change is about 2% decline.

Outlook: Strong, and by a wide margin the best of the four careers in this group. O*NET shows North Dakota going from about 4,110 elementary teachers in 2024 to about 4,350 in 2034, roughly 6% growth, with about 310 openings a year. Nationally this job is shrinking about 2%, so ND is moving the opposite direction from the country. Three hundred and ten openings a year across the state means a new graduate who is willing to look outside the three biggest cities can usually find a job. Rural districts hire hardest and often the fastest.

Who Hires in ND: Public school districts in every North Dakota county, tribal and Bureau of Indian Education schools, Catholic and other private schools, and a small number of charter schools. Large districts like Fargo, West Fargo, Bismarck, Minot, and Grand Forks hire the most positions; rural districts have the least competition per opening.

Work Setting: One classroom that belongs to you, with your own desks, library corner, and wall space. School calendar, roughly late August through late May, with holidays and summers off unless you work summer school or another job. You share lunch, recess, and bus duty with the rest of the staff, which means time outside in ND winter. Realistically you arrive before the students and stay after them, and you take work home.

Physical Demands: Moderate. You are on your feet and moving most of the day, but you do sit sometimes, and you are not carrying children the way a preschool teacher is. The real physical costs are your voice, your back from bending over desks, and recess duty in the cold. You will catch a lot of colds, especially in your first two years.

Sources: U.S. Bureau of Labor Statistics, Occupational Employment and Wage Statistics, North Dakota, May 2025 (SOC 25-2021); North Dakota and U.S. long-term employment projections 2024–2034, via O*NET OnLine. Numbers are rounded.

7Where This Leads

Path 1: Classroom Teacher — Teach the same grade and get better at it every year.

Path 2: Reading or Math Specialist — Add a credential; work with the students who struggle most.

Path 3: Special Education Teacher — Add the license; ND needs these badly.

Path 4: Instructional Coach — Help other teachers improve their teaching.

Path 5: Elementary Principal — Add a master’s degree and lead a building.

Path 6: Curriculum Director — Choose and build what a whole district teaches.

8The Real Talk

What's Great:

  • About 310 openings a year in ND — the most of any job in this group
  • You get one class for a whole year and you can see how far they came
  • You teach every subject, so no two hours look the same
  • A school calendar, real benefits, and a pension
  • Your license covers grades 1 through 8, so you can move between elementary and middle school
  • Small ND districts will hire you right out of school and let you grow fast

What's Hard:

  • The work does not fit in the day — planning and grading come home
  • You plan five subjects, not one, and the first two years are brutal
  • Reading levels in one room can span four or five grade levels
  • ND pay tops out well below the national top — about $75,990 against $104,340
  • Prep periods get eaten by meetings, copies, and phone calls home
  • Behavior and family situations you cannot fix will still land in your room

Be Honest: Are you willing to plan five subjects a week and take grading home most nights, especially for the first two years? Can you teach the same lesson three different ways in one hour because your class reads at three different levels? Can you go home at the end of a bad day and come back warm the next morning for the same child who made it bad? And can you accept ND pay, knowing the starting number is fair and the ceiling is low?
