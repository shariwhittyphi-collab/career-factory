What is a Phlebotomist?

A phlebotomist is a healthcare professional who collects blood samples from patients for medical testing, transfusions, donations, and research. It's a hands-on, patient-facing healthcare job that plays a vital role in the medical lab.

The Main Responsibilities

  • Draw blood from patients using needles (venipuncture) or from fingertips (capillary puncture)
  • Label and prepare blood samples for the lab
  • Talk with patients to explain the procedure and keep them calm
  • Follow strict safety and infection control procedures
  • Keep accurate records and use lab computer systems
  • Work in hospitals, clinics, blood banks, and urgent care centers

Why This Job Matters

Phlebotomists are the bridge between patients and medical labs. The blood samples they collect help doctors diagnose diseases, monitor medications, and save lives through blood donations. It's a job where you directly help people every single day.

What Kind of Person Makes a Good Phlebotomist?

  • Calm and patient with nervous patients
  • Detail-oriented and careful about accuracy
  • Able to handle stressful situations
  • Good at following procedures and safety rules
  • Friendly and good at talking with people
  • Physically able to stand and work all day

What Does Your Day Look Like?

Here's what a typical day might look like for a phlebotomist:

Morning (6:00 AM - 10:00 AM)

  • Arrive early to set up blood draw stations
  • Check lab equipment and supplies
  • Review the list of patients who need blood work
  • Call patients in and greet them warmly
  • Explain the blood draw procedure to calm nervous patients
  • Perform venipunctures (needle sticks) — 15-20 patients
  • Label samples with patient info
  • Send samples to the lab with paperwork

Midday (10:00 AM - 1:00 PM)

  • Continue blood draws for walk-ins and scheduled patients
  • Handle difficult cases (patients who faint, hard-to-find veins)
  • Restock supplies and clean work areas
  • Assist with special collection types (capillary sticks, special tubes)
  • Document everything in the computer system

Afternoon (1:00 PM - 5:00 PM)

  • Finish remaining patients
  • Clean and sanitize all equipment
  • Count supplies and order what you need
  • File paperwork and update patient records
  • May assist the lab with other tasks

The Physical Reality

  • You'll be on your feet most of the day
  • You're working with needles and body fluids (universal precautions required)
  • Early morning shifts are typical (many start at 5-6 AM)
  • Weekend and evening shifts may be required
  • You'll repeat the same motions hundreds of times

Phlebotomy Training in North Dakota

North Dakota does NOT require state licensure for phlebotomists, but training and national certification are strongly recommended by employers.

Training Length & Cost

  • Training Duration: 9 weeks to 9 months for ND classroom programs. Online courses are self-paced.
  • Classroom Hours: Varies by program (BSC has 45 hours in class)
  • Clinical Hours: Most programs include supervised practice on real patients (BSC has 100 hours)
  • Cost: Varies by program. BSC's course fee is $750, plus books and other fees. Check with each school.

What You'll Study

  • Human anatomy and physiology (how the body works)
  • Medical terminology (healthcare words and abbreviations)
  • Infection control and safety procedures
  • Venipuncture techniques (how to draw blood safely)
  • Capillary collection (fingerstick blood draws)
  • Specimen handling and processing
  • Lab equipment and computer systems
  • Patient communication and customer service
  • Ethical and legal issues in healthcare

Programs in North Dakota

Certification Exam & Requirements

In North Dakota, state licensure is NOT required. However, national certification is strongly preferred by employers and will improve your job prospects and earning potential.

National Certifications Available

Here are four common national phlebotomy certifications:

NHA Certified Phlebotomy Technician (CPT)

Organization: National Healthcareer Association

Exam Cost: $134 (NHA, 2026). Sometimes included in school programs.

Good to Know: Two-hour exam. To qualify, you need at least 30 blood draws and 10 fingersticks on real people.

ASCP Phlebotomy Technician (PBT)

Organization: American Society for Clinical Pathology

Exam Cost: $155

Highly Respected: Strong reputation in hospital labs

AMT Registered Phlebotomy Technician (RPT)

Organization: American Medical Technologists

Exam Cost: $125

Good to Know: Fee includes your first year of AMT membership.

NCCT Phlebotomy Technician (NCPT)

Organization: National Center for Competency Testing

Exam Cost: $119

Also Respected: Valid across healthcare settings

What You Need to Pass

  • Finish a training program, or have enough work experience (each exam has its own rules)
  • Show a set number of successful blood draws (for example, NHA needs 30 blood draws and 10 fingersticks)
  • Take and pass the national certification exam
  • The exam covers anatomy, venipuncture, patient care, and safety

ND Employer Preferences

Major healthcare employers in North Dakota—like Sanford Health, CHI St. Alexius Health, and Essentia Health—strongly prefer candidates with national certification. Some smaller or rural facilities may hire and train you on the job, but certification significantly improves your chances.

Additional Requirements

  • CPR Certification (usually required)
  • Background check
  • Drug screening
  • Health screening (vaccinations may be required)

Salary & Pay

Phlebotomists in North Dakota earn competitive pay for an entry-level healthcare position. Here's what you can expect (BLS, May 2025):

Pay Level Hourly Rate Annual Salary
25th percentile (lower pay) ~$20.08/hour ~$41,770/year
10th Percentile (bottom 10%) $16.27/hour $33,840/year
Median (North Dakota) $22.81/hour ~$47,430/year
90th Percentile (top 10%) $28.02/hour $58,290/year
75th percentile (higher pay) $25.83/hour $53,730/year

Factors That Affect Your Pay

  • Certification: Certified phlebotomists earn more than non-certified
  • Location: Rural vs. urban areas pay differently
  • Employer: Hospitals pay more than clinics; major health systems pay more than small practices
  • Shift: Evening, night, and weekend shifts usually pay more
  • Experience: Pay increases with years on the job
  • Specialization: Some positions (blood bank, specialty collections) pay more

Benefits

Most healthcare employers offer:

  • Health insurance
  • Retirement plans (401k or pension)
  • Paid time off (vacation, sick days)
  • Continuing education assistance
  • Shift differentials (extra pay for nights/weekends)
📈 Job Growth: Phlebotomy is growing! BLS projects 7% growth nationally from 2025 to 2035 (much faster than average), with about 18,000 job openings per year nationwide.

Career Paths After Phlebotomy

Starting as a phlebotomist doesn't mean you stay in that role forever. Many healthcare careers use phlebotomy as a stepping stone.

Path 1: Advance Within the Lab

  • Lab Technician / Medical Technologist: Get a 2-year or 4-year degree and move into specimen processing, testing, or quality control
  • Lab Supervisor / Manager: Oversee phlebotomy teams and lab operations (with experience and education)
  • Specialty Areas: Work in blood banks, transfusion services, or specialty testing (higher pay, more training)

Path 2: Transition to Nursing

  • CNA (Certified Nursing Assistant): Move into patient care roles
  • LPN (Licensed Practical Nurse): 12-18 month program
  • RN (Registered Nurse): 2-4 year degree program (highest earning potential)

Path 3: Specialize in Phlebotomy

  • Phlebotomy Educator: Teach phlebotomy courses at colleges or for employers
  • Blood Bank Specialist: Focus on blood donations and transfusions (specialized training)
  • Mobile Phlebotomist: Travel to patients' homes or smaller facilities (higher pay)

Path 4: Stay in Phlebotomy & Grow

  • Master your craft and become highly skilled
  • Take on leadership roles (training new staff, quality control)
  • Move to higher-paying positions (hospitals vs. clinics)
  • The top 25% of ND phlebotomists earn about $26/hour or more (BLS, May 2025)

Why Phlebotomy is a Good Starting Point

  • Short training (weeks to months, not years)
  • Get paid while learning healthcare work
  • Test whether you like working in healthcare
  • Build experience for nursing or other healthcare roles
  • Many employers offer tuition assistance for further education

Is Phlebotomy Right For You?

Before you decide, think about these questions:

Questions to Ask Yourself

  • Do you like working with people? You'll talk to patients all day, including nervous or upset people.
  • Can you handle the physical demands? Standing 8 hours a day, using needles, dealing with blood and body fluids.
  • Are you good with details? Accuracy in labeling and documentation is critical—mistakes can affect patient care.
  • Can you stay calm under pressure? Patients faint, you'll miss veins sometimes, you'll be busy and stressed.
  • Do you like helping people? This is healthcare—you're directly helping patients and supporting diagnosis.
  • Can you work early mornings, weekends, or nights? Lab hours are often 6 AM - 2 PM or include weekend coverage.
  • Are you willing to get certified? It's not required in ND, but employers strongly prefer it.
  • Is this a stepping stone for you? Or do you want a long-term career as a phlebotomist? (Both are valid!)

Red Flags - This Might NOT Be For You If...

  • You're uncomfortable with needles or blood
  • You can't stand for long periods
  • You're not good at following procedures exactly
  • You get stressed easily by difficult patient interactions
  • You can't commit to early morning shifts
  • You need a job with lots of flexibility in scheduling

Green Lights - This Might Be Great For You If...

  • You're genuinely interested in healthcare
  • You like problem-solving with hands-on work
  • You're a natural at calming nervous people
  • You're detail-oriented and follow procedures carefully
  • You want a job with good pay and job security
  • You're willing to learn and get certified
  • You see this as a stepping stone to nursing or other healthcare careers
